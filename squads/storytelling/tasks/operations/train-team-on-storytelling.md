# Train Team on Storytelling
> Running internal storytelling training sessions for team members and cross-squad collaborators.

## Task ID
`train-team-on-storytelling` (matches config.yaml routing)

## Description
Storytelling is a skill, not a talent — and skills require practice, feedback, and structured development. This task covers the planning and execution of internal training sessions that build storytelling capability across the team and, when appropriate, across other squads. Training sessions range from onboarding fundamentals to advanced workshops on specific techniques.

## Inputs
- **Training request**: Who needs training, what level, and what specific capability gap exists
- **Audience assessment**: Current skill level of participants (beginner, intermediate, advanced)
- **Available frameworks**: Squad's framework library for training content
- **Exercise templates**: Pre-built exercises from the exercises template library
- **Schedule constraints**: Available time slots, session duration limits, remote vs. in-person

## Outputs
- **Training session plan**: Agenda, exercises, materials, and facilitation notes
- **Session recording/notes**: Documentation of what was covered for future reference
- **Participant feedback**: Post-session survey results
- **Skill assessment update**: Updated assessment of participant capabilities post-training

## Steps
1. **Assess the need** — Clarify: Who is being trained? What is their current skill level? What specific capability should improve? What does success look like? A training session without clear objectives wastes everyone's time.
2. **Select the training tier** — Match the session to the audience:
   - **Tier 1 — Fundamentals** (60-90 min): What makes a story, story vs. anecdote, the five-second moment, basic story structure. For new team members or non-storytelling squads.
   - **Tier 2 — Applied Skills** (90-120 min): Story selection for business contexts, channel adaptation, audience calibration. For intermediate practitioners.
   - **Tier 3 — Advanced Craft** (120-180 min): Narrative strategy, multi-story architecture, voice development, advanced techniques. For experienced storytellers.
3. **Build the agenda** — Structure the session with a ratio of approximately 30% instruction, 50% practice, 20% feedback/discussion. Every concept taught should be immediately practiced.
4. **Prepare exercises** — Select 2-3 exercises from the exercise library. Customize prompts to the participants' industry and work context. Generic exercises produce generic skills.
5. **Prepare materials** — Gather swipe file examples relevant to the audience. Print or share exercise templates. Prepare any slides (minimal — training should be interactive, not a lecture).
6. **Open with a story** — Model what you are teaching. The facilitator tells a story that demonstrates the session's core concept. Never open with a definition or a slide.
7. **Teach, then practice** — For each concept: demonstrate with an example, explain the principle, then immediately run the exercise. Debrief each exercise before moving to the next concept.
8. **Provide live feedback** — During exercises, circulate and offer specific, constructive feedback. "Your opening was strong because it started with a specific moment" is useful. "Good job" is not.
9. **Close with application** — End every session with a concrete next step: "Before our next session, use the Homework for Life template daily for one week" or "Apply the story spine to your next client email."
10. **Collect feedback** — Distribute a short survey: What was most useful? What was confusing? What do you want to practice more? Rate your confidence in storytelling before/after.
11. **Document and iterate** — Record what worked, what fell flat, and what to change. Update the training plan for the next session. Training improves through the same feedback loop we teach.
12. **Schedule follow-up** — One session changes awareness, not behavior. Schedule a follow-up 2-4 weeks later to review practice results and address new questions.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Neglecting maintenance**: Operations are not glamorous. The story bank degrades without regular maintenance, and the squad pays later.
- **Hoarding knowledge**: If only one person knows how the systems work, the squad is fragile. Document everything.
- **Skipping the sync**: Cross-squad syncs feel optional when things are busy. They are not — skipped syncs create misalignment.
- **Over-engineering process**: Simple processes that people follow beat complex processes that people ignore.
- **Forgetting to archive**: Deleting old content instead of archiving it. Old frameworks, stories, and processes may be needed for reference.
