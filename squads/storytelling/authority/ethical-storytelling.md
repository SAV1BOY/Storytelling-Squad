# Ethical Storytelling

## Guidelines for Responsible Narrative Marketing

Storytelling is powerful precisely because it bypasses rational defenses and speaks directly to emotions and identity. This power creates an ethical obligation. These guidelines define where we draw the line between persuasion and manipulation, between empathy and exploitation.

---

## Core Ethical Principles

### 1. Truth as Foundation

Every story we tell must be grounded in verifiable truth. This does not mean every detail must be literal; narrative compression, composite timelines, and representative examples are legitimate craft tools when disclosed. But the substance, the core claim, the real outcome, the genuine experience, must be true.

**What this means in practice**:
- Customer results cited in stories must be verified and representative
- Timelines may be compressed for narrative flow but not fabricated
- Quotes must be accurate or clearly paraphrased with permission
- Outlier results must be identified as such, not presented as typical

### 2. Informed Consent

Every person whose story we tell must give informed, enthusiastic consent. They must understand how their story will be used, where it will appear, and how long it will be active. Consent must be freely given, not coerced through incentives, power dynamics, or pressure.

**What this means in practice**:
- Written consent agreements for all customer and employee stories
- Right to review and approve the final narrative before publication
- Right to withdraw consent at any time, with reasonable notice
- Clear explanation of all channels and contexts where the story will appear
- No pressure tactics (discounts, access, career implications) to obtain stories

### 3. Authentic Representation

Stories must represent the diversity of the real audience honestly. We do not tokenize, stereotype, or use representation as a performance. If a brand's customer base is diverse, the stories should reflect that diversity naturally. If it is not, we do not fabricate diversity for optics.

**What this means in practice**:
- Representation must reflect reality, not aspiration
- Avoid using marginalized communities as props for brand image
- Ensure people from represented groups review stories about their communities
- Consider intersectionality: people are not defined by a single identity
- Avoid "inspiration porn" that reduces people to their challenges

### 4. No Exploitation of Vulnerability

We do not exploit fear, shame, grief, or insecurity to drive commercial outcomes. There is a line between empathizing with a pain point and weaponizing it. We stay firmly on the side of empathy.

**What this means in practice**:
- Health and wellness stories must not shame bodies or exploit health anxiety
- Financial stories must not terrorize people about their financial situation
- Parenting stories must not leverage parental guilt
- Stories about social issues must not appropriate suffering for brand positioning
- Vulnerability shared by story subjects must be treated with care, not amplified for engagement

### 5. Persuasion, Not Manipulation

Persuasion respects the audience's autonomy. Manipulation undermines it. The difference is whether the audience would still agree with the narrative's framing if they understood all the techniques being used.

**What this means in practice**:
- Do not use false scarcity or artificial urgency in narrative framing
- Do not create misleading before/after narratives
- Do not selectively omit material information that would change the story's meaning
- Do not use dark patterns in story-driven conversion flows
- Ensure the narrative's emotional appeal is proportionate to the actual stakes

## Consent Framework

### Tier 1: Public Stories

Stories about public figures, publicly available information, or general industry trends. No individual consent required, but accuracy and fair representation are mandatory.

### Tier 2: Attributed Customer Stories

Stories that name a specific customer, employee, or partner. Full consent process required:
- Initial verbal agreement to participate
- Formal written consent after reviewing the draft
- Final approval before publication
- Annual renewal for evergreen content

### Tier 3: Sensitive Stories

Stories involving health, finances, personal struggles, family, identity, or controversial topics. Enhanced consent process required:
- Everything in Tier 2, plus:
- Explicit discussion of potential risks of sharing the story
- Offer of anonymization options
- Review by legal and ethics leads
- 48-hour cooling-off period between consent and publication

### Tier 4: Stories Involving Minors or Vulnerable Populations

Stories involving children, elderly individuals, people in crisis, or others in vulnerable positions. Maximum protection required:
- Everything in Tier 3, plus:
- Guardian or advocate review and consent
- External ethics review
- Option for full withdrawal with content removal
- Ongoing check-ins after publication

## Ethical Decision Framework

When facing an ethical gray area in storytelling, work through these questions:

### Question 1: Is it true?

Can the core claims of the story be verified? Would a reasonable skeptic accept the narrative as honest?

### Question 2: Is it consented?

Has everyone whose story is being told given informed, enthusiastic consent? Would they be comfortable with every use of their story?

### Question 3: Is it fair?

Does the story represent all relevant perspectives fairly? Are we omitting information that would materially change the narrative?

### Question 4: Is it proportionate?

Is the emotional intensity of the story proportionate to the actual stakes? Are we amplifying emotions beyond what the situation warrants?

### Question 5: Is it reversible?

If we publish this story and later learn something that changes its meaning, can we correct it? Do we have a plan for corrections and retractions?

### Question 6: Would we be proud?

If the full process of creating this story were made public, including how we sourced it, edited it, and distributed it, would we be proud? If a journalist investigated our storytelling practices, would this story hold up?

## Specific Contexts

### Customer Testimonials

- Results must be real and verified
- "Typical" results must be actually typical, not cherry-picked outliers
- Compensation for testimonials must be disclosed
- Testimonials must not be edited to change their meaning

### Employee Stories

- Employees must not feel that participation is implicitly required
- Stories must not put employees at professional risk
- Departed employees should have the right to withdraw their stories
- Power dynamics between the company and employee must be acknowledged

### Social Impact Narratives

- Do not overstate the brand's contribution to social causes
- Ensure the community being served has voice and agency in the narrative
- Avoid "savior" narratives that center the brand over the community
- Back narrative claims with real, measurable impact data

### Competitive Narratives

- Do not misrepresent competitors' offerings or stories
- Comparison stories must be factually accurate
- Avoid narratives that demean competitors' customers
- Focus on why we are better, not why they are worse

### AI-Generated or AI-Assisted Stories

- Disclose AI involvement when required by platform or regulation
- Never present AI-generated content as human testimony
- Human review is mandatory for all AI-assisted narrative content
- AI must not be used to fabricate stories presented as real

## Enforcement and Accountability

### Pre-Publication Review

All stories in Tier 2 and above must pass through an ethical review before publication. The review checks truth, consent, representation, and proportionality.

### Incident Response

If an ethical concern is raised about a published story, the response protocol is:
1. Acknowledge the concern within 24 hours
2. Investigate the specific claims
3. If warranted, pause or remove the content
4. Correct and republish or permanently retire
5. Document lessons learned

### Annual Audit

Conduct an annual audit of storytelling practices, reviewing a sample of stories across all tiers for ethical compliance. Share findings with the team and update guidelines as needed.

---

## The Line We Will Not Cross

We will never knowingly tell a false story, exploit a vulnerable person, fabricate consent, or manipulate an audience's emotions in ways that undermine their autonomy. These are not guidelines. They are boundaries. Crossing them is a terminable offense.
