# Best Story Openings with Analysis

## Purpose
Analysis of memorable story openings from speeches, articles, books, and brand content.
Each breakdown explains WHY the opening works and how to adapt the technique.

---

## 1. In Medias Res (Starting in the Middle)

**Example — Matthew Dicks, *Storyworthy*:**
> "I am standing in a McDonald's parking lot. It's 1992. I'm nineteen. And I'm
> homeless."

**Why it works:**
- Drops the reader into a specific moment. No preamble.
- Four short sentences, each adding a new dimension of surprise.
- "McDonald's parking lot" is precise and visual. Not "a parking lot."
- The word "homeless" at the end detonates the setup.

**Technique:** Start at the moment of highest tension or strangeness, then fill in context later.

---

## 2. The Bold Declaration

**Example — Peter Thiel, *Zero to One*:**
> "Every moment in business happens only once. The next Bill Gates will not build
> an operating system. The next Larry Page will not make a search engine. If you are
> copying these guys, you aren't learning from them."

**Why it works:**
- Challenges conventional wisdom immediately ("follow successful people").
- Creates productive discomfort — the reader feels implicated.
- Four sentences, building on each other, culminating in a direct address ("you").

**Technique:** Open with a claim the audience will resist, then immediately provide the logic that makes it undeniable.

---

## 3. The Specific Number

**Example — Malcolm Gladwell, *Outliers*:**
> "Roseto Valfortore lies one hundred miles southeast of Rome, in the Apennine
> foothills of the Italian province of Foggia."

**Why it works:**
- Geographic specificity creates the feeling of a documentary. You can see the town.
- The reader wonders: why are we here? What happens in this village?
- Gladwell then reveals: the residents had nearly zero heart disease. The mystery is set.

**Technique:** Ground the reader in a hyper-specific location or data point, then reveal why it matters.

---

## 4. The Question

**Example — Simon Sinek, "Start with Why" talk:**
> "How do you explain when things don't go as we assumed? Or better, how do you explain
> when others are able to achieve things that seem to defy all of the assumptions?"

**Why it works:**
- Questions activate the reader's brain. They can't NOT try to answer.
- "Things that seem to defy assumptions" creates a curiosity gap.
- The setup implies: I have an answer to this question.

**Technique:** Ask a question the audience has felt but never articulated. Then promise the answer.

---

## 5. The Confession

**Example — Brene Brown, TED Talk:**
> "So, I'll start with this: a couple years ago, an event planner called me because
> I was going to do a speaking event. She said, 'I'm really struggling with how to
> write about you on the little flyer.' And I said, 'Well, why?' She said, 'Well, I
> saw you speak, and I'm going to call you a researcher, I think, but… if I call you
> a researcher, no one will come. Because they'll think you're boring and irrelevant.'"

**Why it works:**
- Self-deprecating humor immediately creates likeability.
- The anecdote raises a tension: who is this person? Are they boring?
- It creates a frame for the entire talk: "I'm a researcher, but I'm going to be interesting."

**Technique:** Open with a moment that simultaneously introduces you and creates a question about you.

---

## 6. The Contrast Opener

**Example — Charles Dickens, *A Tale of Two Cities*:**
> "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times."

**Why it works:**
- Paradox. The brain cannot hold two contradictory ideas without seeking resolution.
- Rhythmic parallel structure makes it instantly memorable.
- Applies to almost any era — which is why it's quoted 200+ years later.

**Modern business adaptation:**
> "This was the quarter that nearly killed the company — and the quarter that saved it."

**Technique:** Open with a paradox that the story will resolve.

---

## 7. The "Before I Start" Subversion

**Example — David Foster Wallace, Kenyon College 2005:**
> "There are these two young fish swimming along and they happen to meet an older
> fish swimming the other way, who nods at them and says, 'Morning, boys. How's the
> water?' And the two young fish swim on for a bit, and then eventually one of them
> looks over at the other and goes, 'What the hell is water?'"

**Why it works:**
- A simple parable that the entire speech will unpack.
- Immediately establishes the theme (the invisible obvious).
- Humor and surprise in the punchline.
- Wallace then says: "I am not the wise old fish." — deflating any expectation of a lecture.

**Technique:** Open with a short parable or joke, then tell the audience what it means (or what it doesn't mean).

## Opening Types Ranked by Context

| Context | Best Opening Type | Why |
|---------|------------------|-----|
| Blog post | Bold declaration or question | Earns the scroll |
| Keynote | In medias res or confession | Creates immediate intimacy |
| Case study | Specific number or scene | Grounds in reality |
| Email | Scene or confession | Stops the scan |
| Sales pitch | Customer story (in medias res) | Makes the prospect see themselves |
| Social media | Bold declaration or contrast | Pattern interrupts the feed |
| Video ad | Visual in medias res | Earns the first 3 seconds |

## The Opening Test
Read your opening aloud. Ask:
1. Would you keep reading if you saw this in your own inbox?
2. Does it create a question the reader needs answered?
3. Is it specific? (Not "one day" but "a Tuesday in March")
4. Can you cut the first sentence and start with the second? (Often, yes.)
5. Does it earn the second sentence?
