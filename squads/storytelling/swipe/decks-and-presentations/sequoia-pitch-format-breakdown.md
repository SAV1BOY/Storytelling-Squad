# Sequoia Capital Pitch Format — Narrative Breakdown

## Overview

Sequoia Capital's recommended pitch format is the venture capital industry's most influential narrative template. While not a single deck, the Sequoia format prescribes a specific sequence of elements that, taken together, form a compelling startup story. The format has been refined through decades of evaluating thousands of pitches and reflects what experienced investors need to hear — and in what order — to make investment decisions. This breakdown analyzes the narrative architecture embedded in Sequoia's recommended structure and explains why the sequence itself tells a story.

## Structural Breakdown

### Beat 1: Company Purpose (The Log Line)
Sequoia asks founders to define their company in a single declarative sentence. This is the story's log line — the one sentence that captures the entire narrative. It forces founders to distill complexity into clarity. If the log line does not hook attention, nothing that follows matters.

### Beat 2: The Problem
What is broken in the world? Sequoia wants the problem described from the customer's perspective, with evidence that it is a significant, widespread pain. The problem sets the story's stakes — without a meaningful problem, there is no story worth telling.

### Beat 3: The Solution
How does the company fix what is broken? The solution must be described clearly and concisely, and it must logically follow from the problem. The narrative link between problem and solution should feel inevitable, not forced.

### Beat 4: Why Now
This is the narrative element most pitches miss. What has changed in the world that makes this solution possible and necessary now? Technology shifts, regulatory changes, cultural trends, infrastructure availability. "Why now" answers the investor's implicit question: "If this is such a good idea, why has no one done it before?"

### Beat 5: Market Size
How large is the opportunity? Sequoia distinguishes between total addressable market, serviceable addressable market, and target market. The narrative function of market size is establishing stakes — the larger the market, the higher the story's stakes.

### Beat 6: Competition / Alternatives
Who else is trying to solve this problem? What alternatives does the customer currently use? The competitive landscape slide is a narrative positioning tool — it shows where you sit relative to others and why your position is defensible.

### Beat 7: Product
What does the product actually look like and do? This comes after the narrative setup because by this point, the investor wants to see the solution in action. Product details serve as evidence for the story's claims.

### Beat 8: Business Model
How does the company make money? Revenue model, pricing strategy, unit economics. In narrative terms, this is the "sustainability" proof — the story can continue because the model supports it.

### Beat 9: Team
Why is this team uniquely qualified to win? The team slide is the story's "why us" — the characters who make the narrative credible. Relevant experience, domain expertise, and founder-market fit are the key proof points.

### Beat 10: Financials and Ask
Projections, current metrics, and the funding request. In story terms, this is the "next chapter" — what happens next, and what resources are needed to make it happen.

## Framework Used

**The Sequoia Narrative Sequence**

The sequence creates a logical-emotional narrative:
1. **Hook** (Purpose): Capture attention with a single powerful sentence
2. **Stakes** (Problem): Establish why anyone should care
3. **Resolution Promise** (Solution): Show the path forward
4. **Timing** (Why Now): Create urgency and inevitability
5. **Scale** (Market): Amplify the stakes
6. **Positioning** (Competition): Establish strategic clarity
7. **Evidence** (Product): Prove the solution works
8. **Sustainability** (Business Model): Show it is economically viable
9. **Credibility** (Team): Prove the right people are driving
10. **Future** (Financials/Ask): Define the next chapter

Each element answers a question the investor is already asking. The sequence maps to the investor's internal decision-making process.

## Why It Works

1. **Question-answer architecture**: Each section answers the question the previous section raised. Problem raises "how do you fix it?" Solution raises "why hasn't someone done this?" Why Now answers that. This creates intellectual momentum.

2. **Log line as filter**: The single-sentence purpose forces radical clarity. If the log line fails, the founder is saved from building 20 slides for a story no one will follow.

3. **Why Now creates inevitability**: This element transforms a pitch from "here is our idea" to "this is happening now, and we are positioned to lead it." Timing converts interest into urgency.

4. **Team as character development**: By placing the team near the end, Sequoia ensures the investor evaluates the team in the context of the full story. The team is not interesting in a vacuum — it is interesting because of the problem, solution, and market they are pursuing.

5. **The sequence itself is a trust-building exercise**: Each element builds on the previous one, creating cumulative credibility. By the time the investor reaches the ask, the narrative has established enough trust that the funding request feels like a logical conclusion.

## Key Techniques

### Technique: The Single-Sentence Purpose
Distill your company to one sentence that captures what you do and why it matters. Test it: if someone reads only this sentence, do they want to hear more?

### Technique: The "Why Now" Proof
Identify 2-3 converging forces (technology, market, cultural, regulatory) that make your solution possible and necessary at this specific moment. This is your timing thesis.

### Technique: Question-Chain Architecture
Structure your narrative so each section implicitly raises a question that the next section answers. This creates forward momentum that keeps the audience engaged.

### Technique: Competition as Positioning Map
Use the competition slide not to attack competitors but to clarify your unique positioning. A 2x2 matrix showing where you sit relative to alternatives tells a positioning story visually.

### Technique: The Credibility Stack
Build credibility incrementally: problem awareness → solution insight → timing thesis → market knowledge → product execution → business acumen → team credentials. Each layer adds to the cumulative trust.

## Marketing Application

| Application | How to Adapt |
|---|---|
| Startup pitch decks | Follow the 10-element sequence exactly |
| Internal proposals | Use the question-chain architecture for any business case |
| Product strategy docs | Problem → Solution → Why Now → Market → Competition format |
| Board presentations | Adapt the sequence for quarterly narrative updates |
| Partnership pitches | Frame partnership opportunities using the same logical chain |
| Grant applications | Problem → Solution → Why Now → Impact → Team → Ask |

### Adaptation Template
1. Purpose: "[Company] [does what] for [whom]."
2. Problem: "[Specific pain point] affects [number] of [people/companies]."
3. Solution: "We [solve it] by [approach]."
4. Why Now: "[Converging force 1], [force 2], and [force 3] make this the right moment."
5. Market: "[$X] total market, [$Y] serviceable, [$Z] initial target."
6. Competition: "Existing alternatives [A, B, C] fail because [gaps]. We are positioned [here]."
7. Product: "[Demo or description of working product]."
8. Model: "We charge [pricing] and our unit economics show [metric]."
9. Team: "[Founder 1] brings [relevant credential]. [Founder 2] brings [relevant credential]."
10. Ask: "We are raising [$X] to [specific milestones]. This gets us to [next inflection point]."
