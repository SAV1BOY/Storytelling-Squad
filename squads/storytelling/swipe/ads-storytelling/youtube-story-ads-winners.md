# Winning YouTube Story Ads: Analysis

## Purpose
Breakdown of YouTube ads that use narrative storytelling to hold attention past the
5-second skip window and drive meaningful engagement.

---

## Ad 1: Google — "Loretta"

**Format:** 90 seconds, Super Bowl spot
**Hook (0–5s):** An elderly man's voice: "Hey Google, show me photos of me and Loretta." Screen shows Google Assistant responding.
**Structure:** A widower uses Google Assistant to preserve memories of his late wife.
**Story arc:** Each query reveals a new memory — "Remember, Loretta hated my mustache," "Loretta's favorite flowers were tulips." The assistant files these under "Things to remember about Loretta." Final query: "Hey Google, play our favorite movie."
**Why it worked:**
- The product is the tool. The human story is the payload.
- Progressive emotional escalation — each memory adds weight.
- The viewer fills in the gaps: the love, the loss, the need to hold on.
- No CTA beyond the emotional imprint. Google = helping you remember what matters.
**Storytelling lesson:** Let the product serve the character, not the other way around. The most powerful ad never says "buy."

## Ad 2: Nike — "Dream Crazy" (Kaepernick)

**Format:** 2 minutes
**Hook (0–5s):** A young boy with no legs running. Voiceover: "If people say your dreams are crazy…"
**Structure:** Montage of athletes overcoming extraordinary circumstances, narrated by Colin Kaepernick, building to a single message.
**Story arc:** A series of micro-stories — each athlete faces a barrier (disability, poverty, discrimination, doubt) and overcomes it. The throughline: "Don't ask if your dreams are crazy. Ask if they're crazy enough."
**Why it worked:**
- Each 5–10 second vignette is a complete story: character, obstacle, triumph.
- Kaepernick's presence is itself a story — the ad's existence was the message.
- Building cadence: each story is bigger than the last, creating an emotional crescendo.
- Cultural conversation: the ad was designed to be discussed, debated, shared.
**Storytelling lesson:** A montage of micro-stories can build cumulative emotional power greater than any single story.

## Ad 3: Volvo Trucks — "The Epic Split" (Van Damme)

**Format:** 75 seconds
**Hook (0–5s):** Jean-Claude Van Damme standing between two trucks. "This test was set up to demonstrate the stability and precision of Volvo Dynamic Steering."
**Structure:** Single continuous shot. Two trucks reversing. Van Damme does the splits between them as they separate.
**Story arc:** Setup (calm explanation of what you're about to see) → Execution (the impossible stunt, slowly unfolding) → Resolution (the trucks separate, Van Damme holds the split, Enya plays).
**Why it worked:**
- One impossible image. That's the entire strategy.
- The stakes are physical (will he fall?) which creates visceral tension.
- The product demonstration IS the story — precision steering enables the stunt.
- Extreme shareability: "You have to see this."
**Storytelling lesson:** Sometimes one extraordinary moment is the story. Don't dilute it.

## Ad 4: Always — "#LikeAGirl"

**Format:** 3 minutes (60-second cut for paid)
**Hook (0–5s):** Interviewer asks a teenager: "Show me what it looks like to run like a girl." She flails her arms and mocks the motion.
**Structure:** Social experiment format. Teens perform "like a girl" as mockery. Young girls perform "like a girl" with full effort and pride. The contrast is the story.
**Story arc:** Assumption (the phrase is an insult) → Evidence (teens mock it) → Counter-evidence (young girls don't) → Insight (the insult is learned, not inherent) → Reframe ("Let's make 'like a girl' mean amazing things").
**Why it worked:**
- The viewer's own assumptions are challenged in real time.
- Real people, real reactions — not actors performing surprise.
- Emotional turn is built on contrast: same phrase, different meanings.
- The brand (Always) is barely present. The movement is the message.
**Storytelling lesson:** The most persuasive stories don't tell you what to think. They show you something that changes how you think.

## Ad 5: Squarespace — "Make It" series (various)

**Format:** 30–60 seconds
**Hook (0–5s):** A creator's dream stated simply. "I wanted to build a surf school."
**Structure:** Mini-documentary. Real person, real dream, real website built on Squarespace.
**Story arc:** Dream → Obstacle (no technical skills, no budget for developer) → Discovery (Squarespace) → Result (functioning business with a beautiful site).
**Why it worked:**
- The creator is the hero. Squarespace is the mentor tool.
- Aspirational without being unrealistic — real people, real businesses.
- The website is shown in context (on a phone, on a laptop) — not in isolation.
- Each ad is a different story from a different creator — series creates binge-ability.
**Storytelling lesson:** Make your customer the protagonist. Show their dream, not your feature set.

## The 5-Second Problem: Earning the View

Every YouTube ad faces the skip button at second 5. Strategies that earn the watch:

| Strategy | Example | Why It Works |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| **Visual impossibility** | Van Damme between trucks | Brain demands to see the outcome |
| **Emotional hook** | "Hey Google, show me photos of Loretta" | Intimacy creates curiosity |
| **Challenge/experiment** | "Show me what it looks like to run like a girl" | Social experiments hook curiosity |
| **Celebrity + unexpected context** | Keanu Reeves in Squarespace ad | Familiarity + surprise |
| **Bold claim** | "If people say your dreams are crazy…" | Provocation demands attention |

---

## YouTube Ad Story Checklist
1. Does the first 5 seconds earn the next 25? Would you skip your own ad?
2. Is there a character with a want or a problem?
3. Is there visual interest beyond talking heads?
4. Does the story earn the product moment (vs. forcing it)?
5. Is the emotional arc complete, even in a short cut?
6. Will someone share this because the story itself has value?
7. Does it work as a story independent of the product?
