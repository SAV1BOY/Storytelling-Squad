# Brand Manifestos: Swipe Analysis

## Purpose
Analysis of brand manifestos that use narrative and declaration to define what a
brand stands for. A manifesto is not a mission statement — it's a battle cry.

---

## 1. Apple — "Here's to the Crazy Ones" (1997)

> "Here's to the crazy ones. The misfits. The rebels. The troublemakers. The round
> pegs in the square holes. The ones who see things differently. They're not fond of
> rules. And they have no respect for the status quo.
>
> You can quote them, disagree with them, glorify or vilify them. About the only thing
> you can't do is ignore them. Because they change things. They push the human race
> forward.
>
> And while some may see them as the crazy ones, we see genius. Because the people
> who are crazy enough to think they can change the world are the ones who do."

**Analysis:**
- No product mentioned. Zero. This is a values declaration.
- The audience sees themselves as the subject: "Am I a crazy one? Yes."
- Rhythmic listing creates momentum (misfits, rebels, troublemakers).
- The final line is the brand promise: "the ones who do."
- Written by Rob Siltanen at TBWA\Chiat\Day, narrated by Richard Dreyfuss.

## 2. Nike — "Just Do It" Extended Manifesto

> "If you have a body, you are an athlete. It's not about being the best. It's about
> being better than you were yesterday. Don't wait for perfect conditions. They don't
> exist. Every champion was once a contender who refused to give up.
>
> The finish line is just the beginning of a whole new race. Your only limit is you.
> So move. Run. Jump. Throw. Swim. Lift. Fight. Dance. Walk. Crawl if you have to.
> Just do it."

**Analysis:**
- Inclusive: "if you have a body, you are an athlete" — expands the audience from elite athletes to everyone.
- Action verbs dominate: move, run, jump, throw. The language enacts the message.
- No features, no product, no price. Pure identity.
- The escalation from "run" to "crawl" acknowledges that action doesn't require grace.

## 3. Patagonia — "Don't Buy This Jacket" (2011)

> "It's Black Friday, the day in the year retail turns from red to black and
> consumers spend $45 billion on stuff. But there's a 'dirtier' side... Because
> Patagonia wants to last, we spend time and money building things that will.
>
> But as is true of all things we make, this jacket costs the earth. So before
> you buy it, we ask that you consider whether you really need it. Reduce what
> you buy. Repair what you can. Reuse what you already own. And recycle
> everything else."

**Analysis:**
- Anti-consumerist message from a consumer brand. The paradox IS the point.
- Builds trust by seemingly undermining sales. (Revenue actually increased after this ad.)
- Specificity: names the environmental cost of one jacket.
- CTA is the opposite of every other brand: "Don't buy this."
- Values override commerce. The manifesto proves the brand means what it says.

## 4. Dove — "Real Beauty" Campaign Manifesto

> "For too long, beauty has been defined by narrow, stifling stereotypes. We believe
> real beauty comes in many shapes, sizes, ages, and colors. We believe beauty should
> be a source of confidence and not anxiety. We believe that when women are free to
> define beauty for themselves, they are free to be themselves."

**Analysis:**
- Names the villain: "narrow, stifling stereotypes."
- Repeats "We believe" — anaphora creates credibility through conviction.
- The manifesto created a movement, not a campaign. It's been running since 2004.
- The product (soap) is nowhere in the manifesto. The identity is everything.

---

## 5. Mailchimp — "Did You Mean Mailchimp?"

Not a traditional manifesto, but a brand philosophy expressed through creative:
Mailchimp's 2017 campaign deliberately misspelled and mispronounced their own name
(MailShrimp, KaleLimp, JailBlimp) and created short films, products, and experiences
around each misspelling.

**Analysis:**
- The manifesto is implicit: "We don't take ourselves too seriously."
- Playfulness as brand identity — the campaign said more about Mailchimp's character than any mission statement.
- User-generated: audiences started creating their own misheard versions.
- Won a Grand Prix at Cannes. Proved that brand personality IS brand strategy.

## How to Write a Brand Manifesto

### Structure
1. **Name the enemy.** What's wrong with the world/industry that your brand exists to fight?
2. **State the belief.** "We believe…" What do you stand for?
3. **Describe the people.** Who are you for? What makes them different?
4. **Make the declaration.** What are you going to do about it?
5. **Issue the invitation.** How does the reader join?

### Voice Guidelines
- Short sentences. Declarative. Present tense.
- Anaphora (repeating phrase at the start of successive sentences) builds rhythm.
- No jargon. No corporate speak. Human words.
- Read it aloud. If it doesn't give you chills, it's not ready.

### Length
- A manifesto is 100–300 words. If it's longer, it's an essay.
- Every word must earn its place. Cut ruthlessly.

### Test
- Would an employee be proud to read this aloud?
- Would a customer share this without being asked?
- Does it make someone who disagrees feel excluded? (Good — it should.)
- Does it make someone who agrees feel seen? (Essential — it must.)

---

## Manifesto vs. Mission Statement vs. Tagline

| Element | Length | Purpose | Example |
|---------|--------|---------|---------|
| **Tagline** | 3–7 words | Instant recognition | "Just Do It" |
| **Mission statement** | 1–2 sentences | Strategic direction | "To bring inspiration and innovation to every athlete in the world" |
| **Manifesto** | 100–300 words | Emotional declaration | "Here's to the crazy ones…" |
