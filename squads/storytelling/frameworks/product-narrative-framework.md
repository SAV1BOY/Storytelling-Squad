# Product Narrative Framework
> Tell product stories through origin, need, transformation, and vision — making products characters with arcs.

## Overview
Every product has a story, but most companies never tell it. They list features, specifications, and pricing — the equivalent of describing a character by their height and weight instead of their personality and purpose. This framework treats the product as a character in the customer's story: it has an origin (why it was created), a purpose (the need it serves), a power (the transformation it enables), and a destiny (the vision it is moving toward). When customers understand a product's story, they do not just buy it — they believe in it. Product narrative creates the emotional scaffolding that turns transactions into relationships.

## Core Methodology
The Product Narrative Framework operates on four story beats:

1. **Origin (Why It Exists)** — Every product was born from a moment of frustration, insight, or vision. The origin story humanizes the product by connecting it to a real human experience. Products with origin stories feel intentional rather than manufactured.

2. **Need (The Problem It Solves)** — The need must be articulated from the customer's perspective, not the company's. It is not "we built a faster processor." It is "you are tired of waiting for your tools to catch up with your thinking." The need creates the emotional gap the product fills.

3. **Transformation (How Life Changes)** — This is the product's superpower told as a before/after narrative. Not features but experiences. Not specifications but moments. The transformation must be specific, vivid, and emotionally resonant.

4. **Vision (Where It Is Going)** — The product's future trajectory, framed as a promise to the customer. The vision tells the customer that buying in now is joining a journey, not just a transaction. This creates loyalty beyond the current feature set.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Excavate the Origin
- Interview the product creator, designer, or founding engineer about the moment the product idea crystallized
- Look for the human story behind the technical decision: What frustration, failure, or observation sparked the product?
- Capture the specific scene: "We were in the middle of a client presentation when the demo crashed for the third time. Everyone stared at the blank screen. In that silence, I thought: there has to be a better way."
- If the product originated from customer demand, tell that story: which customer asked for it, why, and what happened when they first used it
- The origin should answer: "Why this product, in this form, at this time?"

### Step 2: Articulate the Need Through Customer Language
- Map the product's functionality to the customer's emotional experience
- For each feature, ask: "What does this let the customer stop doing?" and "What does this let the customer start doing?"
- Express the need using the customer's own words (from interviews, reviews, support tickets)
- Quantify the need where possible: "Marketers spend an average of 11 hours per week on tasks this product eliminates."
- The need should create recognition: the customer reads it and thinks "that is exactly my problem"
- Prioritize one primary need and 2-3 supporting needs — do not list every problem the product could theoretically solve

### Step 3: Narrate the Transformation
- Describe the customer's experience from first use to established user
- **First encounter**: "The first time you open the dashboard, you see every data source in one view. Most users tell us they just stare at it for a minute."
- **First win**: "Within the first week, users typically save 3-5 hours. The most common first reaction is relief — then frustration that they did not switch sooner."
- **Established use**: "After 30 days, the product becomes invisible — it just works. Users stop thinking about the problem because the problem stops existing."
- Use sensory and emotional language: what does the transformation feel like, not just what it does
- Include the negative space: what the customer no longer has to worry about, think about, or spend time on

### Step 4: Cast the Vision
- Where is the product going, and why should the customer care?
- Frame the vision as the customer's future, not the company's roadmap: "We are building toward a world where [customer experience] never requires [current pain]."
- Tie the vision to the origin: "We started because [origin]. We will not stop until [vision]."
- Make the vision ambitious but credible: show the trajectory from where the product is now to where it is going
- Include the customer in the vision: "Every feature request, every support conversation, every piece of feedback shapes where we go next. You are building this with us."

### Step 5: Map the Narrative to Product Touchpoints
- **Landing page**: The full four-beat narrative in condensed form. Origin in the "About" section. Need in the hero copy. Transformation in feature sections. Vision in the closing.
- **Onboarding**: The transformation narrative guides the first-use experience. Each onboarding step reveals a new capability tied to a specific need.
- **Feature announcements**: Each new feature is a chapter in the ongoing product story. Connect it to the origin and the vision.
- **Pricing page**: Frame pricing tiers in terms of transformation level, not feature access. "Starter: solve the problem. Pro: scale the solution. Enterprise: transform the organization."
- **Help documentation**: Even support content can reference the narrative. "This feature was designed to solve [need]. Here is how to get the most from it."

### Step 6: Maintain Narrative Consistency Through Product Evolution
- As the product evolves, the narrative must evolve with it — but the core origin and vision should remain stable
- Each major update or pivot should be framed as a chapter in the ongoing story, not a contradiction of the previous story
- Assign a narrative owner (product marketing, ideally) who approves all product storytelling
- Create a product narrative guide that documents the origin, need, transformation, and vision — updated quarterly

## Examples

### Example 1: Project Management SaaS
- **Origin**: "We built this tool because we nearly lost our first startup to miscommunication. Six people on the team, working on the same project, with six different versions of the truth. We swore that if we ever built another company, we would build the tool we needed first."
- **Need**: "Creative teams do not need more project management. They need less confusion. They need to know what is happening, what is next, and who is responsible — without sitting through another status meeting."
- **Transformation**: "Before: Monday morning begins with a 45-minute standup where half the team reports what the other half already knows. After: Monday morning begins with a glance at the board. Everyone sees the same truth. The standup becomes a 10-minute strategy session."
- **Vision**: "We are building toward a world where project management is invisible. Where creative teams spend 100% of their energy on creative work because the coordination happens automatically."

### Example 2: Consumer Health App
- **Origin**: "Our founder's daughter has severe food allergies. Every grocery trip was 90 minutes of reading labels, Googling ingredients, and second-guessing. She built the first version of the app to scan barcodes and flag allergens. Her daughter called it 'the magic phone.'"
- **Need**: "40 million Americans manage food allergies. Every meal is a risk assessment. Every restaurant is a negotiation. Every new product is a research project. It should not be this hard to feed your family safely."
- **Transformation**: "Scan any product in 2 seconds and know instantly if it is safe for your family's specific allergies. No more squinting at labels. No more Googling on your phone in the grocery aisle. No more anxiety at the dinner table."
- **Vision**: "We are building toward a world where no parent has to choose between convenience and their child's safety. Where every food product, every restaurant menu, and every school cafeteria is accessible to every family."

## When to Use
- Product launch and go-to-market messaging
- Landing page and website copy
- Product marketing materials (one-pagers, decks, demos)
- Onboarding and first-use experience design
- Feature announcement content
- Investor presentations focused on the product

## When NOT to Use
- When the product is a commodity with no meaningful differentiation (narrative cannot create differentiation that does not exist)
- When the audience only cares about specifications and pricing (technical procurement)
- When the product is too early for a transformation narrative (pre-product-market-fit)
- When the product story would reveal competitive advantages that should remain confidential

## Integration
- Origin story connects to the **Founder Story Framework** when they are intertwined
- Need articulation draws from the **Discovery Layer** audience research
- Transformation narrative feeds the **Sales Story Framework** and **Case Study Story Framework**
- Vision connects to the **Brand Story Framework** future state
- Product touchpoint mapping connects to the **Delivery Layer** for format adaptation

## Agent Routing
- **Discovery Agent**: Conducts customer and product team interviews to surface the origin and need
- **Strategy Agent**: Positions the product narrative relative to competitors and market context
- **Structure Agent**: Maps the four beats to specific product touchpoints
- **Drafting Agent**: Writes product copy, landing pages, and feature announcements in narrative form
- **Editing Agent**: Ensures consistency across all product narrative touchpoints
- Route to this framework when the task involves telling the story of a product — why it exists, what it does, and where it is going
