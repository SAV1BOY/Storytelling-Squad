# Duarte's Presentation Form
> A visual design philosophy that treats presentations as a distinct communication medium with its own grammar—where slides function as visual evidence, not teleprompter screens.

## Overview

Nancy Duarte's Presentation Form framework, developed through her work at Duarte Inc. and detailed in *slide:ology* (2008), establishes that presentations are a unique medium—neither documents nor speeches, but a hybrid form with its own design rules. The most common presentation failure is treating slides as projected documents (walls of text) or decorative backgrounds (stock photos with no function). Duarte's framework provides a grammar for visual storytelling where every design element serves a communicative purpose.

For marketers, Presentation Form is essential because presentations are one of the most frequent yet most poorly executed brand touchpoints. A well-designed sales deck communicates professionalism, clarity, and confidence before the content is even absorbed. A poorly designed one communicates the opposite.

## Core Methodology

Duarte's Presentation Form rests on three principles:

1. **Slides are not documents.** A slide should never be able to stand alone as a readable page. If it can, it belongs in a handout, not on screen. Slides exist to amplify and illustrate the speaker's words, not replace them.

2. **One idea per slide.** Each slide should communicate exactly one concept. When an audience must process multiple ideas simultaneously, comprehension drops. The moment you add a second point, you need a second slide.

3. **Visual hierarchy creates narrative.** The arrangement of elements on a slide (size, position, contrast, whitespace) guides the audience's eye in a specific order. This sequence IS the slide's narrative. Poorly designed slides let the eye wander; well-designed slides tell a visual micro-story.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Create the Slide Outline Before Any Design
Map the presentation's narrative arc (using the Sparkline) and assign one key idea to each slide. Write each idea as a complete sentence—this becomes the slide's "assertion headline." The headline is not a topic label ("Q3 Revenue") but an assertion ("Q3 Revenue Exceeded Projections by 23%").

**The assertion test:** If someone read only the headlines in order, they should understand the complete argument without seeing any supporting content.

### Step 2: Classify Each Slide by Type
Duarte identifies five slide types, each with distinct design requirements:

**Data Slides** — Present quantitative evidence. Design rules: one chart per slide, clear labels, highlighted insight (circle the data point that matters), minimal gridlines and decoration. The audience should know what to conclude in 3 seconds.

**Diagram Slides** — Show relationships, processes, or structures. Design rules: use spatial position to encode meaning (left-to-right = time, top-to-bottom = hierarchy), consistent shapes for consistent concepts, directional flow indicators.

**Concept Slides** — Communicate abstract ideas through metaphor or analogy. Design rules: strong central image, minimal text (5 words or fewer), visual metaphor that audiences can immediately decode.

**Evidence Slides** — Present quotes, testimonials, screenshots, or photographic proof. Design rules: the evidence is the hero. Minimize surrounding elements. Let quotes breathe with generous whitespace.

**Bridge Slides** — Mark transitions between sections. Signal to the audience that the topic is shifting. Design rules: stark visual contrast from surrounding slides (different background color, full-bleed image, or single large word). These are the presentation's paragraph breaks.

### Step 3: Apply the Three-Second Rule
Every slide must communicate its key message within three seconds of appearing. If the audience needs more than three seconds to understand what they're looking at, the slide is too complex.

**Three-second design checklist:**
- Is there a clear visual focal point? (One element that the eye goes to first)
- Is the assertion headline readable from the back of the room?
- Is the supporting visual immediately interpretable without the speaker's narration?
- Have non-essential elements been removed?

### Step 4: Design for the Dual Channel
Audiences process visual and auditory information through separate cognitive channels (Allan Paivey's dual coding theory). Presentations are most effective when these channels complement rather than compete. When a slide contains text that the speaker reads aloud, the channels collide—the audience reads ahead and stops listening.

**Dual channel guidelines:**
- If the speaker is telling a story, the slide should show an image, not text.
- If the slide displays data, the speaker should narrate the insight, not read the numbers.
- If a quote is on screen, the speaker should remain silent while the audience reads, then comment.
- Never have the speaker read bullets aloud. If bullets exist, they should appear one at a time as the speaker provides context.

### Step 5: Establish a Visual System
Create a consistent design system for the presentation before designing individual slides:
- **Color palette:** 3-4 colors with defined roles (primary, secondary, accent, background)
- **Typography:** Two fonts maximum (one for headlines, one for body). Size hierarchy: headline > subhead > body > caption
- **Grid system:** Consistent margins, alignment guides, and placement zones
- **Imagery style:** Photography vs. illustration vs. iconography. Consistent treatment (color vs. B&W, cropping style, filter)
- **Transition logic:** Consistent transitions that encode meaning (slide transitions for topic shifts, builds for progressive disclosure within a topic)

### Step 6: Design Slide Builds for Progressive Revelation
Complex information should be revealed progressively rather than displayed all at once. A build sequence turns a single slide into a mini-narrative:
- **Step 1:** The framework or structure appears (empty diagram)
- **Step 2:** The first element populates (with speaker narration)
- **Step 3:** Additional elements appear sequentially
- **Step 4:** The complete picture is visible, and the audience understands the relationships because they watched the picture assemble

This technique prevents cognitive overload and creates a sense of discovery.

## Examples

### Example 1: Sales Deck Redesign
**Before (typical):** 30 slides, each containing 6-8 bullet points, company logo in every corner, clip art icons, inconsistent fonts, data tables with 20+ rows.

**After (Duarte Presentation Form):**
- Slide 1 (Bridge): Full-bleed photo of the customer's industry. One word: "Change."
- Slides 2-5 (Concept): The customer's challenge illustrated through a single visual metaphor per slide.
- Slides 6-8 (Data): Three charts, one per slide, each with a circled insight and assertion headline. "Your team loses 11 hours/week to manual data entry."
- Slides 9-12 (Diagram): Product workflow shown as a progressive build, one step per click.
- Slides 13-15 (Evidence): Customer quotes, one per slide, in large type with generous whitespace and a headshot.
- Slide 16 (Bridge): Return to the opening image, now transformed. "Change. Delivered."

**Result:** Fewer slides that communicate more clearly, with a visual narrative that builds conviction.

### Example 2: Quarterly Business Review
**Before:** 60 dense slides packed with tables, read aloud by the presenter. Audience checks email after slide 5.

**After:**
- Executive summary slide with 3 assertion headlines linked to the full analysis
- One data slide per KPI, each with the number highlighted and the trend annotated
- Bridge slides between sections using the company's strategic themes as headers
- A "deep dive" appendix for follow-up, distributed as a document (not presented)
- Total presented slides: 18. Total time: 20 minutes. Remaining 40 minutes: discussion.

## When to Use

- Designing or redesigning any slide presentation
- Training teams on presentation best practices
- Reviewing a deck for visual and communicative quality
- Creating brand presentation templates
- Any situation where slides are projected for an audience (keynote, sales meeting, webinar, training)

## When NOT to Use

- Documents that will be read, not presented (reports, proposals, memos)—these need different design rules
- Presentations where the slides must function as standalone documents (e.g., decks emailed without a presenter)—in this case, create two versions: a presentation deck and a reading deck
- Informal meetings where a whiteboard or conversation is more appropriate
- When the presenter's charisma and delivery are the primary asset and slides would distract

## Integration

Presentation Form integrates with:
- **Duarte's Resonate Sparkline** — The Sparkline determines the narrative arc; Presentation Form determines the visual execution of each beat
- **Duarte's STAR Moment** — The STAR Moment requires specific visual design to maximize impact (full-bleed images, dramatic reveals, minimal text)
- **Duarte's Audience Journey** — Slide design should match the audience's psychological state (simpler slides during Resistance, more complex during Understanding)
- **Klaff's Frame Control** — Visual design reinforces frame control; premium design signals authority
- **Miller's Grunt Test** — Apply the grunt test to each slide: can a caveman understand the point within 3 seconds?

## Agent Routing

Route to Presentation Form when:
- The user is designing slides or a visual presentation
- The user asks about slide design, deck layout, or visual communication
- A presentation draft exists and needs visual refinement
- The user mentions Duarte's design principles or slide:ology
- The task involves creating brand presentation templates

Do NOT route here when:
- The user needs narrative structure for the presentation (route to Sparkline)
- The user needs audience psychology insights (route to Audience Journey)
- The user needs a single memorable moment (route to STAR Moment)
- The content is not a presentation (route to appropriate narrative framework)
- The user needs written content design rather than slide design
