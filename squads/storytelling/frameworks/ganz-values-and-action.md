# Ganz's Values and Action
> A framework for identifying the deep emotional values that motivate human behavior and translating them into narrative structures that move audiences from passive agreement to committed action.

## Overview

Marshall Ganz's Values and Action framework is the theoretical foundation beneath his entire Public Narrative system. While the Story of Self, Story of Us, and Story of Now provide narrative structures, Values and Action provides the underlying engine: the insight that people are moved to act not by facts, data, or rational argument alone, but by emotional engagement with deeply held values.

Ganz draws on moral psychology, organizing theory, and narrative tradition to argue that values are not abstract principles people hold intellectually—they are emotional dispositions experienced in the body. Hope, outrage, compassion, dignity, solidarity—these are not ideas but feelings that generate the energy for action. The storyteller's job is to activate specific values that connect to specific actions, creating an unbroken chain from emotion to behavior.

For marketers, this framework provides the missing link between brand values (which companies proclaim) and brand action (which audiences observe). When values remain abstract, they are interchangeable corporate clichés. When values are activated through narrative and connected to specific actions, they become differentiating forces.

## Core Methodology

Ganz's Values and Action framework operates on four principles:

1. **Values are emotional, not intellectual.** Saying "we value innovation" activates nothing. Telling a story about the moment your team chose the uncertain path over the safe one—and what it cost them—activates the value of courage. The narrative creates the emotional experience; the value is the name we give to that experience.

2. **Values exist in tension.** Every meaningful value exists in creative tension with another value. Courage vs. prudence. Justice vs. mercy. Innovation vs. reliability. Narratives that engage with the tension between competing values are more powerful than narratives that celebrate a single value unchallenged. The tension is where moral seriousness lives.

3. **Action is the bridge between values and outcomes.** Values without action are sentiment. Action without values is mechanics. The framework insists that every narrative must complete the circuit: a value is activated (through story), a choice is presented (through framing), and an action is specified (through the call to action). Incomplete circuits—stories that inspire but don't direct, or calls to action that lack emotional grounding—fail to produce sustained behavior.

4. **Sources of public motivation are distinct from private motivation.** Ganz identifies specific emotions that move people to public action: hope (the belief that change is possible), anger (the response to perceived injustice), solidarity (the feeling of shared identity and purpose), urgency (the sense that the moment demands action), and YCMAD ("You Can Make A Difference"—the sense of personal agency within collective action). These public emotions are distinct from private emotions like happiness or sadness, which may be felt individually but do not inherently generate collective behavior.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify the Core Value
Begin by naming the specific value the narrative must activate. Be precise. "Customer-centricity" is too abstract. "The belief that every customer deserves to be heard, especially when their feedback is uncomfortable" is a value that can be felt and enacted.

**Value identification exercise:**
- What specific moment in the brand's history revealed what it truly stands for?
- When was the brand (or its people) forced to choose between competing goods?
- What would the brand sacrifice before it would compromise this value?
- If this value were a person, how would they behave in a crisis?

The answers to these questions reveal the value in its lived, emotional form—not its press-release form.

### Step 2: Map the Value's Emotional Signature
Each value, when activated, produces a specific emotional response. Map this precisely:

- **Justice:** Activates righteous anger (when violated) and satisfaction (when fulfilled)
- **Courage:** Activates admiration and aspiration (when witnessed) and fear/resolve (when required)
- **Compassion:** Activates empathy and tenderness (when stories are told) and guilt (when inaction is revealed)
- **Innovation:** Activates excitement and possibility (when demonstrated) and restlessness (when stagnation is named)
- **Integrity:** Activates trust (when consistent) and outrage (when betrayed)
- **Community:** Activates belonging and warmth (when inclusive) and isolation (when excluded)

The emotional signature tells you what your audience should FEEL at each stage of the narrative. If the narrative doesn't produce the mapped emotion, it isn't activating the value.

### Step 3: Construct the Value Narrative
Using Ganz's challenge-choice-outcome structure, build a narrative that activates the value:

**Challenge:** A situation that puts the value under pressure. The value must be at risk—either it will be honored at a cost, or it will be abandoned for convenience. The challenge must be real and specific. Abstract challenges ("the market is competitive") do not activate values. Specific challenges ("our biggest client asked us to do something that would hurt our smallest clients") activate them.

**Choice:** The moment of decision where the value is either upheld or abandoned. This is the narrative's moral center. The choice must be genuinely difficult—if the right action is obvious and costless, there is no drama and no value activation. Show what was sacrificed to honor the value.

**Outcome:** The result of the choice. In a prescriptive narrative, honoring the value leads to a positive outcome (though not necessarily an easy one). In a cautionary narrative, abandoning the value leads to negative consequences. The outcome must feel earned, not guaranteed.

### Step 4: Connect Value to Action
The activated value must flow directly into a specific action the audience can take. The action should:

- **Express the value:** The action itself should embody the value it springs from. If the value is transparency, the action should involve sharing or opening up, not just clicking a button.
- **Be proportional:** The action should match the emotional intensity the narrative creates. A deeply moving story followed by "subscribe to our newsletter" feels like a betrayal. A deeply moving story followed by "join us in committing to [specific meaningful behavior]" honors the emotion.
- **Be immediate:** The gap between emotion and action must be as short as possible. Every hour of delay between value activation and action opportunity reduces conversion.

### Step 5: Sustain the Value Through Repeated Activation
A single narrative activates a value temporarily. Sustained behavior requires repeated activation across multiple touchpoints and over time. Design a value activation cadence:

- **Weekly:** Social content that demonstrates the value in action (micro-stories, behind-the-scenes moments, customer highlights)
- **Monthly:** Deeper narratives that explore the value's complexity and tensions (blog posts, newsletter stories, video features)
- **Quarterly:** Major narratives that test the value under new pressures (campaigns, events, product launches)
- **Annually:** A defining narrative that reaffirms and potentially evolves the value (annual report, brand film, keynote)

### Step 6: Audit for Values Integrity
The greatest risk in values-based marketing is the gap between proclaimed values and observed behavior. Conduct regular audits:

- **Internal audit:** Does the company's internal behavior reflect the proclaimed values? Employees will not authentically narrate values they don't experience.
- **Customer audit:** Do customers experience the values in their interactions with the brand? Survey for values alignment, not just satisfaction.
- **Content audit:** Does every piece of content either activate or at minimum not contradict the core values?
- **Decision audit:** In the last three major business decisions, were the proclaimed values a factor? Can you tell the story of how they influenced the outcome?

## Examples

### Example 1: Outdoor Brand Activating the Value of Stewardship
**Core Value:** Stewardship—the belief that using nature creates an obligation to protect it.
**Emotional Signature:** Reverence (when in nature), guilt (when witnessing degradation), pride (when contributing to preservation), urgency (when the window for action narrows).

**Challenge Narrative:** "Last year, we discovered that one of our most popular materials was contributing to microplastic pollution in the waterways where our customers fish, hike, and camp. The same rivers we feature in our catalogs."

**Choice:** "We had two options: reformulate the material at twice the cost, delaying our spring line by three months. Or keep using it—legally compliant, industry standard, and invisible to customers. We chose the reformulation. It cost us $4 million and our biggest retail window of the year."

**Outcome:** "Our spring line launched in June instead of March. Revenue was down 15% that quarter. But our customer retention hit an all-time high. And this year, 23 other brands adopted the same material standard. Sometimes losing a quarter is how you win a decade."

**Action Connection:** "Every product you buy from us now meets this standard. And 1% of every sale funds waterway restoration. You're not just buying gear—you're casting a vote for the rivers."

### Example 2: B2B Platform Activating the Value of Empowerment
**Core Value:** Empowerment—the belief that every team member, regardless of title, should have the tools and authority to solve problems.

**Challenge Narrative:** "One of our enterprise clients had a junior analyst who noticed a data anomaly that could have saved the company $2M. She flagged it in an email. It sat in her manager's inbox for three weeks. By the time anyone acted, the window had closed."

**Choice:** "That client came to us and said: 'We need a system where anyone can raise a flag and everyone can see it.' We built that feature—even though our enterprise buyers (the managers) initially resisted it. We chose to build for the analyst, not the buyer."

**Outcome:** "Six months later, that same client's junior team members had identified $8M in savings through the flagging system. The managers who initially resisted became the feature's biggest advocates. Building for the people closest to the work turned out to be building for everyone."

**Action Connection:** "Try the open-flagging feature in your next sprint. Give every team member—not just leads—the ability to raise, track, and resolve issues. Watch what happens when you trust the people closest to the problem."

## When to Use

- Brand strategy and positioning (defining what the brand truly stands for)
- Campaign development rooted in purpose rather than promotion
- Internal culture work (aligning team behavior with brand values)
- Cause marketing and social impact campaigns
- Any content where the goal is to move the audience to committed, sustained action
- Crisis communications (reaffirming values under pressure)
- Recruitment marketing (attracting values-aligned talent)

## When NOT to Use

- Purely transactional content where values-based messaging would feel forced
- When the brand hasn't done the internal work to authentically hold the proclaimed values (the audit will reveal this)
- Product-feature marketing where the audience needs functional information, not emotional activation
- When the audience is hostile to values-based messaging (some B2B technical audiences prefer pure utility)
- When the urgency is artificial and the values activation would feel manipulative

## Integration

Ganz's Values and Action integrates with:
- **Ganz's Public Narrative** — Values and Action provides the theoretical engine; Public Narrative provides the three-act delivery structure
- **Ganz's Story of Self** — The narrator's personal values are revealed through Story of Self using this framework's challenge-choice-outcome structure
- **Ganz's Story of Us** — Shared values are the foundation of collective identity
- **Ganz's Story of Now** — Urgency is created by framing the present as a moment when values are at stake
- **Coyne's Controlling Idea** — The controlling idea is the narrative expression of the core value: "[Value] is achieved when [value-aligned action]"
- **Campbell's Archetypes** — The brand archetype expresses values through character identity
- **Miller's StoryBrand SB7** — StoryBrand's "philosophical failure" represents the consequence of abandoning the core value

## Agent Routing

Route to Values and Action when:
- The user needs to identify and articulate authentic brand values (not platitudes)
- The user is building a purpose-driven or cause-related campaign
- Content needs to activate specific emotions that drive behavior
- The user asks about connecting values to action, or about values-based marketing
- The user mentions Ganz's values framework explicitly
- Brand messaging feels generic and needs grounding in authentic conviction

Do NOT route here when:
- The user needs narrative structure rather than values identification (route to Public Narrative or Story of Self)
- The user needs presentation mechanics (route to Duarte)
- The user needs pitch or persuasion tactics (route to Klaff)
- The focus is on story selection rather than values activation (route to Hall Story Selection Matrix)
- The user needs quick structural templates (route to Condensed Monomyth or Story Circle)
