# Dicks' Storyworthy Methodology
> The complete system for finding, crafting, and telling stories that matter — centered on the "5-second moment" of realization and the discipline of daily story discovery.

## Overview

Matthew Dicks is a 59-time Moth StorySLAM champion, author of "Storyworthy," and one of the most prolific personal storytellers alive. His methodology is the gold standard for personal narrative craft, and it translates powerfully to brand storytelling, content creation, founder narratives, and any context where authentic human stories are needed.

The Storyworthy methodology rests on a radical premise: the best stories are not about the most dramatic events in your life. They are about small moments of realization — what Dicks calls the "5-second moment" — when your understanding of yourself or the world permanently changed. A story about surviving a car crash is not inherently better than a story about a conversation at a kitchen table. What matters is the transformation.

This framework covers the complete Storyworthy system: finding stories (Homework for Life), identifying the 5-second moment, structuring the narrative (beginning/end opposites, but/therefore test), and crafting for maximum impact.

## Core Methodology

### The 5-Second Moment

The 5-second moment is the emotional and narrative center of every storyworthy story. It is the instant when something shifts inside the protagonist — a realization, a decision, a change in understanding that cannot be undone. Everything in the story exists to set up and pay off this moment.

Characteristics of a genuine 5-second moment:
- It is internal, not external. The car crash is not the moment; the realization you had while trapped in the car is the moment.
- It is irreversible. After this moment, the protagonist cannot go back to who they were before.
- It is specific. "I realized I needed to change" is not a 5-second moment. "I realized, standing in my daughter's doorway at 2 AM, that I had become my father" is.
- It is small. The most powerful moments are intimate, not epic. They happen in kitchens, parking lots, and phone calls, not on mountaintops.
- It is universal. The specific moment belongs to one person, but the underlying human truth resonates with everyone.

### Finding the 5-Second Moment

Most people do not know their own stories. They know their anecdotes (funny things that happened), their résumé bullets (impressive things they achieved), and their traumas (terrible things they survived). None of these are automatically stories. They become stories only when a 5-second moment is identified within them.

To find the moment, ask:
- "How was I different after this experience than before?"
- "What did I believe before that I no longer believed after?"
- "If I could freeze one instant in this experience and live in it forever, which instant would it be?"
- "What is the sentence I could not have said before this happened that I can say now?"

### Homework for Life

Homework for Life is Dicks' daily practice for story discovery. Every night before bed, reflect on the day and record one storyworthy moment in a single sentence. Not a journal entry. Not a diary. One sentence that captures a moment that could become a story.

Rules:
- Do it every day without exception
- Write only one sentence (two at most)
- The moment does not need to be dramatic; it needs to be real
- If nothing seems storyworthy, you are not looking hard enough — lower your threshold
- Use a spreadsheet, not a notebook (the date column creates accountability)

Over months and years, this practice produces two outcomes:
1. An enormous reservoir of potential stories, organized by date
2. A trained eye that spots storyworthy moments in real time, not just in retrospect

### The Beginning/End Opposites Rule

The first sentence and the last sentence of a story should be as opposite as possible. This structural rule ensures that the story contains genuine transformation — the thing that makes audiences care.

If your story begins with "I was confident" it should end somewhere near "I had never been so unsure." If it begins with "I had nothing," it should end near a version of abundance (not necessarily material). The opposition creates an arc that the audience can feel.

This is not a formula for happy endings. The opposites can move in any direction: positive to negative, certainty to doubt, isolation to connection, ignorance to knowledge. What matters is the distance between the two poles.

### The But/Therefore Test

Dicks, like Howell, uses the but/therefore test (from Randy Olson) to check narrative momentum. Read through your story and mark every transition between beats. If the transitions are "and then... and then... and then..." you have a list, not a story. If the transitions are "but... therefore... but... therefore..." you have narrative tension.

Every "but" introduces a complication. Every "therefore" introduces a consequence. The alternation between the two creates the push-pull rhythm that keeps audiences engaged.

### Stake Placement

Stakes must be established early. The audience needs to know what the protagonist stands to lose before the complications begin. Without stakes, complications are just events. With stakes, complications are threats.

Dicks identifies several types of stakes:
- **Elephant:** The big thing the audience is waiting to find out about. Place it early and let the audience carry it through the story.
- **Backpack:** Load the audience with the protagonist's burden before the journey begins. The more they carry, the more they feel the weight of each complication.
- **Breadcrumbs:** Small hints dropped throughout the story that keep the audience leaning forward, wanting to know what happens next.
- **Hourglass:** The feeling that time is running out. Create temporal urgency.
- **Crystal Ball:** A false prediction about what will happen that the audience carries, creating anticipation and eventual surprise.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Mine for Stories

Use three discovery methods:
1. **Homework for Life** (daily practice, see dedicated framework)
2. **First/Last/Best/Worst** (structured prompt exercise, see dedicated framework)
3. **Crash and Burn** (free-association brainstorm: write every story idea you can think of in 10 minutes, no filtering, no judgment)

### Step 2: Identify the 5-Second Moment

For each potential story, answer: "What is the single instant of realization or change?" If you cannot identify it, the story may be an anecdote, not a narrative. Set it aside and mine deeper.

### Step 3: Determine the Beginning from the End

Once you have the 5-second moment (which is near the end of the story), determine the opposite state. That opposite becomes your opening. If the moment is "I realized I was worthy of love," the opening must establish a world where the protagonist does not feel worthy.

### Step 4: Build the Narrative Spine

Using but/therefore logic, outline the beats between the opening state and the 5-second moment. Each beat should either introduce a complication (but) or a consequence (therefore). Eliminate any beat that is connected by "and then" — it is likely unnecessary.

### Step 5: Place the Stakes

Decide which stake techniques to use. At minimum, establish the elephant (what is this story really about?) in the first 20% of the narrative. Add backpack elements to ensure the audience feels the protagonist's burden.

### Step 6: Craft the Opening

The opening sentence must do three things simultaneously:
1. Establish the opposite state from the ending
2. Ground the audience in a specific time and place
3. Create enough curiosity to earn the audience's attention for the next sentence

Dicks' rule: the opening should be action, not setup. "I was standing in a Walmart parking lot at 3 AM" is better than "Let me tell you about a time when I learned something important."

### Step 7: Craft the Closing

The closing should land on or immediately after the 5-second moment. Do not explain the moment. Do not editorialize. Do not tell the audience what to feel. Trust the structure. If the opening and closing are truly opposite, the transformation will be felt without being stated.

### Step 8: Test and Revise

Read the story aloud. Time it. Check:
- Does the but/therefore test hold throughout?
- Are the beginning and end genuinely opposite?
- Is the 5-second moment clear, specific, and earned?
- Are stakes established in the first 20%?
- Can you cut anything without losing the story? (If yes, cut it.)

## Examples

### Example 1: Founder Story for a Health Tech Startup

**5-second moment:** The founder, sitting in a hospital waiting room, realizes that her mother's misdiagnosis was not a medical failure but an information failure — the data existed but no one connected it.

**Opening (opposite):** "In 2018, I believed doctors knew everything. I believed that if you went to a hospital, the system would catch what was wrong with you."

**Narrative spine:** The mother's symptoms (but no one connects them). The first misdiagnosis (therefore wrong treatment). The worsening condition (but the founder trusts the system). The second opinion that reveals the truth (therefore rage, then clarity). The 5-second moment in the waiting room.

**Closing:** "I do not believe the system catches everything anymore. That is why I built one that does."

**Stakes:** The mother's health (elephant, established in the first two sentences). The founder's trust in institutions (backpack). The ticking clock of the mother's deteriorating condition (hourglass).

### Example 2: Customer Testimonial for a Career Coaching Service

**5-second moment:** The customer, preparing for a job interview, realizes for the first time that she has been apologizing for her career gaps instead of claiming them as evidence of courage.

**Opening (opposite):** "I had rehearsed the apology so many times it sounded natural. 'I took some time off, sorry about the gap on my résumé.'"

**Narrative spine:** Years of apologizing for career gaps (but feeling diminished each time). Hiring the coach (therefore new perspective). The coach asks "What were you doing during that gap?" and she answers: raising two children, caring for a dying parent, starting a business that failed. The coach says "That is not a gap. That is the most impressive part of your résumé." The 5-second moment during interview prep.

**Closing:** "The interviewer asked about the gap. For the first time, I did not apologize. I said, 'Let me tell you what I was doing.' I got the job."

## When to Use

- Crafting founder and origin stories for brands
- Developing customer testimonials and case studies that feel authentic rather than scripted
- Creating keynote speeches, podcast content, or video narratives
- Personal branding and thought leadership content
- Training teams to become better storytellers
- Mining authentic stories from interviews and workshops
- Content creation for social media, blogs, and newsletters
- Any situation where authenticity and emotional resonance are more important than information delivery

## When NOT to Use

- Data-heavy presentations where narrative is secondary to evidence (though an opening story can frame the data)
- Quick-turn tactical content that does not have room for full narrative development (use ABT)
- Strategic narrative planning at the brand level (use Howell's Story Cycle or Canvas)
- Choosing which story to tell in a specific context (use Hall's Story Selection Matrix first, then craft with Storyworthy)
- When the user does not yet have a story and needs a structural framework rather than a craft methodology (use Hall's Gap-Bridge or Howell's ABT)

## Integration

- **Dicks' Homework for Life:** The discovery engine that feeds the Storyworthy methodology with raw material.
- **Dicks' First/Last/Best/Worst:** A structured alternative to Homework for Life for finding stories in workshop or team settings.
- **Dicks' Beginning/End Rule:** The structural backbone of every Storyworthy narrative. Applied automatically within this methodology.
- **Howell's ABT Framework:** The but/therefore test within Storyworthy is the same principle. Use ABT to compress a Storyworthy narrative into a single sentence for pitching or testing.
- **Howell's Story Cycle:** The 5-second moment maps to the Victory + Moral steps of the cycle. The opening state maps to the Backstory.
- **Hall's Story Formula:** Dicks' methodology produces stories that naturally satisfy Hall's formula: identifiable character + authentic emotion + significant moment.
- **Hall's Customer Story type:** Storyworthy is the best methodology for crafting Hall's Customer Stories with maximum emotional impact.
