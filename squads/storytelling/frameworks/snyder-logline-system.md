# Snyder's Logline System
> A formula for distilling any story into a single compelling sentence that sells the concept through irony, a clear protagonist, and a visible arc.

## Overview

Blake Snyder considered the logline the most important sentence in any project. If you cannot pitch your story in one line that makes a listener lean in, the story itself may lack a compelling core. Snyder's logline system goes beyond summary—it is a diagnostic tool. A weak logline reveals a weak concept. A strong logline proves the concept has built-in audience appeal.

In marketing, the logline system applies directly to taglines, subject lines, ad copy, elevator pitches, campaign briefs, and positioning statements. The system forces clarity: who is the hero, what do they want, what's stopping them, and why is this interesting?

## Core Methodology

Snyder's logline formula contains four mandatory elements:

1. **Irony** — The logline must contain an inherent contradiction or tension that creates intrigue. "A germaphobe must survive a plague" has irony. "A doctor fights a disease" does not.
2. **A compelling mental picture** — The audience must be able to visualize the story from the logline alone. Abstract concepts fail.
3. **Audience and cost** — The logline must imply who the audience is and suggest the scope/stakes of the story.
4. **A killer title** — The title works in tandem with the logline to create a complete first impression.

The formula structure: **On the verge of [life situation], a [flawed hero type] must [action/goal] when [complication], or else [stakes].**

Not every logline uses this exact syntax, but every effective logline contains these elements.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify the Irony
The irony is the hook—the element that makes someone say "wait, tell me more." Three types of irony work in loglines:
- **Situational irony:** The hero is the worst possible person for this situation (a pacifist drafted into war)
- **Conceptual irony:** Two ideas that shouldn't go together are forced together (a dating app for divorcing couples)
- **Tonal irony:** The subject matter and treatment are mismatched (a comedy about death)

**Exercise:** State your story concept. Now ask: "What's the catch?" The catch is the irony.

### Step 2: Define the Adjective-Noun Hero
The hero must be described in two words: one adjective (the flaw or defining trait) and one noun (the role). "Burned-out teacher." "Overconfident founder." "Risk-averse investor." The adjective should create tension with the situation they face.

**Exercise:** Try five adjective-noun pairs for your hero. The best one will feel like it contains a story within itself.

### Step 3: Specify the Primal Goal
The goal must be simple, visual, and universal. Audiences connect with primal goals: survive, escape, win, save someone, find love, get home, prove worth. Abstract goals ("achieve synergy," "optimize performance") fail the logline test.

**Exercise:** Reduce your hero's goal to a verb and an object. "Win the contract." "Save the company." "Find the truth."

### Step 4: Introduce the Complication
The complication is what makes the goal difficult and interesting. It should connect to the irony. If the hero is a germaphobe during a plague, the complication isn't just "the plague is spreading"—it's "the only cure requires handling infected samples."

**Exercise:** Ask "What's the worst possible version of this obstacle for THIS specific hero?"

### Step 5: Establish Stakes
What happens if the hero fails? The stakes must be concrete and proportional. Not every story needs life-or-death stakes, but the audience must care about the outcome. Professional ruin, relationship loss, missed opportunity, public humiliation—all work if they matter to the hero.

### Step 6: Assemble and Test
Write the logline. Then apply Snyder's four tests:
- **The "So What?" Test:** Read it to someone. If they shrug, the irony is missing.
- **The "How Does It End?" Test:** If the ending is obvious from the logline, the tension is missing.
- **The "Who Cares?" Test:** If the hero isn't someone the audience identifies with, the empathy is missing.
- **The "What's Different?" Test:** If this logline could describe a dozen existing stories, the specificity is missing.

### Step 7: Iterate Through Versions
Write at least ten versions. Snyder wrote hundreds of loglines before committing to a screenplay. Vary the emphasis: lead with irony in one version, lead with stakes in another, lead with the hero's flaw in a third. The version that consistently gets the strongest reaction wins.

## Examples

### Example 1: SaaS Product Logline
**Weak version:** "A project management tool that helps teams collaborate better."
**Problem:** No irony, no hero, no stakes, no mental picture.

**Strong version:** "When a remote team scattered across 12 time zones realizes they've been building the wrong product for three months, a first-time PM must unite the team around a single source of truth—before the board meeting in 30 days."
**Why it works:** Specific hero (first-time PM = underdog), irony (building the wrong thing), clear stakes (board meeting deadline), mental picture (scattered global team).

**Tagline derived from the logline:** "One team. One truth. Zero excuses."

### Example 2: Nonprofit Campaign Logline
**Weak version:** "We help underprivileged youth access education."
**Problem:** No irony, generic, no narrative tension.

**Strong version:** "In a city that spends more per prisoner than per student, a 16-year-old who taught herself to code in a public library must convince a skeptical school board to fund a technology program—or watch her entire neighborhood lose its only path out of poverty."
**Why it works:** Devastating irony (prison vs. education spending), specific hero (self-taught 16-year-old), visible obstacle (skeptical board), universal stakes framed locally.

**Tagline derived from the logline:** "She coded her way out. Now she's going back for everyone else."

### Example 3: Campaign Brief Logline
**Context:** A B2B cybersecurity firm launching a new product.
**Logline:** "When the world's most secure companies discover that their biggest vulnerability is the software meant to protect them, an unlikely team of ethical hackers must prove that the industry's emperor has no clothes—before the next breach makes it undeniable."
**Tagline:** "Your security software is the breach."

## When to Use

- Campaign concept development (before any creative work begins)
- Pitch decks and investor presentations (the one-sentence version of your story)
- Creative briefs (the logline becomes the brief's north star)
- Email subject lines and ad headlines (logline compression)
- Internal alignment (the logline ensures everyone is telling the same story)
- Content ideation (logline each piece of content to test if the concept is compelling)
- Social media hooks (first line of a post follows logline principles)

## When NOT to Use

- When the content requires nuance that a single sentence destroys (sensitive topics, complex B2B solutions)
- When the audience is already committed and needs depth, not hooks
- For evergreen institutional content that doesn't need narrative tension
- When the story is genuinely simple and forcing irony would feel contrived

## Integration

Snyder's Logline System integrates with:
- **Snyder's Save the Cat** — The logline is created first, then the beat sheet is built to deliver on the logline's promise
- **Snyder's 10 Genres** — Genre selection informs the logline's structure (each genre implies different irony types)
- **Klaff's Pitch Anything** — Loglines are the compressed version of Klaff's pitch narrative
- **Miller's One-Liner** — Miller's one-liner formula is a marketing-specific adaptation of logline principles
- **Dicks' Five-Second Moment** — Both frameworks demand radical compression and the ability to hook in a single sentence

## Agent Routing

Route to Snyder's Logline System when:
- The user needs to articulate a campaign concept in one sentence
- The user is struggling with clarity ("I can't explain what this campaign is about")
- The task is writing headlines, subject lines, taglines, or pitch sentences
- The user needs to test whether a story concept is strong enough to develop
- The user mentions loglines, one-liners, or elevator pitches for creative concepts

Do NOT route here when:
- The user needs full narrative structure (route to Save the Cat or Hero's Journey)
- The user needs a business-context one-liner (route to Miller One-Liner specifically)
- The task is about visual storytelling rather than verbal hooks (route to Duarte)
- The user needs a multi-paragraph pitch (route to Klaff Pitch Anything)
