# Crisis Narrative Framework
> Recognition, responsibility, action, learning, rebuilding — the five-phase narrative structure for communicating through organizational crisis.

## Overview

A crisis is a story whether you tell it or not. The only question is whether you author that story or someone else does. When organizations face crises — product failures, data breaches, layoffs, public controversies, leadership failures — the narrative they construct determines whether they emerge with trust intact or permanently damaged.

This framework provides a five-phase narrative structure for crisis communication that balances transparency, accountability, and forward motion. It draws on research in organizational communication, reputation management, and the narrative psychology of trust repair.

The core principle: audiences do not expect perfection. They expect honesty, speed, and demonstrated change. A crisis narrative that acknowledges reality, takes responsibility, and shows concrete action rebuilds trust faster than silence, deflection, or premature optimism.

The five phases are not optional, and the sequence matters. Skipping Recognition sounds evasive. Skipping Responsibility sounds arrogant. Skipping Action sounds helpless. Skipping Learning sounds shallow. Skipping Rebuilding sounds like it will happen again.

## Core Methodology

**The Five Phases:**

1. **Recognition:** Acknowledge what happened, clearly and specifically, before anyone has to ask.
2. **Responsibility:** Own what is yours to own. Distinguish between cause, contribution, and context without using any of them as deflection.
3. **Action:** Describe what you are doing right now — concrete, specific, time-bound steps.
4. **Learning:** Articulate what you have learned and what systemic changes will prevent recurrence.
5. **Rebuilding:** Describe the path forward — how you will earn back trust, and what the audience can expect.

**The Speed Principle:**
The first 24 hours define the narrative. If you do not fill the narrative vacuum, speculation, anger, and misinformation will fill it for you. A fast, incomplete, honest statement beats a slow, polished, comprehensive one.

**The Audience Hierarchy:**
In crisis, you are speaking to multiple audiences simultaneously. Prioritize:
1. Those directly harmed (customers, employees, affected communities)
2. Those indirectly affected (partners, investors, broader community)
3. The general public and media

Each audience needs the same five phases but with different emphasis and detail.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Recognition — Name It Before You Frame It
- State what happened in plain language. No euphemisms, no jargon, no minimization.
- Include what you know, what you do not yet know, and when you will provide updates.
- Acknowledge the impact on the people affected. Name the harm specifically.
- Do not speculate on cause if you do not yet know. Say "we are investigating" rather than guessing.

**Template:** "On [date], [what happened]. This affected [who, specifically]. We understand the impact this has on [specific consequences for affected people]. Here is what we know so far: [facts]. Here is what we are still determining: [unknowns]. We will provide our next update by [specific time]."

### Step 2: Responsibility — Own What Is Yours
- Distinguish between:
  - **Cause:** "We caused this because [specific action or failure]"
  - **Contribution:** "Our systems/processes contributed to this, even though the trigger was [external factor]"
  - **Context:** "This happened in the context of [industry challenge], but that does not absolve us of [specific responsibility]"
- Never use "mistakes were made" passive construction. Use "we" and active voice.
- Do not blame individuals publicly. Organizations bear organizational failures.
- If the crisis is genuinely not your fault, say so — but still take responsibility for how it affects your stakeholders.

### Step 3: Action — Show, Don't Promise
- List concrete actions already taken (past tense, specific):
  - "We have shut down the affected system"
  - "We have contacted all affected users individually"
  - "We have engaged [third party] to conduct an independent review"
- List concrete actions in progress (present tense, specific):
  - "Our engineering team is rebuilding the authentication layer"
  - "We are issuing refunds to all affected accounts this week"
- Include timelines where possible. "Soon" is not a timeline. "By Friday" is.

### Step 4: Learning — Demonstrate Systemic Understanding
- Articulate the root cause (when investigation is complete) or interim findings.
- Describe systemic changes, not just fixes:
  - "We are adding a second authentication factor" (fix)
  - "We are restructuring our entire security review process to include external audits quarterly" (systemic change)
- Name what you got wrong in your processes, culture, or priorities — not just what broke technically.
- Be specific about what will be different going forward.

### Step 5: Rebuilding — Earn Trust Through Transparency
- Describe how you will demonstrate improvement over time (not just promise it).
- Offer ongoing accountability: "We will publish monthly security reports for the next 12 months."
- Invite scrutiny: "We welcome questions and will host an open Q&A on [date]."
- Acknowledge that trust rebuilding takes time and cannot be declared — it must be demonstrated.

## Examples

### Example 1: SaaS Data Breach

**Recognition:** "On March 3, unauthorized access to our systems exposed the email addresses and encrypted passwords of approximately 14,000 user accounts. We detected the breach at 2:17 AM EST and contained it by 3:45 AM. No financial data, payment information, or message content was accessed."

**Responsibility:** "This breach was caused by a vulnerability in our authentication system that we should have identified during our January security audit. Our audit process was not comprehensive enough. That is our failure."

**Action:** "We have already: forced password resets for all affected accounts, engaged CrowdStrike for forensic investigation, notified all affected users individually by email within 4 hours of detection, and reported the breach to relevant regulatory authorities. This week, we are implementing multi-factor authentication as a default for all accounts."

**Learning:** "Our investigation revealed that our security audit process checked for known vulnerability classes but did not include adversarial testing of authentication flows. We are restructuring our security program to include quarterly external penetration testing, a bug bounty program launching April 1, and a dedicated security engineering team (we are hiring three roles this month)."

**Rebuilding:** "Starting April 1, we will publish a monthly security transparency report detailing our security posture, any incidents, and the status of our improvement program. We will host a live Q&A with our CTO and new Head of Security on March 15. We know trust is earned through action over time, not through statements. We intend to earn it."

### Example 2: Product Recall (Consumer Brand)

**Recognition:** "We are voluntarily recalling 23,000 units of our Model X blender sold between January and February 2026. We have received 7 reports of the blade assembly loosening during use. Two customers reported minor cuts. We are deeply sorry for any harm caused."

**Responsibility:** "The issue was traced to a manufacturing change we implemented in December to increase production speed. We approved the change without sufficient testing under sustained high-speed use conditions. This was a failure of our quality assurance process."

**Action:** "All affected units can be returned for a full refund or free replacement with the corrected assembly. We have set up a dedicated return line at [number] and a web portal at [URL]. Returns will be processed within 48 hours. We have already halted production using the affected assembly and reverted to the previous manufacturing process."

**Learning:** "We are implementing a new protocol requiring 500-hour stress testing for any manufacturing change, with sign-off from both engineering and quality assurance. We are also establishing an independent quality review board that includes external safety experts."

**Rebuilding:** "We have been making kitchen products for 12 years, and safety is foundational to everything we do. We fell short of our own standard. Over the coming months, we will share updates on our new testing protocols and invite customers to tour our manufacturing facility. If you have questions or concerns, please reach out directly to [contact]. Every message will be answered by a person, not a bot."

## When to Use

- Any organizational crisis requiring public communication
- Product failures, recalls, or safety issues
- Data breaches or security incidents
- Layoffs or major organizational changes affecting employees and customers
- Public controversies involving leadership or company values
- Service outages or operational failures with significant customer impact

## When NOT to Use

- Minor issues that do not rise to crisis level (routine bugs, small delays) — do not over-dramatize
- Legal situations where counsel has specifically directed communication strategy (coordinate with legal, but advocate for transparency)
- Situations where the facts are still completely unknown — release a holding statement and wait for the Recognition phase to be factual
- Competitive attacks or market rumors that do not involve actual organizational failure — use PR strategy, not crisis narrative

## Integration

- **Brand Story Framework** — crisis narrative exists within the brand story; the crisis becomes a chapter, not the whole story
- **Internal Communications Story** — employees are the first audience; internal crisis narrative precedes external
- **Proof-in-Narrative** — actions and systemic changes are proof; embed them in the narrative
- **Emotional Arc Mapping** — crisis narrative follows a fall-rise arc (recognition of harm → rebuilding of trust)
- **Show Don't Tell** — "we take this seriously" is telling; specific actions are showing
- **Story Closing Patterns** — the rebuilding phase uses the Forward Look closing pattern

## Agent Routing

| Signal in brief/prompt | Route to |
|---|---|
| "Crisis" or "incident response" | Crisis Narrative Framework as primary |
| "Data breach communication" | Crisis Narrative + technical specificity guidance |
| "Product recall" | Crisis Narrative + customer-facing language |
| "Layoff announcement" | Crisis Narrative + Internal Communications Story |
| "Public controversy" | Crisis Narrative + brand alignment check |

**Routing logic:** This framework activates whenever the brief involves communicating about an organizational failure, incident, or controversy. It takes priority over all other frameworks because the stakes — trust, reputation, legal exposure — are highest. Always recommend legal review alongside this framework without letting legal review replace transparent communication.
