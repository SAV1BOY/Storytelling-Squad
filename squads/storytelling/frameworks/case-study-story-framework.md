# Case Study Story Framework
> Turn customer outcomes into compelling narratives using context, friction, action, result, and proof.

## Overview
Case studies are the most underutilized asset in marketing. Most companies produce case studies that read like spec sheets — dry recitations of features used and metrics achieved. This framework transforms customer outcomes into stories that prospects actually want to read, share, and act on. A great case study does not describe what happened; it makes the reader feel what it was like to go through the transformation. The structure follows a narrative arc that mirrors the prospect's own journey, creating identification and desire.

## Core Methodology
The Case Study Story Framework follows a five-beat narrative arc designed to move a prospect from curiosity to conviction:

1. **Context** — Establish the world before the change. Who is the customer? What was their situation? What were the stakes? Context creates identification — the reader must see themselves in the customer's position.

2. **Friction/Challenge** — Name the specific problem, obstacle, or pain point. Friction creates tension. Without tension, there is no story. The challenge should be one the target audience recognizes from their own experience.

3. **Action Taken** — Describe the decision to change and the implementation. This is not a feature list; it is a narrative of choices made, obstacles overcome during implementation, and the human experience of adopting something new.

4. **Result Achieved** — Quantify and qualify the outcome. Numbers matter, but so does the emotional shift. What changed in the customer's daily experience? What became possible that was not possible before?

5. **Proof/Evidence** — Layer in supporting evidence: direct quotes, screenshots, data visualizations, third-party validation. Proof transforms a claim into a fact.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Select the Right Customer Story
- Prioritize customers whose situation mirrors your ideal prospect profile
- Look for stories with clear before/after contrast and measurable outcomes
- Choose customers who are articulate and willing to be named publicly
- Avoid customers whose results are anomalous or unrepeatable
- Ensure the story showcases the specific use case you want to promote

### Step 2: Conduct the Story Interview
- Use the Testimonial Interview Framework for detailed guidance on interview technique
- Focus 60% of interview time on the "before" state — this is where identification lives
- Ask for specific moments, not generalizations: "Tell me about the day you realized the old system was broken"
- Probe for emotional language: frustration, fear, relief, pride, surprise
- Capture exact quotes — paraphrased testimonials lose their power
- Record the interview (with permission) to capture tone and emphasis

### Step 3: Map the Narrative Arc
- From interview notes, identify the single most compelling tension point
- Build the story around that tension — everything else supports it
- Timeline: Context (20% of word count), Friction (25%), Action (20%), Result (25%), Proof (10%)
- Identify the "turning point" — the specific moment when things changed
- Find the emotional peak — usually the moment of first result or unexpected benefit

### Step 4: Write the Draft
- Open with a scene, not a summary: "It was 11 PM on a Tuesday when Maria realized she had spent the entire day on reports that nobody would read."
- Use the customer's actual language wherever possible
- Write in third person but maintain intimacy through specific details
- Include dialogue from the interview as pull quotes
- End with the customer's own words about the transformation
- Keep total length between 800-1500 words for written case studies

### Step 5: Layer in Proof Elements
- Embed quantitative results at the moment of maximum narrative impact
- Use callout boxes for key metrics so scanners get the headlines
- Include before/after comparisons with specific numbers
- Add timeline markers showing how quickly results appeared
- Reference any third-party tools or audits that validated results

### Step 6: Get Approval and Distribute
- Share the draft with the customer for factual accuracy and comfort level
- Offer to let them adjust quotes (but push back on corporate sanitization)
- Create derivative assets: social snippets, email excerpts, slide deck versions, video scripts
- Map distribution to the buyer's journey: awareness (social snippets), consideration (full case study), decision (detailed proof appendix)

## Examples

### Example 1: B2B SaaS — Marketing Analytics Platform
- **Context**: "Greenfield Marketing was a 15-person agency managing $2M in monthly ad spend across 23 clients. Their reporting process consumed 40% of their analysts' time — time that should have been spent on strategy."
- **Friction**: "Every Monday morning began with a scramble. Analysts pulled data from six platforms, reconciled discrepancies in spreadsheets, and assembled reports that were already outdated by the time clients received them. Three analysts had quit in the past year, citing report fatigue."
- **Action**: "Greenfield's operations lead, Tom Chen, ran a 2-week pilot with three clients. The integration took 4 hours per client. 'I expected a month of setup,' Tom said. 'We were pulling automated reports by Thursday.'"
- **Result**: "Reporting time dropped from 16 hours per client per month to 2 hours. The agency reallocated 340 analyst hours per month to strategy work. Client retention increased 18% over six months."
- **Proof**: Direct quote from Tom, before/after time audit data, client satisfaction survey results, screenshot of the dashboard.

### Example 2: DTC E-commerce — Subscription Box Brand
- **Context**: "Bloom & Gather launched in 2021 as a curated home goods subscription. By 2023, they had 8,000 subscribers but a 45% annual churn rate that was quietly destroying their unit economics."
- **Friction**: "Founder Aisha Patel described the problem bluntly: 'We were amazing at acquisition and terrible at keeping people. Every month we filled a leaky bucket. I could see the math — we had 18 months before the economics broke completely.'"
- **Action**: "Aisha implemented a personalized retention sequence using the platform's predictive churn model. The system identified at-risk subscribers 30 days before cancellation and triggered personalized outreach."
- **Result**: "Annual churn dropped from 45% to 28% in five months. Customer lifetime value increased from $180 to $267. Aisha's team estimated the intervention saved $340,000 in annual revenue."
- **Proof**: Aisha's direct quote, anonymized churn curve chart, LTV comparison table, CFO-verified revenue impact figure.

## When to Use
- When you have a customer with clear, measurable results and willingness to participate
- When sales teams need proof assets for specific objections or use cases
- When entering a new vertical and need a reference story from that industry
- When a prospect is in the consideration or decision stage of the buyer's journey
- When building a library of proof that supports the brand promise

### Case Study Pitfalls
- **The Corporate Gloss**: Letting the customer's PR team sanitize the story until all humanity is removed. Push back on corporate editing that eliminates emotional language, specific details, and honest struggle. The messy truth is more persuasive than polished fiction.
- **The Feature Tour**: Turning the case study into a product walkthrough instead of a customer story. The customer is the protagonist, not the product. The product is the tool the protagonist used to succeed.
- **Missing the "Before"**: Rushing through the context and friction to get to the result. The "before" state is where reader identification lives. Without it, the result has no contrast and no emotional weight.
- **Vague Metrics**: "The customer saw significant improvement" is not proof. "Close rate increased from 18% to 34% in 90 days" is proof. Push for specific numbers on every result claim.
- **One-Dimensional Stories**: Only telling success stories about revenue or efficiency. The most compelling case studies include unexpected benefits: team morale improved, the customer's own customers noticed the change, the customer's career advanced because of the results.

## When NOT to Use
- When the customer's results are preliminary or unverified
- When the customer is unwilling to be named or provide specific data
- When the story requires so much context about the customer's industry that the narrative loses momentum
- When you have fewer than three months of post-implementation data
- When the customer's success depended on factors unrelated to your product
- When the customer relationship is under strain and a case study request could worsen it

## Agent Routing
- **Discovery Agent**: Identifies candidate customers, conducts initial screening, schedules interviews
- **Interview Agent**: Conducts the story interview using the Testimonial Interview Framework
- **Structure Agent**: Maps the narrative arc from interview transcripts
- **Drafting Agent**: Writes the case study following the five-beat structure
- **Editing Agent**: Refines for clarity, emotional impact, and proof density
- **Distribution Agent**: Creates derivative assets and maps distribution plan
- Route to this framework when the task involves turning a specific customer's experience into a marketing asset

### Case Study Quality Checklist
- Does the opening drop the reader into a scene, not a company description?
- Is the "before" state vivid enough that the reader identifies with the customer's pain?
- Are results quantified with at least 2 specific metrics?
- Does the story include at least 3 direct customer quotes?
