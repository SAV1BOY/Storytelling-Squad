# Klaff Push/Pull Engagement Pattern
> The dynamic tension technique of alternating between drawing the audience in and pushing them away to maintain peak engagement.

## Overview

The Push/Pull pattern is Oren Klaff's engagement maintenance system — the technique that keeps an audience invested throughout the entire length of an interaction, not just the opening. While the Crocodile Brain theory explains how to capture attention and Frame Control explains how to establish power dynamics, Push/Pull explains how to sustain engagement over time.

The principle is rooted in behavioral psychology: humans habituate to constant stimuli. A pitch that is uniformly enthusiastic becomes background noise. A conversation that is relentlessly positive loses emotional texture. A marketing sequence that always "pulls" (always offering value, always building desire) creates satiation, not desire.

Push/Pull works by alternating between two modes:

**Pull**: Drawing the audience toward you by showing value, creating desire, offering inclusion, demonstrating benefit, and making the audience feel wanted.

**Push**: Creating distance by showing scarcity, introducing qualification criteria, withdrawing attention, expressing doubt about fit, or raising the cost of engagement.

This alternation creates dynamic tension — the audience is never fully comfortable and never fully rejected. They are in a state of engaged uncertainty, which is the neurological state most conducive to attention, desire, and action.

The pattern mirrors healthy human relationships: attraction requires both approach and withdrawal, interest and challenge, warmth and standards. All-pull is needy. All-push is hostile. The oscillation between the two is magnetic.

## Core Methodology

### Understanding Pull Mechanics

Pull is any action that increases the audience's desire to engage with you, your product, or your idea. Pull creates positive emotional association and forward momentum.

**Types of Pull**:

*Value Pull* — Demonstrating clear, tangible benefit: "Companies using our platform see a 40% reduction in customer churn within 90 days." The audience thinks: "I want that result."

*Vision Pull* — Painting an aspirational future: "Imagine your team spending 80% of their time on creative strategy instead of manual reporting." The audience thinks: "I want that future."

*Social Proof Pull* — Showing that desirable others have already engaged: "Three of the five companies on your slide at last quarter's board meeting are already using this." The audience thinks: "I should be doing what they're doing."

*Emotional Pull* — Creating personal connection through vulnerability or shared experience: "We built this because we lived the exact pain you're describing — three years of managing a 200-person team with spreadsheets." The audience thinks: "They understand me."

*Inclusion Pull* — Making the audience feel part of something: "What we're building is going to change how this entire industry works, and the early partners will shape it." The audience thinks: "I want to be part of this."

### Understanding Push Mechanics

Push is any action that creates distance, introduces doubt, or raises the barrier to engagement. Push creates scarcity and communicates standards.

**Types of Push**:

*Scarcity Push* — Limiting availability: "We're only onboarding three new enterprise clients this quarter because integration quality matters more to us than revenue speed." The audience thinks: "I might not get in."

*Qualification Push* — Setting criteria: "This isn't a fit for every company. We work best with teams that have at least $10M in revenue and a dedicated operations lead." The audience thinks: "Do I qualify?"

*Doubt Push* — Expressing uncertainty about fit: "I'm not sure this is the right timing for you. Your infrastructure might need another six months before you'd see the full benefit." The audience thinks: "Wait, I want to convince you it IS the right time."

*Withdrawal Push* — Pulling back attention or energy: After making a compelling point, lean back physically, slow your speech, reduce eye contact slightly. The audience feels the warmth recede and leans in to recapture it.

*Standards Push* — Demonstrating that you have criteria: "We turned down a major contract last quarter because the client wanted to cut corners on implementation. That's not how we work." The audience thinks: "Their standards are high — their product must be worth it."

### The Push/Pull Rhythm

The key to effective Push/Pull is rhythm. The pattern should feel natural, not mechanical. General principles:

**Start with Pull**: Open with value and vision. The audience needs a reason to engage before you can meaningfully push them away.

**Pull longer than you Push**: A 3:1 or 4:1 ratio of pull-to-push usually feels right. Too much push and you seem hostile or uninterested. The push should be a punctuation mark, not the main sentence.

**Push at peak interest**: Deploy push when the audience is most engaged, not when they're drifting. A push during high engagement amplifies desire. A push during low engagement signals disinterest on both sides.

**Escalate gradually**: Early pushes should be mild (small qualification criteria). Later pushes can be stronger (expressing genuine doubt about fit). This mirrors the escalation of commitment — the more the audience has invested, the more they'll tolerate being pushed.

**Always end with Pull**: The final beat of any interaction should be pull — a positive vision, a clear next step, an invitation. You push to create desire, but you close by fulfilling it (conditionally).

### Push/Pull in Written vs. Live Contexts

In live interactions (pitches, sales calls, presentations), Push/Pull operates through behavioral signals: body language, vocal tone, attention allocation, and pacing.

In written contexts (email sequences, landing pages, content marketing), Push/Pull operates through structural and linguistic choices:
- Email 1 (Pull): Value-dense, generous, inviting
- Email 2 (Pull): Social proof, success stories, inclusion
- Email 3 (Push): Scarcity signal, qualification criteria, "this might not be for you"
- Email 4 (Pull): New value, deeper insight, renewed invitation
- Email 5 (Push/Pull blend): Limited-time opportunity with clear criteria for who should apply

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Map Your Current Engagement Pattern
Audit your existing pitch, email sequence, or sales conversation. Plot each moment as either "pull" or "push." Most people discover their pattern is all-pull with no push — constant value-giving, constant enthusiasm, no tension, no scarcity, no standards.

### Step 2: Identify Natural Push Points
Find the moments in your narrative where push would feel organic:
- After demonstrating strong results (push: "Of course, these results require a specific type of partner")
- After sharing your vision (push: "We're being very selective about who we bring into this early")
- After the audience shows strong interest (push: withdraw slightly, create space)
- After answering a question well (push: redirect with your own qualifying question)

### Step 3: Craft Specific Push Language
Write out 3-5 push statements appropriate for your context:
- "I want to be transparent — we're not the right fit for everyone, and I'd rather we figure that out now than three months in"
- "The companies that get the most value from this share three characteristics: [X, Y, Z]. Does that describe your team?"
- "We have more demand than capacity right now, so I want to make sure this is a mutual priority"
- "I should mention — our onboarding process is intensive. Teams that aren't ready to commit fully tend to underperform"

### Step 4: Sequence Pull and Push Deliberately
Restructure your communication to alternate:
1. **Pull** — Open with compelling value or insight (capture attention)
2. **Pull** — Deepen with story or evidence (build desire)
3. **Push** — Introduce qualification or scarcity (create tension)
4. **Pull** — Resolve tension with renewed vision (reward engagement)
5. **Push** — Stronger qualification or doubt (test commitment)
6. **Pull** — Close with clear invitation and next step (channel desire into action)

### Step 5: Read and Respond to Audience Signals
In live contexts, Push/Pull must be responsive, not scripted:
- If the audience is leaning in eagerly → push (they can handle it, and it will deepen desire)
- If the audience is pulling back → pull (re-engage before pushing further)
- If the audience is asking qualifying questions about themselves → your push is working; maintain with light pull
- If the audience seems confused → you've pushed too hard or too early; pull back to value

### Step 6: Calibrate Intensity by Context
High-stakes, competitive contexts (investor pitches, enterprise sales) support stronger pushes. Relationship-building contexts (first conversations, community engagement) require gentler pushes. Match the intensity to the audience's investment level and the competitive landscape.

## Examples

### Example 1: SaaS Sales Conversation
A sales rep for an enterprise analytics platform redesigned their demo flow using Push/Pull:

**Pull**: "Let me show you what one of our clients — similar size, similar industry — achieved in their first quarter. They reduced report generation from 6 hours to 15 minutes." (Value + social proof)

**Pull**: "We built this specifically for teams like yours that are drowning in data but starving for insight. The integration with your existing stack takes about a week." (Inclusion + ease)

**Push**: "I should mention — we require a dedicated internal champion for implementation. Without that, the results I just showed you won't materialize. Is that something you can commit to?" (Qualification + doubt)

**Pull**: "When you do have that champion in place, the compound effect is remarkable. Month one is learning. Month two is optimization. Month three is when your CFO starts asking 'How did we ever operate without this?'" (Vision + escalation)

**Push**: "We're at capacity for Q2 implementations. If you want to go live before summer, we'd need to start the scoping process this week. Otherwise, the next slot opens in September." (Scarcity + time frame)

**Pull (close)**: "I think this could be a really strong fit. Let me send you the scoping questionnaire today, and if the answers look right on both sides, we'll lock in your Q2 slot." (Invitation + mutual qualification)

The push moments transformed the conversation from a demo into a mutual evaluation, increasing close rates by 25% and reducing sales cycle length by two weeks.

### Example 2: Product Launch Email Sequence
A D2C wellness brand redesigned their launch sequence for a new product line:

**Email 1 (Pull)**: "We've been working on something for 18 months. Here's the first look." Generous preview, founder story, science behind the formula. Open rate: 42%.

**Email 2 (Pull)**: Customer beta-tester results. Before/after stories. Emotional testimonials. Open rate: 38%.

**Email 3 (Push)**: "This isn't for everyone." Detailed explanation of who the product is designed for — and explicitly who it's NOT for. "If you're looking for a quick fix, this isn't it. This is a 90-day commitment." Open rate: 44% (higher than Pull emails — the push created curiosity).

**Email 4 (Pull)**: Free educational content about the science. No selling. Pure value. "Whether you buy or not, here's what you should know about [topic]." Open rate: 36%.

**Email 5 (Push/Pull)**: "Launch day. 500 units. No restock for 8 weeks." Clear scarcity plus clear value. Waitlist for overflow. Sold out in 4 hours.

The Push emails (3 and 5) generated higher engagement than the Pull emails — the counter-intuitive tension of being told "this might not be for you" created more desire than any amount of value-adding could achieve alone.

## When to Use

- Sales conversations of any kind (SaaS, services, enterprise, D2C)
- Pitch delivery — investor, partnership, or internal
- Email marketing sequences (nurture, launch, re-engagement)
- Content marketing strategy (alternating value-giving with exclusivity)
- Brand positioning where premium or selective positioning is desired
- Social media engagement strategy (alternating generous content with exclusive access)
- Any ongoing relationship where engagement needs to be maintained over time

## When NOT to Use

- Crisis communications (push during crisis reads as callous or dismissive)
- Customer support interactions (the customer needs resolution, not tension)
- Purely informational communications (documentation, tutorials, FAQs)
- Contexts where the power dynamic is already heavily in your favor (pushing someone who's already supplicating is cruel, not strategic)
- Very early relationship stages where no trust has been established (push requires existing engagement to work)
- Cultural contexts where the withdrawal element of push is interpreted as rejection or insult

## Integration

- **With Klaff's STRONG Method**: Push/Pull operates primarily within the "O" (Offering the prize) and "N" (Nailing the hookpoint) phases. The prize frame IS a push. The hookpoint requires pull to land.
- **With Klaff's Frame Control**: The prize frame is maintained through Push/Pull. Pure pull surrenders the prize frame. Strategic push preserves it.
- **With Klaff's Crocodile Brain**: Push creates the novelty and contrast the croc brain needs. Constant pull becomes familiar (croc brain filters it out). The push is what keeps the message novel.
- **With Johnstone's Status Transactions**: Pull is a low-to-medium status behavior (offering, including). Push is a high-status behavior (qualifying, withdrawing). Push/Pull is a status transaction pattern.
- **With Ganz's Story of Now**: The "Now" framework is a pull-push-pull structure: pull (shared values), push (the threat to those values), pull (the action that resolves the threat).

## Agent Routing

Route to the Klaff Push/Pull framework when:
- The user describes engagement that starts strong but fades over time
- Sales conversations are "going well" but not closing
- Email sequences have strong opens but declining engagement across the series
- The user's approach is described as "too nice," "too eager," or "too salesy" (both extremes indicate a Push/Pull imbalance)
- The request involves creating dynamic tension in a sales or marketing sequence
- Content or brand positioning feels "desperate" or "needy"
- The user asks about scarcity, urgency, or maintaining audience interest

Do NOT route here when:
- The need is for initial attention capture (use Crocodile Brain)
- The request is for pitch structure (use STRONG method)
- The focus is on movement building or community (use Ganz)
- The context is customer retention where push would damage relationships
- The deliverable is a single piece of content rather than a sequence or conversation
