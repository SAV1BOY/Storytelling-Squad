# Campbell's Archetypes
> Universal character patterns drawn from collective mythology that give brand personas and narrative roles psychological depth and instant recognition.

## Overview

Joseph Campbell, building on Carl Jung's theory of the collective unconscious, identified recurring character archetypes that appear across every culture's mythology. These archetypes are not stereotypes—they are deep psychological patterns that audiences recognize instinctively. In marketing, archetypes provide a shortcut to emotional resonance: when a brand consistently embodies an archetype, audiences feel a sense of familiarity and trust without needing extensive explanation.

The twelve primary archetypes (later codified by Margaret Mark and Carol Pearson in *The Hero and the Outlaw*) each carry distinct motivations, fears, and communication styles. Campbell's original work focused on narrative roles—the characters that populate any story. This framework covers both: the identity archetypes (who the brand is) and the narrative role archetypes (who appears in the brand's stories).

## Core Methodology

Archetypes work because they tap into shared human psychology. The methodology has three layers:

1. **Brand Identity Archetype** — Select the single primary archetype that defines the brand's personality, voice, and values. This becomes the consistent through-line across all communications. A secondary archetype can add nuance.
2. **Narrative Role Archetypes** — When crafting stories, assign archetypal roles to the characters: the customer, the brand, the competitor, the problem. Campbell's key narrative roles include the Hero, Mentor, Herald, Threshold Guardian, Shapeshifter, Shadow, Trickster, and Allies.
3. **Archetype Consistency Audit** — Periodically review all brand touchpoints to ensure the chosen archetype is expressed consistently. Mixed archetypes create confusion and erode trust.

The twelve identity archetypes organized by core motivation:

**Ego/Mastery:** Hero (strength through courage), Magician (transformation through vision), Outlaw/Rebel (liberation through disruption)
**Order/Structure:** Caregiver (service through compassion), Ruler (control through leadership), Creator (innovation through imagination)
**Social/Belonging:** Everyperson (connection through authenticity), Lover (intimacy through passion), Jester (joy through playfulness)
**Freedom/Fulfillment:** Innocent (safety through optimism), Sage (wisdom through understanding), Explorer (freedom through discovery)

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify Core Motivation
Ask: What fundamental human desire does this brand serve? Map it to one of four quadrants (Ego, Order, Social, Freedom). This narrows candidates to three archetypes.

### Step 2: Assess Brand Personality
For each candidate archetype, evaluate fit across five dimensions: tone of voice, visual style, value proposition, customer relationship model, and competitive positioning. Score each 1-5. The highest-scoring archetype becomes primary.

### Step 3: Define the Archetype's Expression
Document specific guidelines: vocabulary choices, imagery rules, emotional register, storytelling themes, and taboo behaviors (things the archetype would never do). For example, the Sage archetype uses precise language, avoids hyperbole, and never dumbs things down.

### Step 4: Assign Narrative Roles
For each story the brand tells, map characters to Campbell's narrative roles:
- **Hero** — The customer (almost always). They drive the action.
- **Mentor** — The brand. Provides tools, wisdom, or confidence. Never solves the problem directly.
- **Herald** — The trigger event or message that initiates the customer's journey. Often a piece of content, an ad, or a referral.
- **Threshold Guardian** — Objections, fears, or competitors that block the hero's progress. The brand helps the hero overcome these.
- **Shadow** — The antagonist. In marketing, this is the problem, the status quo, or the competitor philosophy the brand opposes.
- **Shapeshifter** — Ambiguous figures whose loyalty is uncertain. In marketing, these might be industry analysts, reviews, or word-of-mouth that could go either way.
- **Trickster** — Figures that inject humor, chaos, or unexpected perspective. Useful for brands with a Jester archetype.
- **Allies** — Supporting characters: other customers, community members, partner brands.

### Step 5: Stress-Test with Counter-Archetype
Ask: What would the opposite archetype say or do? If the brand is the Sage, imagine it as the Jester. This contrast sharpens the archetype's definition and reveals potential blind spots.

### Step 6: Apply Across Touchpoints
Map the archetype's expression to every customer touchpoint: website copy, email tone, social media voice, customer support scripts, packaging, event design. Consistency compounds recognition.

## Examples

### Example 1: Outdoor Gear Brand as Explorer Archetype
**Brand Identity:** Explorer — motivated by freedom, discovery, and self-sufficiency.
**Voice:** Direct, adventurous, slightly rugged. Avoids corporate jargon. Uses second person ("you") and imperative verbs ("go," "discover," "push").
**Narrative Roles in Campaign:**
- Hero: A weekend hiker who wants to attempt their first multi-day trek
- Mentor: The brand's gear and trail guides
- Shadow: The sedentary routine, the fear of being unprepared
- Threshold Guardian: Weather uncertainty, gear confusion
- Herald: A friend's Instagram post from a stunning summit
- Allies: The online community forum where experienced hikers share routes

**Campaign Tagline:** "The trail doesn't care about your excuses. Neither do we."

### Example 2: Financial Planning App as Sage Archetype
**Brand Identity:** Sage — motivated by understanding, truth, and wisdom.
**Voice:** Calm, authoritative, precise. Uses data and evidence. Never sensationalizes. Explains complexity without condescension.
**Narrative Roles in Campaign:**
- Hero: A young professional confused by contradictory financial advice
- Mentor: The app's AI advisor and educational content
- Shadow: Financial anxiety, predatory products, misinformation
- Threshold Guardian: The complexity of investment options, jargon overload
- Herald: A jarring credit card statement that triggers a wake-up call
- Allies: The app's community of users sharing transparent portfolio updates

**Campaign Tagline:** "Clarity is the first return on your investment."

### Example 3: Snack Brand as Jester Archetype
**Brand Identity:** Jester — motivated by joy, play, and living in the moment.
**Voice:** Irreverent, witty, self-aware. Breaks the fourth wall. Uses pop culture references freely.
**Narrative Role in Social Campaign:**
- Hero: A bored office worker trapped in a meeting
- Trickster (brand as ally-trickster): The snack that becomes a moment of rebellion
- Shadow: The monotony of corporate routine
**Campaign Tagline:** "Meetings end. Flavor doesn't."

## When to Use

- Brand identity development and positioning exercises
- Voice and tone guideline creation
- Campaign character development when multiple figures appear in the narrative
- Competitive differentiation (claim an underoccupied archetype in your category)
- Internal culture alignment (archetype gives employees a shared language for decision-making)
- Influencer or partnership selection (choose partners who share or complement the archetype)

## When NOT to Use

- When the brand has not yet defined its core value proposition (archetype is expression, not strategy)
- For short-form tactical content where character depth is irrelevant
- When the audience expects pure utility (API documentation, technical specs)
- If the team cannot commit to archetype consistency—inconsistent use is worse than none
- When dealing with sensitive or crisis communications where persona-play feels inauthentic

## Integration

Campbell's Archetypes integrate with:
- **Campbell's Hero's Journey** — Archetypes populate the journey's roles; the two frameworks are designed to work together
- **Miller's StoryBrand SB7** — StoryBrand's "Guide" role maps to the Mentor archetype; archetypes add depth to the Guide's personality
- **Klaff's Frame Control** — The archetype chosen determines how the brand holds frame in pitch situations
- **Ganz's Public Narrative** — Archetype consistency strengthens the "Story of Self" by giving the narrator a clear identity
- **Hall's Story Selection Matrix** — Archetype guides which stories to collect and tell (choose stories that reinforce the archetype)

## Agent Routing

Route to Campbell's Archetypes when:
- The user asks about brand personality, brand voice, or character development
- The brief requires defining who the brand "is" at a psychological level
- The user mentions archetypes, personas, or Jungian character types
- A campaign requires multiple characters with distinct roles
- The user needs competitive positioning based on personality differentiation

Do NOT route here when:
- The user needs narrative structure (route to Hero's Journey or Story Circle)
- The focus is on plot mechanics rather than character identity (route to Snyder or Coyne)
- The user needs presentation structure rather than character work (route to Duarte)
- The task is logline or pitch creation (route to Snyder Logline or Klaff)
