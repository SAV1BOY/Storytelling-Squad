# Harmon Story Circle
> An 8-step circular narrative framework that maps the universal pattern of human transformation, distilled from Joseph Campbell's monomyth into a practical storytelling engine.

## Overview

Dan Harmon developed the Story Circle while writing for the TV series Community and later Rick and Morty. His insight was that Joseph Campbell's 17-stage Hero's Journey, while powerful, was too granular for episodic storytelling. Harmon compressed it into 8 steps arranged in a circle, divided by two axes: the horizontal axis separates order (top) from chaos (bottom), and the vertical axis separates the conscious self (left) from the unconscious or unknown (right).

The genius of the circle is its symmetry. Step 1 mirrors Step 5. Step 2 mirrors Step 6. Step 3 mirrors Step 7. Step 4 mirrors Step 8. Every descent into the unknown has a corresponding ascent back to the known — but the character is never the same on the return trip. This makes the Story Circle not just a plotting tool but a psychological map of transformation.

For marketing, the Story Circle solves a persistent problem: how do you make a brand story feel complete and satisfying in any format, from a 60-second ad to a 45-minute webinar? The answer is that completeness comes from structural wholeness, not length. A story that touches all 8 points in a single paragraph will feel more satisfying than a rambling 20-page case study that skips steps.

## Core Methodology

The 8 steps of the Story Circle are arranged clockwise:

**1. YOU — A character in a zone of comfort.**
Establish the protagonist in their ordinary world. The audience must understand who this person is and what "normal" looks like for them before anything disrupts it. In marketing, this is the customer's current state — their daily reality, their routines, their existing worldview. Specificity matters here. "A marketing manager" is weak. "A marketing manager at a mid-size SaaS company who spends 3 hours every Monday manually pulling reports from 6 different dashboards" is strong.

**2. NEED — But they want something.**
Desire enters the picture. Something is missing, incomplete, or frustrating about the status quo. This is not yet an external event — it is an internal recognition that things could be better. In marketing, this maps to the customer's latent pain or aspiration. The need must feel authentic, not manufactured. The audience should nod and think "yes, that is exactly what I want."

**3. GO — They enter an unfamiliar situation.**
The protagonist crosses a threshold into unknown territory. They leave the safety of the known world and enter a space where the old rules do not apply. In marketing, this is the moment the customer decides to try something new — visits your site, signs up for a trial, attends your webinar, reads your whitepaper. The key insight: this step requires courage. Acknowledge that the customer is taking a risk.

**4. SEARCH — They adapt to it.**
The protagonist fumbles, experiments, learns the rules of the new world. This is the messy middle — the part most marketers skip because it is uncomfortable. But this step is essential because it builds credibility. In marketing, show the customer's learning curve. Show the implementation challenges. Show the moments of doubt. Audiences trust stories that include struggle.

**5. FIND — They get what they wanted.**
The protagonist achieves their goal. They find the treasure, win the prize, get the answer. In marketing, this is the moment of product-market fit — the customer discovers your solution works. But Harmon warns that "find" is not the end. It is only the midpoint. This is where most marketing case studies stop, and it is exactly where they should keep going.

**6. TAKE — They pay a heavy price for it.**
Getting what you want always costs something. The protagonist must sacrifice, compromise, or face an unexpected consequence. In marketing, this is the most counterintuitive step: you must show what the customer gave up or struggled with even after finding the solution. Maybe it was a painful migration. Maybe it meant changing team workflows. Maybe it required executive buy-in that was hard to get. This step is what separates propaganda from storytelling.

**7. RETURN — They go back to where they started.**
The protagonist returns to the ordinary world, but everything looks different now. The geography is the same; the perception has changed. In marketing, the customer is back in their daily work — same office, same team, same Monday meetings — but their experience has been fundamentally altered. Show the contrast between the old Monday and the new Monday.

**8. CHANGE — Having changed.**
The protagonist is transformed. They are not the person they were in Step 1. The circle is complete, but it is not a loop — it is a spiral. The character has moved to a new level. In marketing, this is the customer's new identity. They are no longer "a marketing manager drowning in manual reports." They are "a data-driven marketing leader who makes decisions in real time." The transformation must be earned by everything that came before.

## Detailed Steps

### Mapping the Circle to Marketing Formats

**For a social media post (micro-circle):**
Compress all 8 steps into 3-5 sentences. You can imply steps rather than stating them explicitly. The minimum viable circle touches steps 1, 3, 5, and 8: who they were, what they tried, what they found, who they became.

**For a case study (full circle):**
Dedicate a section to each step. The most common mistake is rushing through steps 4 and 6 (Search and Take). These are the steps that build trust and differentiation. Any competitor can claim steps 5 and 8 (Find and Change). Only a truthful storyteller will dwell in the struggle.

**For a webinar or long-form presentation (expanded circle):**
Each step becomes a section of the presentation. Steps 2 and 3 (Need and Go) should take up roughly 30% of the total time — this is where you build tension and empathy. Steps 4 through 6 (Search, Find, Take) are the meaty middle at 40%. Steps 7 and 8 (Return, Change) are the payoff at 20%. Step 1 (You) is the opening 10%.

**For an email sequence (distributed circle):**
Each email covers 1-2 steps. An 8-email sequence maps perfectly to the circle. A 4-email sequence pairs steps: Email 1 covers You/Need, Email 2 covers Go/Search, Email 3 covers Find/Take, Email 4 covers Return/Change. The key is that each email must end with enough tension to pull the reader into the next step.

### The Two Axes

The horizontal axis (Order vs. Chaos) divides the circle into top and bottom halves:
- Top half (steps 1, 2, 7, 8): the known world, conscious decisions, comfort
- Bottom half (steps 3, 4, 5, 6): the unknown world, unconscious drives, discomfort

The vertical axis (Self vs. Other) divides the circle into left and right halves:
- Left half (steps 1, 8, 7, 6): the protagonist's internal world, identity, selfhood
- Right half (steps 2, 3, 4, 5): the external world, goals, objects of desire

This means each quadrant has a distinct emotional quality:
- Top-left (steps 1, 8): Identity — who you are
- Top-right (steps 2, 3): Desire — what you want
- Bottom-right (steps 4, 5): Achievement — what you get
- Bottom-left (steps 6, 7): Cost — what you pay

## Examples

### Example 1: B2B SaaS Case Study for a Project Management Tool

1. **YOU:** Priya runs a 12-person design agency. Projects are tracked in spreadsheets, Slack threads, and sticky notes on monitors. She knows where everything is because she holds it all in her head.
2. **NEED:** The agency wins a contract twice the size of anything they have handled before. Priya realizes her mental project map will not scale. She needs a system that works without her as the single point of failure.
3. **GO:** She signs up for three project management trials simultaneously and commits to running the new client project on one of them. Her team resists — they like the informality of Slack and sticky notes.
4. **SEARCH:** Two weeks of parallel testing. One tool is too rigid. Another is too complex. The third feels right but requires the team to change how they report progress. Priya holds a team meeting where the pushback is honest and uncomfortable.
5. **FIND:** By week four, the third tool clicks. The team can see the entire project timeline. Dependencies are visible. Priya stops being the bottleneck.
6. **TAKE:** But the transition costs three days of billable work. One designer nearly quits over the new check-in requirement. Priya has to have a difficult conversation about accountability.
7. **RETURN:** The big project ships on time. Priya walks into Monday's standup meeting — same room, same team — but now the project status is on the screen, not in her head. She does not have to narrate the state of the world.
8. **CHANGE:** Priya is no longer the agency's memory. She is its strategist. The agency takes on two more large contracts that quarter because the system — not Priya — holds the complexity.

### Example 2: Consumer Brand Story for a Running Shoe Campaign

1. **YOU:** Marcus runs three times a week. Same route, same pace, same shoes he has worn for two years. Running is his therapy, his constant.
2. **NEED:** He signs up for his first marathon on impulse after a friend's Instagram post. Suddenly three-mile jogs are not enough. He needs to train for 26.2.
3. **GO:** His first long run — 10 miles — destroys his feet. His old shoes cannot handle the distance. He walks into a running store for the first time in years and feels overwhelmed by options and jargon.
4. **SEARCH:** He tries two pairs on a treadmill in the store. A fitting specialist watches his gait and says something about pronation he has never heard before. He learns his old shoes were wrong for his foot strike.
5. **FIND:** The new shoes feel different immediately — not better in a flashy way, but more stable, more forgiving. His first long run in them is still hard, but his feet are not the problem.
6. **TAKE:** The shoes cost three times what he usually pays. His partner raises an eyebrow. He has to commit to the marathon now because the shoes are a promise he made to himself.
7. **RETURN:** Race day. Mile 20. Same legs, same lungs, same Marcus — but the shoes are doing their job and his feet are still with him when everything else wants to quit.
8. **CHANGE:** He crosses the finish line. He is no longer "a guy who jogs." He is a marathon runner. The shoes did not make him faster. They made the transformation possible by removing the barrier that would have stopped him at mile 10.

## When to Use

- When you need a complete narrative arc in any format, from a tweet to a keynote
- When your brand story feels flat or one-dimensional — the circle reveals which steps you are skipping
- When you need to audit an existing piece of content for structural completeness
- When writing case studies, customer stories, or testimonials that need to feel emotionally satisfying
- When planning a multi-part content series (email sequence, video series, podcast arc) and need each part to feel purposeful
- When the audience needs to identify with a transformation, not just a transaction

## When NOT to Use

- When the goal is purely informational (a product spec sheet, a feature comparison, a pricing page). Not every piece of content needs a narrative arc.
- When the customer's journey is genuinely simple and adding struggle would feel manufactured. Forcing a "heavy price" (step 6) onto a frictionless experience is dishonest.
- When time constraints demand a single persuasive point, not a journey. A billboard needs a slogan, not a story circle.
- When the story is about the brand, not the customer. The Story Circle works best when the protagonist is the audience. If the protagonist is your company, you risk sounding self-congratulatory.
- When you are writing for an audience that is already deep in the decision process. A prospect comparing two finalists does not need a transformation narrative — they need proof points.

## Integration

The Story Circle pairs naturally with several other frameworks:
- **Coyne's Five Commandments:** Each step of the circle can be analyzed for whether it contains an inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, and resolution at the micro level.
- **Harmon's But/Therefore:** Use the But/Therefore test between each step of the circle to ensure causal connections. Step 2 should connect to Step 3 with "therefore" (I needed something, therefore I went looking). Step 5 should connect to Step 6 with "but" (I found what I wanted, but it cost me).
- **Coyne's Controlling Idea:** The Story Circle generates the raw material; the Controlling Idea distills its meaning into a single sentence.
- **Coyne's Genre Clover:** The genre dimensions tell you which emotional beats to emphasize at each step of the circle.

- **Pass to Harmon But/Therefore** when the circle draft is complete and needs causal connection testing.

- **Pass to Coyne Controlling Idea** when the circle is complete and needs a thematic summary.
- **Input required:** Protagonist identity, their initial state, their desire, the product/service/idea involved, the real cost or sacrifice, and the transformation outcome.
