# Story Brief Framework
> The standard briefing document for any storytelling task—the intake form that ensures no narrative begins without strategic clarity.

## Overview

The story brief is the single most important document in the storytelling process. It is the contract between strategy and execution. Without it, writers create content that sounds good but serves no purpose. With it, every creative decision has a compass.

The concept of a creative brief has existed in advertising since the 1960s, formalized by agencies like DDB, Ogilvy, and BBDO. The story brief adapts the creative brief specifically for narrative-driven content, adding fields for character, belief change, proof, and narrative structure that a traditional creative brief omits.

In a storytelling squad, the story brief is the input layer. No narrative task should begin without a completed brief. The brief doesn't constrain creativity—it focuses it. A writer working from a clear brief produces better work faster than a writer working from vague instructions, every time.

The brief also serves as an accountability tool. When stakeholders request changes, the brief provides an objective reference: "Does this change serve the objective stated in the brief, or is it a new objective?"

## Core Methodology

1. **Define the Objective**: What must this piece of content accomplish? One primary objective only.
2. **Identify the Audience**: Who exactly is this for? Demographic, psychographic, and behavioral specifics.
3. **Articulate the Core Belief to Change**: What does the audience currently believe that this content must shift?
4. **Specify the Desired Action**: What should the audience do after consuming this content?
5. **Inventory Available Proof**: What evidence exists to support the narrative?
6. **Set Constraints**: Word count, format, channel, brand guidelines, legal requirements, timeline.
7. **Define Tone**: How should this content feel? Emotional register and voice characteristics.
8. **Identify the Channel**: Where will this content live? Channel shapes structure.
9. **Draft the Logline**: Compress the brief into a one-sentence story summary.
10. **Get Approval Before Execution**: The brief must be approved by the stakeholder before writing begins.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Define the Objective
- Substep: State one primary objective. "This case study will convince mid-market SaaS CTOs that our platform reduces engineering onboarding time by 60%."
- Substep: If you cannot state the objective in one sentence, the task is not yet clear enough.
- Substep: The objective should be measurable or at least observable. "Build brand awareness" is vague. "Position the brand as the thought leader on developer experience for mid-market SaaS" is more actionable.
- Substep: Distinguish between business objectives (generate leads, drive sales) and communication objectives (change a belief, create awareness). The brief should state both, but the communication objective drives the narrative.
- Substep: Reject briefs with multiple competing objectives. If the stakeholder insists on three objectives, negotiate one primary and acknowledge the others as secondary.

### Step 2: Identify the Audience
- Substep: Go beyond demographics. "CTO, 35-50, Series B-C companies" is a start. Add psychographics: "Technically brilliant but increasingly frustrated by management demands. Reads Hacker News. Values craft but is being pushed toward business metrics. Secretly worries they're becoming a bureaucrat."
- Substep: Identify where the audience is in their awareness journey: Unaware, Problem-Aware, Solution-Aware, Product-Aware, or Most Aware (Eugene Schwartz's levels).
- Substep: Name one to three specific audience members if possible. "This should work for someone like [actual customer name] at [company]." Real people prevent generic writing.
- Substep: Identify what the audience is consuming before and after this content. What are they reading, watching, and thinking about in the context where they'll encounter this?

### Step 3: Articulate the Core Belief to Change
- Substep: State the audience's current belief: "Mid-market CTOs currently believe that onboarding speed is primarily a documentation problem."
- Substep: State the desired belief: "We want them to believe that onboarding speed is a tooling and environment problem that documentation alone cannot solve."
- Substep: The gap between current and desired belief is the narrative's job. Every element of the story should serve this shift.
- Substep: The belief change should be one step, not a leap. If the audience currently believes X and you need them to believe Z, this piece should move them to Y. The next piece moves them to Z.
- Substep: This field maps directly to the Lie in the Character Want-Need-Lie framework.

### Step 4: Specify the Desired Action
- Substep: What concrete action should the audience take after consuming this content?
- Substep: Actions range from micro (click a link, share the content, reply to the email) to macro (book a demo, make a purchase, change a behavior).
- Substep: The desired action should be proportional to the content's investment. A 500-word blog post shouldn't ask for a $50,000 purchase decision. A 60-minute webinar can ask for more.
- Substep: If there is no desired action, question whether the content should exist.

### Step 5: Inventory Available Proof
- Substep: List all proof assets available: customer testimonials, case study data, third-party research, product demonstrations, before/after metrics, expert endorsements, user counts, awards.
- Substep: Rate each proof asset's strength: Strong (specific, verifiable, attributed), Medium (directional, partially attributed), Weak (anecdotal, unattributed).
- Substep: Identify proof gaps. If the narrative requires proof that doesn't exist, flag it now—before the writer discovers the gap mid-draft.
- Substep: Indicate which proof can be shown (demonstrated, visualized) vs. which must be told (cited, quoted).

### Step 6: Set Constraints
- Substep: Word count or time length (with acceptable range, e.g., 1,500-2,000 words).
- Substep: Format (blog post, email, video script, landing page, slide deck, social post).
- Substep: Brand guidelines (voice, visual style, approved terminology, restricted terminology).
- Substep: Legal and compliance requirements (disclaimers, approval processes, restricted claims).
- Substep: Timeline (draft due date, review cycles, publish date).
- Substep: Budget (if relevant—e.g., video production budget affects script ambition).

### Step 7: Define Tone
- Substep: Use a tone spectrum, not a single word. "Confident but not arrogant. Technical but accessible. Urgent but not panicked."
- Substep: Provide tone references—"Like a Stripe blog post, not like a HubSpot blog post." Real examples prevent misinterpretation.
- Substep: Identify tone elements to avoid: "Not snarky. Not condescending. Not overly casual."
- Substep: If the brand has a voice guide, reference the specific section that applies.

### Step 8: Identify the Channel
- Substep: Name the primary channel: blog, email, YouTube, LinkedIn, podcast, landing page, stage.
- Substep: Note channel-specific requirements: email has subject line and preview text needs. YouTube has thumbnail and title needs. LinkedIn has character limits and formatting constraints.
- Substep: Identify if the content will be repurposed across channels. If so, note the primary channel (optimize for this) and secondary channels (adapt for these).
- Substep: Consider the consumption context: Is the audience on mobile or desktop? In a professional or personal context? Actively seeking or passively scrolling?

### Step 9: Draft the Logline
- Substep: Using the Logline Formula framework, compress the brief into one sentence.
- Substep: The logline serves as a gut-check: does the brief describe a compelling story? If the logline feels flat, the brief needs revision.
- Substep: Include the logline at the top of the brief for easy reference.

### Step 10: Get Stakeholder Approval
- Substep: Send the brief to the decision-maker BEFORE writing begins.
- Substep: Ask for explicit sign-off on: objective, audience, belief change, desired action, and tone.
- Substep: Document approval with a timestamp. This prevents scope creep and "I never agreed to that" conversations.
- Substep: If the stakeholder changes the brief after approval, document the change and its impact on timeline and quality.

## Examples

### Example 1: Case Study Brief
- **Objective**: Convince Series B CTOs that our platform reduces engineering onboarding from 14 days to 3 days.
- **Audience**: CTO or VP Engineering, Series B-C SaaS, 50-200 employees, currently using ad hoc onboarding.
- **Belief to Change**: From "Onboarding speed is a documentation problem" to "Onboarding speed is an environment-provisioning problem."
- **Desired Action**: Book a 30-minute demo.
- **Proof Available**: DataFlow case study (14→3 days, verified), CTO quote (approved), team satisfaction survey data.
- **Constraints**: 1,200-1,800 words, blog format, brand voice guide v3.2, legal review required for data claims.
- **Tone**: Technical and credible. Peer-to-peer, not vendor-to-buyer.
- **Channel**: Blog (primary), LinkedIn excerpt (secondary), email summary (tertiary).
- **Logline**: "A fast-growing SaaS CTO discovers that the onboarding crisis threatening her team's productivity wasn't a people problem—it was an infrastructure problem no one was talking about."

### Example 2: Brand Video Brief
- **Objective**: Position the brand as the champion of independent e-commerce sellers against platform consolidation.
- **Audience**: Independent e-commerce operators, $500K-$5M annual revenue, feeling squeezed by Amazon and Shopify fee increases.
- **Belief to Change**: From "I have no choice but to accept platform terms" to "There's a growing movement of sellers building independent infrastructure."
- **Desired Action**: Join the email list for the "Independent Seller" content series.
- **Proof Available**: 3 seller interviews (filmed), revenue data from 12 sellers who transitioned, industry analyst quote.
- **Constraints**: 3-4 minutes, produced video, $15K production budget, no competitor name mentions.
- **Tone**: Defiant but optimistic. David vs. Goliath energy without bitterness.
- **Channel**: YouTube (primary), Instagram Reels cut (secondary), homepage embed (tertiary).
- **Logline**: "Three independent sellers who were told the platform economy would crush them reveal how they built something the platforms can't replicate—and why thousands more are following."

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: The Brief as Bureaucracy
If the brief takes longer to complete than the content takes to create, the process is broken. Briefs should be proportional: a social media post needs a 5-minute mental brief, a blog post needs a one-page written brief, a brand campaign needs a comprehensive document. Scale the brief to the stakes.

### Pitfall 2: Multiple Objectives
Stakeholders often want a single piece of content to do five things: build awareness, generate leads, establish thought leadership, support SEO, and announce a product feature. This guarantees mediocrity across all five. The brief must have one primary objective. Push back diplomatically but firmly.

### Pitfall 3: Skipping the Belief Change
The "core belief to change" field is the most frequently skipped and the most important. Without it, the writer does not know what the content is arguing. Content without a belief-change target informs without persuading. Every marketing narrative should shift a belief, even slightly.

### Pitfall 4: Vague Audience Definition
"Business owners" is not an audience. "Series A SaaS founders who have just hired their first dedicated marketer and are overwhelmed by the number of channels they could be investing in" is an audience. The more specific the audience definition, the more specific (and effective) the content will be.

### Pitfall 5: No Approval Before Execution
Writers who skip brief approval to save time often lose far more time in revision cycles. A brief approved by the stakeholder creates a shared contract. Changes after approval are scope changes, not creative feedback. This distinction protects everyone.

## When to Use

- Before every narrative content task, without exception.
- When a stakeholder requests content—the brief replaces vague instructions with structured clarity.
- When multiple writers or teams are working on related content—briefs ensure consistency.
- When revising content that isn't working—write the brief retroactively to diagnose what's missing.

## When NOT to Use

- For purely reactive content (real-time social media responses, crisis communications) where speed overrides process. Even then, a mental brief helps.
- When the brief becomes bureaucratic theater—if the brief takes longer than the content, simplify the brief.

## Integration

- **Logline Formula**: The brief generates the logline. The logline validates the brief.
- **Character Want-Need-Lie**: The audience section and belief-change section of the brief map to Want, Need, and Lie.
- **Theme-Premise-Controlling Idea**: The objective and belief-change fields should align with the brand's Controlling Idea.
- **Proof in Narrative Framework**: The proof inventory field feeds directly into the Proof in Narrative framework.
- **All Structural Frameworks**: The brief's format and channel fields determine which structural framework to apply (Three-Act, Five-Act, etc.).

## Agent Routing

- **Primary Owner**: Project Manager Agent — creates, distributes, and manages approval of story briefs.
- **Assisting Agents**:
  - Brand Strategist Agent — validates alignment between the brief and brand strategy.
  - Research Agent — fills the proof inventory and audience insight sections.
  - Story Architect Agent — reviews the brief for narrative viability and selects the structural framework.
  - All Agents — receive the approved brief as the authoritative input for their tasks.
