# Harmon But/Therefore Principle
> The acid test for causal storytelling: every beat connects to the next through conflict ("but") or consequence ("therefore"), never random sequence ("and then").

## Overview

The But/Therefore Principle originated with Trey Parker and Matt Stone (creators of South Park), who explained in a 2011 NYU masterclass that the words connecting any two story beats should be "but" or "therefore," never "and then." Dan Harmon adopted this principle as a core diagnostic tool in his writing process, applying it rigorously across Community, Rick and Morty, and his broader story theory.

The principle exposes a fundamental truth about narrative: stories are chains of cause and effect, not lists of events. When you connect beats with "and then," you are writing a sequence. When you connect them with "but" or "therefore," you are writing a story. Sequences are boring because they could be rearranged without consequence. Stories are compelling because each beat is inevitable given what came before.

"But" introduces a complication — something that blocks, contradicts, or reverses the trajectory. "Therefore" introduces a consequence — something that logically follows from the previous beat. Together, they create the rhythm of tension and release that keeps audiences engaged.

For marketing, this principle is devastatingly practical. It provides a one-word test you can apply to every transition in any piece of content. Email to email in a sequence. Paragraph to paragraph in a case study. Slide to slide in a pitch deck. Scene to scene in a video. If the connection is "and then," the content is failing. If it is "but" or "therefore," the content is working.

## Core Methodology

### The Three Connectors

**"AND THEN" (the failure state):**
This happens, and then this happens, and then this happens. Each event exists independently. Remove any event and the story does not change. This is the hallmark of lazy writing and lazy marketing. A product page that says "We do X. And we also do Y. And we also do Z" is an "and then" list, not a story. A case study that says "The client tried our tool. Then they used it for a month. Then they got results" is a timeline, not a narrative.

How to detect it: If you can rearrange the beats in any order without losing meaning, you have an "and then" problem.

**"BUT" (the complication):**
This happens, but then this complication arises. "But" reverses direction. It introduces obstacles, contradictions, unexpected consequences, and irony. "But" is the engine of conflict. In marketing, "but" is the most powerful word you can use because it signals honesty. "Our product is fast — but it requires a learning curve." "The client saw immediate results — but scaling to their enterprise team revealed new challenges." Every "but" earns trust because it shows you are not hiding the friction.

How to detect it: "But" should create a moment where the audience thinks "oh no" or "wait, what?" It changes the emotional direction.

**"THEREFORE" (the consequence):**
This happens, therefore this follows. "Therefore" connects cause to effect. It shows that the previous beat had weight and consequence. "The client implemented the new workflow, therefore their team reclaimed 15 hours per week." "The campaign tested poorly with the initial audience, therefore the team pivoted to a different segment." Every "therefore" reinforces the logic of the narrative. It tells the audience that events matter and choices have consequences.

How to detect it: "Therefore" should feel inevitable in hindsight. Given what happened before, this outcome makes sense.

### The Rhythm

Strong stories alternate between "but" and "therefore" in a rhythm that builds momentum:

- The team launched the campaign (THEREFORE) it gained early traction (BUT) engagement dropped after week two (THEREFORE) they analyzed the data and found the audience was wrong (THEREFORE) they pivoted to a new segment (BUT) the new segment had different expectations (THEREFORE) they rewrote the messaging (THEREFORE) conversion doubled (BUT) the client's fulfillment team could not keep up (THEREFORE) they had to scale operations before scaling the campaign further.

Notice how each "but" raises the stakes and each "therefore" advances the action. The rhythm is not mechanical — you do not need perfect alternation — but the overall pattern should weave complication and consequence throughout.

### The Diagnosis Process

To apply the But/Therefore test to any existing piece of content:

1. **Break the content into beats.** A beat is a single event, claim, or scene. In an email, each paragraph might be a beat. In a video, each scene or talking point. In a slide deck, each slide.

2. **Write the connector between each pair of beats.** Literally write "and then," "but," or "therefore" between them. Be honest. If the connection is not clearly "but" or "therefore," it is "and then" by default.

3. **Count the connectors.** A strong narrative should have zero or near-zero "and then" connections. If more than 20% of your connections are "and then," the content needs restructuring.

4. **Fix the "and then" connections.** For each "and then," ask: What complication could make this beat connect to the next with "but"? What consequence could make this beat connect to the next with "therefore"? Sometimes the fix is reordering. Sometimes it is adding a missing complication. Sometimes it is cutting a beat that does not serve the chain.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Extract the Beat Sheet

Take your content — whatever format it is in — and reduce each section to a single sentence that captures the core event or claim. Strip away decoration, examples, and supporting detail. You want the skeleton.

Example from a SaaS case study:
- Beat 1: The company was growing but their CRM could not handle the volume.
- Beat 2: They evaluated five CRM platforms over three months.
- Beat 3: They chose Platform X because of its API flexibility.
- Beat 4: Implementation took six weeks with dedicated support.
- Beat 5: The sales team saw a 40% increase in pipeline visibility.
- Beat 6: Revenue grew 25% in the following quarter.

### Step 2: Apply the Test

Now connect each pair:
- Beat 1 to Beat 2: "The CRM could not handle the volume, THEREFORE they evaluated five platforms." This works.
- Beat 2 to Beat 3: "They evaluated five platforms AND THEN chose Platform X." This fails. Why did they choose X? What made the decision difficult? Where is the conflict?
- Beat 3 to Beat 4: "They chose Platform X AND THEN implemented it." This fails. Implementation is just the next chronological event. No complication, no consequence.
- Beat 4 to Beat 5: "Implementation took six weeks AND THEN the sales team saw results." Fails again. There is no causal link shown between the implementation process and the results.
- Beat 5 to Beat 6: "Pipeline visibility increased, THEREFORE revenue grew." This works, but it is too easy. Where is the "but"?

Score: 2 out of 5 connections work. This case study is a timeline, not a story.

### Step 3: Rewrite the Connections

- Beat 1 to Beat 2: "The CRM could not handle the volume, THEREFORE they evaluated five platforms." (Keep)
- Beat 2 to Beat 3: "They evaluated five platforms, BUT three failed their API integration test. THEREFORE they narrowed to two finalists — and chose Platform X because it was the only one that could handle their custom objects." (Fixed: added complication and consequence)
- Beat 3 to Beat 4: "They chose Platform X, BUT the implementation revealed that their legacy data was messier than anyone expected. THEREFORE the six-week timeline stretched to eight, and the support team had to build a custom migration script." (Fixed: added complication and real consequence)
- Beat 4 to Beat 5: "The migration was painful, BUT once the clean data was in the new system, the sales team could see their entire pipeline for the first time. THEREFORE they identified three stalled deals that had been invisible in the old system." (Fixed: the pain of migration directly caused the clarity of results)
- Beat 5 to Beat 6: "Pipeline visibility led to recovered deals, THEREFORE revenue grew 25%. BUT the real change was not the number — it was that the sales team stopped blaming marketing for lead quality because they could finally see the full picture." (Fixed: added a deeper consequence beyond the metric)

### Step 4: Rebuild the Content

The rewritten beat sheet becomes the structural backbone of the revised content. Each "but" and "therefore" is a hinge point that the prose must honor. The decoration, examples, and supporting detail can be layered back in, but the skeleton must remain causal.

## Examples

### Example 1: Email Sequence for a Course Launch

**Before (And Then sequence):**
- Email 1: We are launching a new course on content strategy.
- Email 2: Here is what the course covers.
- Email 3: Here is a testimonial from a past student.
- Email 4: Enrollment closes Friday.

**After (But/Therefore rewrite):**
- Email 1: Most content strategies fail because they optimize for volume instead of resonance. THEREFORE we built a course that teaches you to find your one essential message before you write a single word.
- Email 2: BUT finding your essential message is harder than it sounds. In the first module, students confront the gap between what they think they are saying and what the audience actually hears. THEREFORE we included a live audit exercise where you test your message on real people before week two.
- Email 3: Sarah Kim took the course last spring. She had 12,000 email subscribers BUT her open rates were falling every month. THEREFORE she applied the essential message framework and rewrote her welcome sequence. Her open rates did not just recover — they hit the highest point in her list's history. BUT she will tell you the hardest part was not the rewrite; it was admitting her old message was about herself, not her reader.
- Email 4: Enrollment closes Friday. We keep cohorts small THEREFORE every student gets direct feedback on their essential message. BUT that means we turn people away every round. If this resonates, now is the time.

### Example 2: Product Demo Script for a Data Analytics Platform

**Before (And Then sequence):**
"Our platform connects to your data sources. It creates dashboards automatically. You can share dashboards with your team. We also have AI-powered insights. And we integrate with Slack."

**After (But/Therefore rewrite):**
"Your team collects data from 15 different sources. THEREFORE you spend hours building dashboards manually every week. BUT even after all that work, the dashboards are static — they are already outdated by the time your team sees them. THEREFORE we built a platform that connects to all your sources in real time. BUT real-time data is overwhelming without context. THEREFORE the platform uses AI to surface only the anomalies and trends that actually require a decision. You see what matters, not everything. THEREFORE when you share a dashboard with your team in Slack, it is not a report — it is a recommendation."

## When to Use

- When auditing any existing content for narrative strength — the But/Therefore test is the fastest diagnostic available
- When an email sequence feels flat or gets declining open rates across the series
- When a case study reads like a timeline instead of a story
- When a pitch deck puts the audience to sleep during the middle slides
- When a video script feels like a list of features instead of a journey
- When planning content structure before writing begins — map the But/Therefore chain first, then write
- When training junior writers or content creators who struggle with narrative momentum
- When you suspect a piece of content is "correct but boring" — this framework reveals exactly why

## When NOT to Use

- When the content is genuinely a list and the audience expects a list. A feature comparison chart does not need narrative causality. A pricing page does not need "but" and "therefore." Respect the format.
- When the story is so simple that forcing complications would feel artificial. If the customer's experience was genuinely smooth, do not invent friction just to create a "but." Audiences can smell manufactured drama.
- When you are writing technical documentation, API references, or instructional content where clarity and sequence matter more than narrative tension. "Step 1, then Step 2, then Step 3" is correct for a tutorial.
- When the audience is already bought in and just needs logistical information. A post-purchase onboarding email does not need a story arc — it needs clear instructions.
- When overuse has made the rhythm predictable. If every piece of content follows an aggressive But/Therefore pattern, the audience starts to feel manipulated. Vary the intensity.

## Integration

- **Harmon Story Circle:** Apply But/Therefore between each of the 8 steps of the Story Circle. Step 2 (Need) to Step 3 (Go) should connect with "therefore" — the need drives the action. Step 5 (Find) to Step 6 (Take) should connect with "but" — finding the prize comes with a cost.
- **Coyne Five Commandments:** The Inciting Incident is a "but" that disrupts the status quo. Progressive Complications are a chain of "but" and "therefore." The Crisis is a "but" that forces a choice. The Climax is a "therefore" — the choice leads to action. The Resolution is a "therefore" — the action leads to a new state.
- **Coyne Controlling Idea:** The But/Therefore chain, when fully resolved, should point toward the controlling idea. The final "therefore" in the chain is the thematic conclusion.
- **Harmon Embryo Theory:** The But/Therefore chain is the circulatory system of the story organism. It is how narrative blood flows from beat to beat. If the chain is broken, the organism dies.
