# Coyne Five Commandments of Storytelling
> Every scene, every marketing unit, every content block must contain five essential elements: Inciting Incident, Progressive Complications, Crisis, Climax, and Resolution.

## Overview

Shawn Coyne's Five Commandments are the irreducible structural elements of any unit of story. They operate fractally — present at the level of a single scene, present at the level of an act, present at the level of the global story. No matter how large or small the narrative unit, if it is missing any of the five commandments, it will feel incomplete, arbitrary, or flat.

The power of the Five Commandments lies in their universality and their precision. Unlike vague advice like "create tension" or "tell a compelling story," the Five Commandments give you a concrete checklist. You can point to a specific paragraph in a case study and ask: where is the crisis? You can look at a sales page section and ask: what is the inciting incident? If the answer is "there is not one," you have found the structural problem.

For marketing, the Five Commandments are the single most practical storytelling tool available. They work at every scale — a single email, a landing page section, a video scene, a podcast segment, a slide in a deck. They transform the question "is this good?" into the question "is this complete?" Completeness is diagnosable. Goodness is subjective.

## Core Methodology

### The Five Commandments

**1. INCITING INCIDENT — Something happens that upsets the balance.**

Every story unit begins in a state of relative balance — not necessarily a good state, but a stable one. The inciting incident disrupts that stability. It is the event that makes the rest of the scene necessary.

There are two types of inciting incidents:
- **Causal:** An action taken by a character that disrupts the balance. "The CEO decided to cut the marketing budget by 40%."
- **Coincidental:** An external event that disrupts the balance without anyone choosing it. "A new competitor launched with a product identical to ours at half the price."

Causal incidents feel more powerful because they imply agency and responsibility. Coincidental incidents feel more dramatic because they imply vulnerability and unpredictability. The best marketing narratives use both types at different scales.

The inciting incident must be specific and concrete. "Things were not going well" is not an inciting incident. "On March 15th, the largest client — representing 30% of revenue — sent a termination notice with 30 days' warning" is an inciting incident.

**2. PROGRESSIVE COMPLICATIONS — Things get worse or more complex.**

After the inciting incident, the protagonist cannot simply solve the problem. Complications arise — obstacles, reversals, escalations, new information — that make the situation progressively more difficult or more complex.

Progressive complications must escalate. Each complication should be more challenging than the last. If the complications are lateral (equally difficult, just different), the audience loses the sense of momentum. The trajectory must be upward in stakes and intensity.

Types of progressive complications:
- **Obstacles:** Something blocks the path forward. "The team tried to replace the lost revenue with new clients, but the sales cycle was six months minimum."
- **Reversals:** Something that seemed like progress turns out to be a setback. "They landed a promising lead, but due diligence revealed the prospect was in financial trouble."
- **Escalations:** The original problem grows in scope. "While focused on replacing the lost client, they discovered two more clients were considering departure."
- **Revelations:** New information changes the understanding of the situation. "A departing team member revealed that the lost client had been unhappy for over a year and no one had escalated the concern."

The final progressive complication is the most important. Coyne calls this the "turning point progressive complication" — the complication that forces the crisis. It must be the most intense, the most irreversible, and the most demanding of a response.

**3. CRISIS — A dilemma with no easy answer.**

The crisis is the heart of every story unit. It is the moment of impossible choice. The protagonist must decide between two paths, and neither path is free of cost.

Coyne identifies two types of crisis:
- **Best Bad Choice:** All options are negative. The protagonist must choose the least terrible path. "Do we lay off 20% of the team to preserve cash, or do we keep everyone and risk running out of money in six months?"
- **Irreconcilable Goods:** All options have genuine value, but choosing one means sacrificing the other. "Do we double down on our existing market where we are strong, or pivot to the emerging market where the future growth is?"

The crisis MUST be a genuine dilemma. If one option is clearly better than the other, there is no crisis — just a delayed decision. The audience should be able to argue for either side. This is what creates tension: the audience does not know what the protagonist will choose because both choices have real merit and real cost.

In marketing, the crisis is the element most often missing. Case studies skip from "we had a problem" to "we found a solution" without dwelling on the moment of agonizing choice. Sales pages present the product as the obvious answer without acknowledging the legitimate reasons a prospect might say no. The crisis is where trust is built because it is where honesty lives.

**4. CLIMAX — The decision and the action.**

The climax is the protagonist's response to the crisis. It is not just a decision — it is a decision expressed as action. Thinking about what to do is the crisis. Doing it is the climax.

The climax must be irreversible. Once the action is taken, there is no going back. This is what gives it weight. "They decided to pivot" is not a climax until they actually pivot — cancel the old product line, reallocate the team, call the existing customers and explain the change.

The climax must also be the protagonist's choice. It cannot be an external event that solves the problem. If the crisis is resolved by coincidence, luck, or someone else's action, the story feels hollow. The protagonist must make the hard choice themselves. In marketing, this means the customer must be the hero of their own story. Your product or service can be the tool they use, but the decision and courage must be theirs.

**5. RESOLUTION — The new normal.**

The resolution shows the world after the climax. The balance has been restored, but it is a different balance than before. The resolution answers the question: what changed as a result of this choice?

The resolution must be concrete and specific. "Things got better" is not a resolution. "Within 90 days of the pivot, three of the five churned customers returned because the new product addressed their actual needs — and the team had a shared language for talking about customer problems that they had never had before" is a resolution.

The resolution should also hint at a new inciting incident — the next cycle. This is what Coyne calls the "hook for the next beat." In marketing, the resolution of a case study should naturally suggest the next phase of the customer's journey, which might involve deeper engagement with your product, a new challenge they are now equipped to face, or a transformation that opens new possibilities.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify Your Unit of Story

Define the scope of the narrative unit you are analyzing or writing. Is it a single email? A section of a sales page? An entire case study? A slide in a presentation? The Five Commandments apply at every scale, but you need to know which scale you are working at.

### Step 2: Find or Write the Inciting Incident

Ask: What event disrupts the balance? If you cannot identify it, the unit has no engine. Nothing is driving the narrative forward. Either find the inciting incident in your material or create one.

Test: Could the audience walk away after the inciting incident without needing to know what happens next? If yes, the incident is not disruptive enough.

### Step 3: Map the Progressive Complications

Ask: What makes the situation progressively more difficult or complex after the inciting incident? List every complication and check that they escalate. If they are flat (equal intensity), reorder them so they build. If there are fewer than two complications, the scene is too thin.

Test: Does each complication make the previous situation seem easier by comparison? If yes, you have proper escalation.

### Step 4: Identify or Create the Crisis

Ask: What impossible choice does the protagonist face? Is it a best bad choice or irreconcilable goods? Can you argue for both sides?

Test: If you present the crisis to a colleague and they immediately say "obviously they should do X," the crisis is not a true dilemma. Revise until reasonable people would disagree.

### Step 5: Write the Climax

Ask: What does the protagonist actually DO? Not think, not plan, not consider — do. The action must be irreversible and must flow from the crisis.

Test: Does the climax answer the crisis question with a clear, definitive action? Is it the protagonist's choice, not an external rescue?

### Step 6: Write the Resolution

Ask: What does the world look like now? What has changed? Be specific and concrete.

Test: If you covered the inciting incident and jumped straight to the resolution, would the change feel earned? If not, your middle (complications, crisis, climax) is not doing its job.

## Examples

### Example 1: Sales Page Section for a Consulting Service

**Inciting Incident:** "Last year, the average B2B company spent $47,000 on content marketing and generated a 1.2% conversion rate. That means for every 1,000 people who saw their content, 12 took action. The other 988 left without a trace." (Coincidental inciting incident — a market reality that disrupts the assumption that content marketing is working.)

**Progressive Complications:**
- "Most teams respond by producing more content. But volume does not fix a conversion problem — it amplifies it. Now you are paying more to be ignored at scale."
- "Some teams try to fix it with better targeting. But targeting without compelling narrative just means you are reaching the right people with the wrong message."
- "A few teams invest in premium content — longer, more researched, more polished. But polish without structure is a beautiful house with no doors. People admire it from outside and never enter."

**Crisis (Irreconcilable Goods):** "You face a choice: continue investing in the content machine you have built — the team, the tools, the processes, the institutional knowledge — or admit that the machine is optimized for the wrong metric and needs to be fundamentally rebuilt around narrative structure, not content volume."

**Climax:** "The companies that choose to rebuild discover something counterintuitive: they produce less content but generate more conversion. Not because less is more, but because structure is more. A single case study built on the Five Commandments outperforms ten blog posts built on keyword research."

**Resolution:** "The 1.2% conversion rate was never a content problem. It was a story problem. The 988 people who left were not uninterested — they were unengaged. They encountered information when they needed narrative. They received facts when they craved meaning."

### Example 2: Case Study Section for a Healthcare Technology Company

**Inciting Incident:** "In January, St. Mary's Regional Hospital received notification that their primary electronic health records vendor would be discontinuing support for their current system in 18 months. The system touched every department, every workflow, every patient interaction." (Causal — the vendor made a decision that disrupted the hospital's stability.)

**Progressive Complications:**
- "The IT team identified three alternative vendors, but evaluating them required clinical input from departments that were already understaffed and overworked."
- "The budget for migration was estimated at $2.3 million — $800,000 more than the hospital had allocated for all technology spending that year."
- "Midway through the evaluation, the hospital's chief medical officer — the strongest internal advocate for digital transformation — accepted a position at another institution."
- "Then the turning point: a nearby hospital that had recently migrated to one of the three candidate systems reported a critical data integrity issue that resulted in a patient safety incident. The candidate list dropped to two, and confidence in the entire process cratered."

**Crisis (Best Bad Choice):** "The hospital leadership faced an impossible choice: rush the migration to meet the 18-month deadline with an under-tested system and a demoralized team, or negotiate a costly support extension with the departing vendor to buy more evaluation time — knowing the extension would consume the budget needed for the migration itself."

**Climax:** "The CFO and interim CMO presented a third option to the board: partner with a migration specialist firm that would absorb the evaluation risk, guarantee data integrity during transition, and spread the cost across a 30-month payment schedule tied to performance milestones. The board voted 7-2 to approve. It was not the cheapest option. It was not the fastest. It was the one that addressed the actual crisis — not the technology gap, but the confidence gap."

**Resolution:** "The migration completed in 14 months, four months ahead of the vendor's end-of-support deadline. Zero patient safety incidents. But the real outcome was organizational: St. Mary's IT team, which had entered the process terrified of failure, emerged with a migration playbook they later used for three ancillary system transitions. The crisis became curriculum."

## When to Use

- When writing or auditing any individual unit of marketing content — a sales page section, an email, a video scene, a case study section, a pitch deck slide
- When content feels structurally incomplete but you cannot articulate why — the Five Commandments checklist will reveal the missing element
- When a scene or section lacks tension — check for a missing or weak crisis
- When a case study reads as a simple before/after with no middle — the progressive complications are absent
- When a sales page presents the product as the obvious choice with no real alternative — the crisis is missing
- When training writers to think in narrative structure at the scene level

## When NOT to Use

- When the content unit is genuinely informational and not narrative. A FAQ answer, a product specification, or a pricing tier description does not need the Five Commandments. Not every piece of content is a story.
- When the content is so short that the Five Commandments would feel forced. A one-line tagline or a CTA button does not have room for progressive complications. Use the Controlling Idea framework instead for ultra-compressed content.
- When you are doing macro-level analysis of a whole narrative. The Five Commandments are a scene-level tool. For whole-story analysis, use the Story Grid.
- When the audience is post-decision and needs reassurance, not narrative tension. A post-purchase confirmation email should reduce anxiety, not create it.

## Integration

- **Coyne Story Grid:** The Five Commandments operate at the scene level within the Story Grid's spreadsheet. Each row in the scene spreadsheet should contain all five commandments. If the polarity shift in the spreadsheet feels arbitrary, check whether the scene has a genuine crisis and climax driving the shift.
- **Coyne Genre Clover:** The genre determines what type of crises are appropriate. An action/conquest genre requires crises about physical or competitive survival. A worldview/education genre requires crises about what to believe. The genre clover tells the Five Commandments what flavor to take.
- **Coyne Controlling Idea:** The climax of the global story should directly express the controlling idea. The climaxes of individual scenes should collectively point toward the global controlling idea like compass needles pointing north.
- **Harmon Story Circle:** The Five Commandments provide internal structure for each step of the Story Circle. Step 3 (Go) is an inciting incident. Steps 4-5 (Search, Find) contain progressive complications. Step 6 (Take) is the crisis and climax. Steps 7-8 (Return, Change) are the resolution.
- **Harmon But/Therefore:** Each "but" in the But/Therefore chain is likely a progressive complication. The crisis is the "but" that cannot be bypassed. The climax is the "therefore" that follows the crisis. The resolution is the final "therefore."

## Agent Routing

- **Use this framework** when the agent detects: scene-level writing or analysis requests, "this section feels flat" diagnostics, sales page construction, email writing, case study section development, or any task requiring micro-structural narrative construction.
- **Pass to Coyne Story Grid** when the Five Commandments analysis reveals that the problem is not at the scene level but at the global structure level — the scenes are individually complete but the overall arc is broken.
- **Pass to Coyne Genre Clover** when the crisis type feels wrong for the story — this usually means the genre has been misidentified, and the genre clover can clarify what type of crisis the audience expects.
- **Pass to Harmon But/Therefore** when progressive complications do not escalate properly — the But/Therefore test can reveal whether the complications are causally connected or randomly sequenced.
- **Input required:** The content unit to be analyzed or the brief for a new content unit, including the protagonist, the context, and the intended outcome.
- **Output:** A content unit with all five commandments clearly present, or a diagnostic report identifying which commandments are missing and how to add them.
