# The Duarte Sparkline
> A presentation structure that alternates between "what is" (current reality) and "what could be" (future possibility), creating a persuasive wave pattern that builds toward a transformed "new bliss."

## Overview

The Sparkline is Nancy Duarte's signature contribution to presentation design, introduced in her book *Resonate*. It was discovered through rigorous analysis of the greatest presentations in history — from Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" to Steve Jobs's iPhone launch — and it reveals a universal structural pattern that makes presentations irresistibly persuasive.

The core insight is this: **great presentations do not follow a linear path from problem to solution. They oscillate.** They swing the audience back and forth between the discomfort of "what is" (the current, flawed reality) and the appeal of "what could be" (the possible, better future). Each swing creates contrast, and contrast is the engine of persuasion.

The visual representation of this pattern looks like a sparkline — a simple line graph that alternates above and below a center axis. The "what is" moments pull the line down (into the current reality). The "what could be" moments push the line up (into the future possibility). The presentation opens in "what is," builds through several oscillations of increasing intensity, and culminates in the "new bliss" — a permanent elevation to "what could be" that becomes the audience's new reality.

This is fundamentally different from a traditional presentation, which typically follows a flat, linear structure: "Here's the problem. Here are three solutions. Here's a recommendation. Questions?" That structure transfers information but does not transform belief. The Sparkline does not merely inform — it creates a gap between reality and possibility that the audience feels compelled to close.

## Core Methodology

### The Anatomy of a Sparkline

```
        "What Could Be"
         /\        /\        /\
        /  \      /  \      /  \     ★ NEW BLISS
       /    \    /    \    /    \   /
------/------\--/------\--/------\-/--------
     /        \/        \/
    /     "What Is"
   /
START
(in "What Is")
```

**The structure follows this arc:**

1. **Opening in "What Is"** — Begin by describing the current reality that the audience lives in. Establish common ground. Make them nod in recognition.

2. **First swing to "What Could Be"** — Introduce the possibility of a better future. Create the gap. The audience should feel the contrast between where they are and where they could be.

3. **Return to "What Is"** — Bring them back to reality. This is not a regression — it deepens the understanding of why the current state is inadequate. It might introduce new evidence, a story, or a detail that makes the status quo more painful.

4. **Second swing to "What Could Be"** — Raise the stakes. Show more of the future. Make it more vivid, more specific, more desirable. The audience leans further forward.

5. **Continue oscillating** — Each cycle increases in emotional intensity. The "what is" moments become more urgent. The "what could be" moments become more inspiring.

6. **The Call to Adventure** — Near the end, issue a direct call to action. Ask the audience to cross the threshold from "what is" to "what could be."

7. **The New Bliss** — Close with a vision of the transformed future, now presented not as a possibility but as an inevitability — if they act. The audience leaves in "what could be" permanently.

### The Psychology Behind the Sparkline

**Cognitive Dissonance:** When people are presented with a gap between their current state and a desirable state, they experience psychological tension. The brain is wired to resolve this tension — either by dismissing the possibility (which the oscillation prevents by reinforcing it) or by taking action to close the gap.

**Contrast Principle:** Humans perceive things not in absolutes but in contrasts. A warm room feels hotter if you just came from the cold. The "what is" moments make the "what could be" moments feel more powerful, and vice versa.

**Emotional Entrainment:** The oscillation creates a rhythm that the audience's emotions begin to follow. Like music, the pattern builds tension and release, tension and release, each cycle amplifying the emotional response.

**The Zeigarnik Effect:** Unresolved tension stays in the mind. By opening a gap between "what is" and "what could be" and not immediately resolving it, the Sparkline keeps the audience cognitively engaged throughout.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Define Your "What Is"

Describe the current reality in specific, relatable terms. This is not about statistics (yet) — it is about lived experience.

**Techniques for compelling "What Is":**
- Tell a story about a specific person experiencing the current reality
- Use sensory detail: what does the current reality look, feel, and sound like?
- Acknowledge the audience's experience: "You've all been in the meeting where..."
- Use data to validate the feeling: "You're not imagining it — 73% of teams report the same thing"

**Rules:**
- Do not be preachy. Describe the reality; do not judge it (yet).
- Do not rush through "what is" to get to "what could be." The audience needs to feel fully seen in their current reality before they will trust your vision of the future.
- Ground "what is" in the audience's direct experience, not abstract industry trends.

### Step 2: Define Your "What Could Be"

Describe the future possibility in equally specific, vivid terms.

**Techniques for compelling "What Could Be":**
- Paint a picture of a specific future scenario: "Imagine if..."
- Show a concrete example of someone who already lives in "what could be"
- Use contrast language: "Instead of [current pain], you would [future state]"
- Make it feel achievable, not fantastical — the audience should believe it is possible

**Rules:**
- "What could be" must be genuinely desirable to the audience, not just to you
- It must feel achievable — if it sounds like fantasy, the audience disconnects
- Ground it in evidence: someone has achieved this, data supports it, a prototype exists
- Connect it to the audience's deepest desires (autonomy, mastery, purpose, security)

### Step 3: Plan Your Oscillations

Map out 3-5 oscillation cycles. Each cycle should cover a different facet of the argument:

**Cycle 1: The Emotional Hook**
- "What Is": A story or scenario that emotionally grounds the problem
- "What Could Be": A story or scenario showing the emotional resolution

**Cycle 2: The Evidence**
- "What Is": Data, research, or case studies proving the problem is real and widespread
- "What Could Be": Data, research, or case studies proving the solution works

**Cycle 3: The Mechanism**
- "What Is": Why the current approach is structurally flawed
- "What Could Be": How the proposed approach solves the structural issue

**Cycle 4: The Objection**
- "What Is": Acknowledge the resistance — "You might be thinking..."
- "What Could Be": Address the objection and re-open the gap

**Cycle 5: The Vision**
- "What Is": The long-term cost of inaction
- "What Could Be": The long-term vision if the audience acts

### Step 4: Build Toward the Call to Adventure

Near the end of the presentation (roughly the final 15-20%), transition from oscillation to a direct appeal. This is the moment where you ask the audience to choose: stay in "what is" or step into "what could be."

**Techniques:**
- Name the choice explicitly: "You can continue doing [what is], or you can [action]"
- Make the action specific and immediate: "By the end of this week, you could..."
- Lower the barrier: "All it takes to start is..."
- Connect the action to the audience's identity: "You are the kind of team that..."

### Step 5: Close With the New Bliss

The final moment should not be "what could be" (which is still uncertain) but "new bliss" (which feels inevitable). The audience should leave feeling that the future you described is not just possible — it is coming, and they get to be part of it.

**Techniques:**
- Return to the opening story and complete it in the future state
- Use present tense to describe the future: "We are the generation that..."
- End with a specific, vivid image that the audience can carry with them
- Emotional resonance over information — the last thing they feel matters more than the last thing they learn

## Examples

### Example 1: Martin Luther King Jr. — "I Have a Dream"

Duarte's analysis of this speech reveals a near-perfect Sparkline:

**Opening "What Is":** King begins by describing the current reality — the "manacles of segregation," the "lonely island of poverty," the "shameful condition." He grounds the audience in the pain of the present.

**First "What Could Be":** He references the promissory note of the Constitution and Declaration of Independence — the promise of equality. This is "what could be" rooted in an existing agreement.

**Return to "What Is":** He addresses the urgency — "Now is the time." He warns against the "tranquilizing drug of gradualism." The current reality is not just painful but urgent.

**Oscillations continue:** He alternates between the injustices of "what is" ("we can never be satisfied as long as...") and the aspirations of "what could be" ("I have a dream that one day...").

**The New Bliss:** The speech culminates in the "I have a dream" sequence — a vivid, emotionally overwhelming vision of the future stated with prophetic certainty. It is no longer "what could be." It is "what will be." The audience is permanently elevated.

**Key lesson:** The speech does not present a linear argument. It is a wave of contrast — reality and dream, reality and dream — that builds to an emotional crescendo the audience cannot resist.

### Example 2: Steve Jobs — iPhone Launch (2007)

**Opening "What Is":** Jobs describes the current state of smartphones — they are not smart. They have tiny keyboards, clunky interfaces, and fixed functionality. He shows actual competitor products and the audience laughs at how bad they are.

**First "What Could Be":** "What we want to do is make a leapfrog product that is way smarter than any mobile device has ever been." He teases the possibility without revealing it.

**Return to "What Is":** He shows the competitive landscape — a 2x2 matrix of existing phones — and explains why they all fail. The gap between current reality and what is possible widens.

**"What Could Be" escalation:** He reveals the iPhone incrementally. Each feature is presented as a contrast: "This is what you have now [what is]... and this is what we built [what could be]." Touch screen vs. physical keyboard. Visual voicemail vs. call-and-listen. Pinch-to-zoom vs. tiny scroll buttons.

**The New Bliss:** Jobs uses the iPhone live on stage. The future is not hypothetical — it is in his hand. The audience experiences the "new bliss" in real time. The transformed future is not an aspiration. It exists.

**Key lesson:** Jobs does not just present features. He creates contrast between the broken present and the elegant future, over and over, building desire with each oscillation.

### Example 3: Marketing Application — Annual Strategy Presentation

A VP of Marketing presenting next year's strategy to the executive team:

**"What Is" (Opening):** "Last year, we generated 12,000 leads. Our sales team says only 15% were qualified. We spent $2M on acquisition and our CAC went up 22%. We are working harder and getting less."

**"What Could Be" (Cycle 1):** "What if we could generate fewer leads but 3x the qualified pipeline? What if our CAC dropped by 30% because we stopped chasing the wrong people?"

**"What Is" (Cycle 2):** "Here's why this is happening. Our current strategy treats all channels equally. We spread budget across 8 channels, and the data shows 80% of qualified pipeline comes from 2 of them."

**"What Could Be" (Cycle 2):** "If we concentrate 70% of budget on those 2 channels and use the rest for experimentation, our model shows qualified pipeline increases by 180%."

**"What Is" (Cycle 3):** "I know what you're thinking: 'But we've always been in those other channels.' Here's the cost of that comfort — $1.2M spent last year for 4% of pipeline."

**"What Could Be" (Cycle 3):** "That $1.2M, reallocated, funds a content engine that builds compounding organic traffic. By Q3, our cost per qualified lead drops below $50."

**Call to Adventure:** "I'm asking for your approval to reallocate, not for more budget. Same investment, radically different deployment. The decision is: do we keep doing what feels safe, or do we follow the data?"

**New Bliss:** "Twelve months from now, we walk into this room with a marketing engine that generates predictable, qualified pipeline at half the cost. Sales stops complaining about lead quality. Finance stops questioning marketing ROI. And we have a playbook that scales."

## When to Use

- **High-stakes presentations** — board meetings, investor pitches, annual strategy reviews
- **Persuasive talks** — when you need the audience to change behavior, approve a budget, or adopt a new approach
- **Keynote speeches and conference talks** — the Sparkline is the structure behind the most memorable talks in history
- **Change management communications** — helping organizations move from "what is" to "what could be" is literally the Sparkline's purpose
- **Sales presentations** — especially consultative sales where the buyer needs to see the gap between their current state and possible future
- **Fundraising pitches** — nonprofit and political contexts where emotional momentum drives action

## When NOT to Use

- **Information-transfer presentations** — if the goal is purely to share data, quarterly results, or project updates, the Sparkline adds unnecessary emotional weight (see `duarte-presentation-form.md` for the right form)
- **Technical training sessions** — the oscillation can confuse when the audience needs linear, step-by-step instruction
- **When the audience already agrees** — if there is no gap between "what is" and "what could be," the Sparkline creates false tension
- **Very short presentations (under 5 minutes)** — the oscillation needs time to build; in short formats, a single problem-solution arc works better
- **Written long-form content** — the Sparkline is designed for spoken, linear delivery; written content allows non-linear consumption and the oscillation effect is weaker
