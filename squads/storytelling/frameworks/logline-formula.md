# Logline Formula
> Who (hero) + wants what (goal) + against what (obstacle) + what's at stake (consequences)—compressing a story to one sentence.

## Overview

A logline is a one- or two-sentence summary that captures the essential dramatic engine of a story. The concept comes from Hollywood, where screenwriters must pitch entire films in a single breath. Industry lore says the term originated from the one-line descriptions logged next to each script in studio filing systems.

Blake Snyder (*Save the Cat!*, 2005) popularized the modern logline formula for screenwriters. In marketing, the logline serves a broader purpose: it is the atomic unit of story communication. Every elevator pitch, every ad headline, every subject line, every social media hook is, at its core, a logline.

The formula works because it contains the four elements that trigger narrative curiosity: a character we can root for, a goal that creates forward momentum, an obstacle that creates tension, and stakes that create urgency. Remove any one element and the logline—and the story—weakens.

For storytelling squads, the logline is both a planning tool (clarify the story before writing it) and a compression tool (distill existing stories to their essence). If you cannot write a logline for your narrative, you do not yet understand your narrative.

## Core Methodology

1. **Identify the Hero**: Who is the protagonist? Be specific about what makes them compelling or relatable.
2. **Define the Goal**: What does the hero want? The goal must be concrete, specific, and achievable (or fail-able).
3. **Name the Obstacle**: What stands in the way? The obstacle should be formidable enough to create genuine doubt.
4. **Articulate the Stakes**: What happens if the hero fails? Stakes create urgency and emotional investment.
5. **Compress into One Sentence**: Combine all four elements with economy and energy.
6. **Add an Ironic Element** (optional): The strongest loglines contain irony—an unexpected twist, contrast, or contradiction that makes the story feel unique.
7. **Test the Logline**: Does it create the desire to hear more? Does it work as a standalone hook?

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify the Hero
- Substep: Describe the hero with a compelling adjective and a noun. Not "a person" but "a burned-out ER nurse" or "an overconfident startup founder" or "a risk-averse CFO."
- Substep: The adjective should create either sympathy (we feel for them) or intrigue (we want to understand them).
- Substep: In marketing loglines, the hero is almost always the customer archetype, not the brand.
- Substep: The hero's descriptor should be relevant to the story. "A father of three" matters if the story is about work-life balance. "A Stanford graduate" matters if the story challenges credentialism.
- Substep: Avoid generic descriptors. "A struggling entrepreneur" is overused. "An entrepreneur who just lost her third business" is specific.

### Step 2: Define the Goal
- Substep: The goal must be external and concrete. "Wants to feel better" is internal and vague. "Wants to launch her product before the competitor's release date" is external and concrete.
- Substep: The goal should be achievable but difficult. Too easy = no story. Impossible = no hope.
- Substep: The goal should connect to what the audience wants for themselves. It functions as a proxy for the reader's own desires.
- Substep: Use active verbs: "must save," "struggles to build," "races to discover," "fights to keep."

### Step 3: Name the Obstacle
- Substep: The obstacle should match the hero in specificity. Not "faces challenges" but "faces a board that wants to shut down the division."
- Substep: The best obstacles are both external (a force, a person, a condition) and internal (a flaw, a fear, a false belief).
- Substep: In marketing, common obstacles include: market conditions, competitor actions, internal limitations, resource constraints, entrenched behaviors, or systemic forces.
- Substep: The obstacle should create genuine doubt about whether the hero will achieve the goal. If the outcome is obvious, the logline has no tension.

### Step 4: Articulate the Stakes
- Substep: Stakes answer "so what?" They tell the audience why the outcome matters.
- Substep: Personal stakes: "or she'll lose the business she built over 10 years."
- Substep: Relational stakes: "before her team loses faith and the best engineers leave."
- Substep: Universal stakes: "in a battle that will determine whether small publishers can survive the platform economy."
- Substep: The stakes should be proportional to the story's scope. A product launch story doesn't need "save the world" stakes. "Prove that her unconventional approach actually works" may be sufficient.

### Step 5: Compress into One Sentence
- Substep: Use the formula: "A [adjective] [hero noun] must [goal] against [obstacle], or [stakes]."
- Substep: Variations:
  - "When [inciting incident], a [hero] must [goal] before [deadline/stakes]."
  - "A [hero] who [defining characteristic] discovers [catalyst] and must [goal] despite [obstacle]."
  - "[Hero] wants [goal], but [obstacle] threatens [stakes]."
- Substep: Target 25-40 words. Under 20 feels thin. Over 50 loses the compression benefit.
- Substep: Read the logline aloud. It should feel like a single breath.

### Step 6: Add Irony (Optional but Powerful)
- Substep: Irony in a logline is an element that seems contradictory or unexpected.
- Substep: Examples of ironic loglines:
  - "A productivity guru who can't manage his own calendar must redesign a Fortune 500 company's workflow—in 30 days." (The irony: the expert has the same problem.)
  - "A data-driven marketer discovers that her most successful campaign was the one she made without any data." (The irony: the method contradicts the practitioner.)
- Substep: Irony signals originality. It tells the audience this story will surprise them.
- Substep: Not every logline needs irony. Straightforward loglines work for straightforward stories. But when irony is available, use it.

### Step 7: Test the Logline
- Substep: The "Would you want to hear more?" test. Read the logline to someone unfamiliar with the story. Do they ask what happens next?
- Substep: The "Mental movie" test. Does the logline create a vivid mental image? If it's abstract, it needs more specificity.
- Substep: The "Uniqueness" test. Could this logline describe a thousand different stories, or does it feel specific to this one?
- Substep: The "Audience" test. Would the target audience see themselves in the hero or care about the goal?

## Examples

### Example 1: SaaS Case Study Loglines
- **Basic**: "A growing e-commerce company must fix its broken inventory system before Black Friday, or lose the revenue that would fund next year's expansion."
- **With irony**: "An operations director who built her career on spreadsheets must convince her data-loving team to trust an AI-powered system she doesn't fully understand herself—with their biggest sales weekend three weeks away."
- **Compressed for ad headline**: "She had 21 days to replace the system she built—or lose Black Friday."

### Example 2: Brand Origin Story Loglines
- **Basic**: "Two teachers who couldn't find affordable science kits for their students must build a company from their garage, competing against established brands with 100x their budget."
- **With irony**: "Two teachers who flunked business class in college must build a million-dollar education company—while still grading papers on weekends."

### Example 3: Thought Leadership Loglines
- **Basic**: "A veteran CMO argues that the marketing industry's obsession with attribution is destroying the creativity that makes marketing work."
- **With irony**: "The executive who pioneered data-driven marketing now argues that data is killing marketing—and she has the data to prove it."

## When to Use

- Pitch development—loglines are the skeleton of any pitch, investor deck, or proposal.
- Ad headline and subject line creation—loglines compress to hooks.
- Content planning—writing the logline first ensures the content has a clear narrative engine before a single paragraph is drafted.
- Social media hooks—loglines adapted for platform-specific formats.
- Internal alignment—when multiple team members need to understand the story being told, the logline is the shared reference point.
- Testing story concepts—write three loglines for three different approaches and test which generates the most interest.

## When NOT to Use

- Stories that are deliberately ambiguous or experimental—loglines require clarity.
- Informational content that has no narrative element.
- When the story is already written and the logline would be a post-hoc rationalization rather than a structural tool.
- Complex, multi-thread narratives that resist single-sentence compression—use the Story Brief Framework instead.

## Integration

- **Story Brief Framework**: The logline is the compressed version of the story brief. Start with the brief, then distill to the logline.
- **Character Want-Need-Lie**: The hero and goal in the logline map to the Want. The obstacle often embodies the Lie. The stakes hint at the Need.
- **Three-Act Structure**: The logline contains the DNA of all three acts: hero in status quo (Act I), pursuing goal against obstacle (Act II), with stakes determining the resolution (Act III).
- **Theme-Premise-Controlling Idea**: The logline should implicitly carry the Controlling Idea. If the logline's resolution proves the Controlling Idea, the story is aligned.
- **Story Opening Patterns**: The logline often becomes the opening hook when adapted for format.
- **Contrast and Juxtaposition**: Ironic loglines use contrast as their primary engine.
