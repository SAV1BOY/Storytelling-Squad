# Presentation Narrative Framework
> Structure business presentations as stories using Duarte's sparkline, integrated proof, and CTA design.

## Overview
Most presentations fail because they are organized around information, not narrative. A list of bullet points does not persuade; a story does. This framework applies Nancy Duarte's sparkline methodology — alternating between "what is" and "what could be" — combined with strategic proof integration and a deliberate call-to-action design. The result is a presentation that holds attention, builds conviction, and drives action. Whether it is a boardroom pitch, a conference keynote, or an internal strategy review, this framework turns slides into a narrative experience.

## Core Methodology
The Presentation Narrative Framework is built on three interlocking systems:

1. **Duarte's Sparkline** — The emotional architecture. Presentations alternate between the current reality ("what is") and the future possibility ("what could be"), creating tension that pulls the audience forward. Each swing between these poles increases emotional investment until the final call to action, which Duarte calls "the new bliss."

2. **Proof Integration** — Evidence placed at moments of maximum narrative impact. Proof does not live in an appendix; it appears exactly when the audience's skepticism peaks, which is immediately after a "what could be" claim.

3. **CTA Design** — The call to action is not an afterthought appended to the last slide. It is the narrative destination that every preceding beat has been building toward. The audience should feel that the CTA is the only logical response to what they have just experienced.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Define the Transformation
- Identify the single transformation the presentation must achieve in the audience's thinking
- Frame it as: "The audience currently believes X. After this presentation, they will believe Y."
- This is not the topic of the presentation; it is the shift in belief or behavior
- If you cannot articulate the transformation in one sentence, the presentation is not focused enough
- Write the transformation on a card and reference it at every stage of development

### Step 2: Map the Sparkline
- **Opening "What Is"**: Begin with the audience's current reality. Describe it accurately and without judgment. The audience must feel recognized.
- **First "What Could Be"**: Introduce the possibility. Not the solution — the possibility. "Imagine if..." or "What if you could..."
- **Return to "What Is"**: Bring the audience back to reality. Show the gap between current state and the possibility. This creates productive discomfort.
- **Second "What Could Be"**: Deepen the vision. Add specificity, data, and examples to the possibility.
- **Return to "What Is"**: Show the cost of remaining in the current state. Quantify the gap.
- **Final "What Could Be" / New Bliss**: Present the call to action as the bridge from current reality to the envisioned future.
- Each "what is" should feel increasingly uncomfortable. Each "what could be" should feel increasingly achievable.

### Step 3: Place Proof at Tension Points
- After every "what could be" claim, the audience will silently ask: "Is this real?"
- Place your strongest proof immediately after the first major claim
- Types of proof for presentations: customer quotes on slides, live demonstrations, data visualizations, video testimonials, before/after comparisons
- Match proof type to audience: executives respond to metrics, practitioners respond to demonstrations, investors respond to social proof
- Never present proof without first creating the tension that makes the audience want proof

### Step 4: Design the Visual Narrative
- One idea per slide. If a slide contains two ideas, it is two slides.
- Use images and data visualizations for "what could be" moments — they activate imagination
- Use text and lists (sparingly) for "what is" moments — they ground reality
- Create visual contrast between "what is" slides (darker, constrained) and "what could be" slides (brighter, expansive)
- Eliminate slide clutter: no logos on every slide, no bullet-point walls, no reading your slides aloud
- Use progressive revelation: build complex diagrams one element at a time

### Step 5: Craft the Opening and Closing
- **Opening options**: A provocative statistic, a personal story, a customer scenario, a contrarian statement. Never open with an agenda slide or "a little about us."
- The opening must create curiosity or tension within the first 30 seconds
- **Closing**: Return to the opening image, story, or statistic and show how it has been transformed by the presentation's argument
- The closing must include a specific, actionable CTA: "Here is what I am asking you to do."
- The final slide should be the CTA, not "Thank you" or "Questions?"

### Step 6: Rehearse for Narrative Flow
- Rehearse the transitions between slides, not just the slides themselves
- Time each section: Opening (10%), Sparkline cycles (70%), CTA (20%)
- Practice the emotional modulation: energy up for "what could be," grounded for "what is"
- Identify the three slides where the audience is most likely to disengage and add interaction points
- Rehearse the CTA delivery until it feels natural, not scripted

## Examples

### Example 1: Annual Strategy Presentation to Leadership Team
- **Opening (What Is)**: "Last year, we grew revenue 12%. That sounds good until you realize our competitors grew 28%. We are working harder and falling behind. Here is why." [Slide: competitive growth comparison chart]
- **What Could Be #1**: "But there is a pattern in the data. The companies outpacing us all share one trait: they stopped selling products and started selling outcomes. Here is what that looks like." [Slide: case study of outcome-based competitor]
- **What Is #2**: "Today, 85% of our sales conversations lead with feature lists. Our win rate on those calls is 18%. When our reps accidentally lead with outcomes — and I say accidentally because we do not train for it — the win rate jumps to 34%." [Slide: internal win rate data]
- **What Could Be #2**: "If we shifted to outcome-based selling across the org, the math says we would close $4.2M in additional revenue without adding a single new lead." [Slide: projection model]
- **CTA**: "I am proposing a 90-day pilot with the East region team. Retrain 12 reps on outcome-based selling. Measure win rate, deal size, and cycle time. If the results match the model, we roll it out company-wide in Q3. I need your approval and $180K for the training program."

### Example 2: Conference Keynote on Customer Experience
- **Opening (What Is)**: "I asked 200 people to describe their last customer service interaction in one word. The top three answers were: frustrating, slow, and robotic. We have spent billions on CX technology and customers still feel this way." [Slide: word cloud]
- **What Could Be #1**: "But some companies have cracked this. I want to tell you about a hotel in Austin that has a 94% repeat booking rate. They do not have the best rooms or the best location. They have the best story." [Slide: hotel photo, metric]
- **What Is #2**: "Most CX strategies optimize for efficiency: faster responses, fewer touches, lower cost per interaction. But efficiency is not what customers remember. They remember how you made them feel." [Slide: efficiency vs. emotion matrix]
- **What Could Be #2**: "What if you designed your CX around moments that customers would tell their friends about? Not every moment — just three per journey." [Slide: moment mapping framework]
- **CTA**: "Here is the exercise I want you to do this week: map your customer journey. Identify the three moments with the highest emotional potential. Then redesign those three moments to be remarkable. Not efficient. Remarkable."

## When to Use
- Boardroom strategy presentations
- Conference keynotes and panel talks
- Internal all-hands and team meetings
- Client presentations and proposals
- Board of directors updates
- Any scenario where you have 10+ minutes and an audience that needs to be moved to action

## When NOT to Use
- Quick status updates or stand-up meetings (too much structure for a 5-minute update)
- Written documents or reports (use Content Marketing or Data Storytelling frameworks)
- One-on-one conversations (use Sales Story or Pitch Story frameworks)
- When the audience just needs information, not persuasion
- Highly technical audiences who want depth over narrative (supplement with appendix)
