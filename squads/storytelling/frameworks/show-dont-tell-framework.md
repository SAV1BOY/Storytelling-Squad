# Show Don't Tell Framework
> When to show (scenes, actions, sensory details) vs when to tell (summary, exposition)—and the hierarchy that governs the choice.

## Overview

"Show, don't tell" is the most frequently given and most frequently misunderstood writing advice in existence. Taken literally, it would mean every story is nothing but scenes—no summary, no exposition, no authorial voice. That would be exhausting and impractical. The real principle is about choosing the right delivery mode for each piece of information based on its importance to the story.

The concept traces to Anton Chekhov's often-paraphrased advice: "Don't tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass." The underlying psychology is sound: the brain processes concrete sensory information more deeply than abstract statements. Showing creates mental images; telling creates mental notes. Images are remembered; notes are forgotten.

For marketing storytelling, the Show Don't Tell framework provides a decision hierarchy: when the stakes are high, show. When the stakes are low, tell. When proof is involved, show. When context is needed, tell. The hierarchy prevents two failures: over-showing (every detail dramatized, exhausting the reader) and over-telling (every claim stated, boring the reader).

This framework is particularly critical for case studies, testimonials, brand stories, and any content where credibility depends on the audience believing the transformation is real, not just claimed.

## Core Methodology

1. **Understand the Four Modes**: Show through action, show through dialogue, tell with voice, tell with exposition.
2. **Apply the Hierarchy**: Rank each piece of information by its importance to the narrative.
3. **Assign the Mode**: High-importance information gets shown; low-importance information gets told.
4. **Convert Key Tells to Shows**: Identify the most critical "telling" passages and rewrite them as scenes or actions.
5. **Preserve Strategic Tells**: Recognize that some telling is efficient and necessary.
6. **Balance Density**: Ensure the ratio of showing to telling matches the format and pacing needs.
7. **Audit with Sensory Check**: Verify that shown passages engage at least two senses.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Understand the Four Modes

**Mode 1 — Show Through Action (Strongest)**
The information is conveyed through what characters do, not what anyone says about them.
- "She deleted the 47-tab spreadsheet and opened a single blank document." This shows decisiveness, frustration with complexity, and a commitment to simplicity—without stating any of those things.
- Action is the strongest mode because it is the most concrete and the least arguable. The audience draws their own conclusion, which feels more trustworthy than being told what to think.

**Mode 2 — Show Through Dialogue (Strong)**
The information is conveyed through what characters say to each other.
- "'I haven't slept through the night in three months,' she told her co-founder. 'And I'm starting to wonder if this is what building a company is supposed to feel like.'" This shows exhaustion, doubt, and vulnerability through the character's own words.
- Dialogue is strong because it feels immediate and authentic. The audience is overhearing, not being lectured.

**Mode 3 — Tell With Voice (Moderate)**
The narrator or author states information with personality, opinion, or emotional coloring.
- "Nobody warns you that the hardest part of scaling isn't finding customers—it's keeping the ones you have." This is telling, but the voice makes it feel like a conversation, not a textbook.
- Tell-with-voice works for transitions, thematic statements, and moments where the author's perspective adds value.

**Mode 4 — Tell With Exposition (Weakest)**
The narrator states information flatly, without scene, dialogue, or voice.
- "The company experienced rapid growth in Q3, with revenue increasing 40% over the previous quarter." This is pure exposition. It is efficient but forgettable.
- Exposition works for context that the audience needs but does not need to feel.

### Step 2: Apply the Hierarchy
- Substep: List every piece of information the content must convey.
- Substep: Rank each piece by narrative importance: Critical (the story's meaning depends on the audience understanding and feeling this) > Important (the story is better if the audience grasps this) > Contextual (the audience needs this information but does not need to feel it).
- Substep: Assign modes:
  - Critical = Show through action or dialogue.
  - Important = Show through dialogue or tell with voice.
  - Contextual = Tell with voice or exposition.

### Step 3: Convert Key Tells to Shows
- Substep: Identify the three to five most important "telling" statements in the draft.
- Substep: For each one, ask: "What scene, action, or dialogue would make the audience understand this without being told?"
- Substep: Conversion examples:
  - **Tell**: "The team was demoralized after the product launch failed."
  - **Show (action)**: "The Monday after the failed launch, three desks in the engineering pod were empty. The ones who showed up wore headphones and did not make eye contact."
  - **Tell**: "The new system was much faster than the old one."
  - **Show (dialogue)**: "'Wait—that is it? That used to take me forty-five minutes,' Sarah said, staring at the screen. 'I literally brought a book to read while the old system processed.'"
  - **Tell**: "Customer satisfaction improved significantly."
  - **Show (scene-embedded data)**: "When the quarterly CSAT email arrived, Priya forwarded it to the whole company with a single line: 'Remember when this number was 62? Look at it now.' The number was 91."

### Step 4: Preserve Strategic Tells
- Substep: Not everything should be shown. Showing is expensive in word count and attention.
- Substep: Use telling for:
  - Time transitions: "Three months later" is efficient telling. Showing three months passing would be wasteful.
  - Background context: "The SaaS market had consolidated significantly since 2020" sets the stage without dramatizing industry history.
  - Low-stakes information: Technical specifications, logistical details, and minor data points can be told efficiently.
  - Pacing relief: After intense shown scenes, a paragraph of voice-driven telling gives the reader breathing room.
- Substep: The goal is not zero telling. The goal is telling by choice, not by default.

### Step 5: Balance the Ratio
- Format-specific show/tell ratios:
  - **Video scripts**: 80% show / 20% tell. Video is a visual medium; showing is its natural strength.
  - **Case studies**: 60% show / 40% tell. Key moments shown, supporting context told.
  - **Blog posts**: 50% show / 50% tell. Balance of narrative scenes and explanatory content.
  - **Sales pages**: 55% show / 45% tell. Proof moments shown, benefits and features can be told with voice.
  - **Email copy**: 40% show / 60% tell. Space constraints favor efficient telling, but hooks and key moments should be shown.
  - **Social media**: 70% show / 30% tell. Very limited space, so every word must create a mental image.

### Step 6: Audit with Sensory Check
- Substep: For every "shown" passage, verify that it engages at least two senses: sight, sound, touch, taste, smell, or proprioception (the sense of body position).
- Substep: If a shown passage only engages one sense (usually sight), it is telling disguised as showing. Add a second sensory channel.
- Substep: "She saw the revenue number on her screen" is one sense. "She leaned forward, coffee going cold in her hand, as the revenue number loaded on her screen—$847,000, the digits so unexpected she whispered them aloud" engages sight, touch (cold coffee), and sound (whispering).

### Step 7: Apply the "Camera Test"
- Substep: For passages you intend to show, ask: "If a camera were in the room, could it capture this?"
- Substep: If yes, the passage is genuinely showing—it depicts observable reality.
- Substep: If no (because it describes thoughts, feelings, or abstract concepts), it may be telling in disguise. Convert it: show the thought through behavior, the feeling through physical response, the abstract through specific instance.

## Examples

### Example 1: Brand Origin Story

**Over-Told Version**: "We were passionate about solving this problem. We worked incredibly hard. We faced many obstacles but persevered. Eventually, we succeeded and built a great company."

**Properly Shown Version**: "At 2 AM on a Tuesday, Alex was lying on the office couch—the one they had dragged in from a Craigslist find because no one wanted to commute home just to come back in five hours. Her laptop screen cast blue light on the whiteboard behind her, still covered in diagrams from a brainstorm that had started optimistic and ended with three people arguing about whether the whole approach was wrong. She opened the customer email that had come in at midnight—the one from the teacher in Kansas who wrote, 'This is the first tool that actually works the way I think.' Alex screenshot the email and pinned it to the Slack channel called #why-we-do-this. Nobody responded for hours. But in the morning, every single person on the team had added a reaction emoji."

### Example 2: Product Benefit (Email Copy)

**Told**: "Our platform saves you hours every week on reporting."

**Shown**: "Friday afternoon. Your competitors are still wrestling with spreadsheets, trying to get the weekly report out before everyone leaves. You hit one button at 3:15. By 3:16, the report is in every stakeholder's inbox. By 3:17, you are closing your laptop."

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Showing Everything
Taking "show don't tell" as an absolute rule rather than a hierarchy. Showing everything results in bloated content where trivial details get the same immersive treatment as critical moments. Context, transitions, and background information should be told efficiently so that shown moments have room to breathe and impact.

### Pitfall 2: Telling Disguised as Showing
"She looked angry" is telling disguised as showing. The verb "looked" is a report, not a scene. "She slammed the laptop shut and left the meeting without a word" is genuine showing—the audience infers the anger from observable action. The test: could a camera capture this? If yes, it is showing. If it requires mind-reading, it is telling.

### Pitfall 3: Over-Written Showing
Showing that tries too hard—piling on sensory details until the prose becomes purple and self-conscious. "She opened her laptop, the blue glow casting ethereal shadows across her furrowed brow as her trembling fingers navigated to the dashboard that would change everything." This is not showing; it is performing. Good showing is precise and economical.

### Pitfall 4: Ignoring Format Constraints
Email copy has limited space. Trying to show everything in a 200-word email results in an email that is either too long or too cryptic. Match the show/tell ratio to the format. Emails can tell more. Long-form blog posts can show more. Video should show almost everything.

### Pitfall 5: Showing Without Purpose
A beautifully rendered scene that does not serve the narrative or the argument is a distraction, not an asset. Every shown moment should advance the story, reveal character, or deliver proof. If the scene exists only because the writer enjoyed writing it, apply the editing compression framework.

## When to Use

- Every narrative content task benefits from conscious show/tell decisions.
- Case studies and customer stories—the transformation must be shown to be believed.
- Brand stories—showing builds authenticity; telling builds suspicion.
- Ad creative—limited space makes every word's mode critical.
- Pitch decks—key slides should show (demo, visual result); supporting slides can tell.

## When NOT to Use

- Technical documentation where showing would obscure clarity.
- Data-heavy reports where the audience expects and prefers exposition.
- When the audience is in an analytical, not narrative, mindset (comparison shopping, feature evaluation).
- When word count is so constrained that showing is physically impossible (meta descriptions, button text, microcopy).

## Integration

- **Story Beats and Scenes**: High-value beats should be shown. Low-value beats can be told. The beat map determines where to invest showing effort.
- **Proof in Narrative Framework**: Proof benefits enormously from showing. Scene-embedded proof and demonstrated proof are "shown" proof types.
- **Editing Compression Framework**: When compressing, told passages are easier to cut or shorten than shown passages. Protect shown moments.
- **Emotional Arc Mapping**: Emotional peaks MUST be shown. Transitional moments can be told. The arc determines the showing priority.
- **Tension-Release Loop**: Tension moments benefit from showing (slows pace, builds immersion). Release moments can be told (faster pace, moving forward).

## Agent Routing

- **Primary Owner**: Copywriter Agent — makes show/tell decisions at the sentence and paragraph level.
- **Assisting Agents**:
  - Story Architect Agent — identifies which beats are high-value (show) vs contextual (tell).
  - Editor Agent — audits the show/tell ratio and flags telling in critical moments.
  - Emotional Intelligence Agent — ensures emotional peaks are shown, not summarized.
  - Research & Proof Agent — provides specific details that enable showing (names, numbers, sensory facts).
