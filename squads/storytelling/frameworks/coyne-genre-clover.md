# Coyne Genre Clover
> Every story operates in four genre dimensions simultaneously — External, Internal, Style, and Reality — and each dimension carries obligatory scenes and conventions that the audience demands.

## Overview

Shawn Coyne's Genre Clover is a classification system that treats genre not as a single label but as a four-dimensional coordinate. A story is never just "an action story" or "a comedy." It operates simultaneously in four independent genre dimensions, each of which makes its own demands on the narrative. Misidentifying or neglecting any dimension leads to audience dissatisfaction — a feeling that the story is "off" in a way that is hard to articulate.

The four leaves of the clover are: External Genre (what happens in the world), Internal Genre (what changes inside the protagonist), Style (the tone and approach), and Reality (the rules of the story's universe). Every story occupies one position in each dimension, and the combination creates a unique genre fingerprint.

Coyne developed this framework from his editorial observation that the most common structural failure in storytelling is not missing beats or weak characters — it is genre confusion. The writer thinks they are telling one type of story, but the audience expects another. The disconnect is subtle but fatal. The content might be well-written at the sentence level and structurally sound at the scene level, yet it fails because it violates the implicit contract between storyteller and audience about what kind of story this is.

For marketing, genre awareness is essential but almost universally ignored. Every brand narrative operates in multiple genres, and every audience brings genre expectations that must be either fulfilled or deliberately subverted. A case study that promises a conquest narrative (external action) but delivers a worldview education narrative (internal) will confuse the reader. A product launch that adopts a comedic style when the audience expects authoritative drama will undermine credibility. The Genre Clover ensures you know exactly what you are promising and what you must deliver.

## Core Methodology

### Leaf 1: External Genre

The external genre describes what happens in the story on the surface level — the visible, tangible events. Coyne categorizes external genres into several primary types:

**Action:** The protagonist faces a threat and must survive or conquer it. The core value is life/death (literally or metaphorically). In marketing, action stories are conquest narratives: a company overcomes a competitor, defeats a market challenge, or survives a crisis. Obligatory scenes include: a villain or antagonist force, escalating danger, a hero at the mercy of the antagonist, and a final confrontation.

**Thriller:** Similar to action but the emphasis is on the protagonist's ingenuity rather than physical prowess. The core value is life/damnation. In marketing, thrillers are strategic narratives: a team uncovers a hidden problem, outsmarts a complex system, or navigates political minefields. Obligatory scenes include: a ticking clock, a hidden enemy, and a moment where the protagonist must think their way out.

**Love/Loyalty:** Two entities move from separation to union (or the reverse). The core value is love/hate or loyalty/betrayal. In marketing, love stories are relationship narratives: a customer finds a brand they trust, a team bonds around a shared mission, a partner ecosystem creates mutual value. Obligatory scenes include: first meeting, a proof of commitment, a betrayal or test of loyalty, and a public declaration of commitment.

**Performance:** The protagonist must achieve mastery or excellence. The core value is accomplishment/failure. In marketing, performance stories are the most common: a team improves their metrics, a company achieves a milestone, an individual develops a skill. Obligatory scenes include: a training/practice montage, a moment of doubt, a public performance, and recognition of achievement.

**Society:** The protagonist challenges or is challenged by a social system. The core value is power/subjugation or freedom/oppression. In marketing, society stories are disruption narratives: a startup challenges industry conventions, a movement reshapes cultural norms, a company democratizes access to something previously reserved for elites. Obligatory scenes include: exposure of systemic injustice, a sacrifice for the greater good, and a shift in the social order.

**Status:** The protagonist rises or falls in social standing. The core value is success/failure or admiration/shame. In marketing, status stories are growth narratives: a founder goes from obscurity to recognition, a company climbs from startup to market leader, a team member goes from overlooked to essential. Obligatory scenes include: the initial low status, the mentor or opportunity, the temptation of shortcuts, and the moment of reckoning.

### Leaf 2: Internal Genre

The internal genre describes the inner transformation of the protagonist. It operates independently of the external genre — a story can have action on the outside and worldview change on the inside.

**Worldview:** The protagonist's understanding of how the world works changes fundamentally. Sub-types:
- **Education:** Moves from ignorance to knowledge. "We did not know what we did not know — and discovering the truth changed everything."
- **Revelation:** Moves from believing a lie to discovering a truth. "The metric we had been optimizing for was the wrong metric all along."
- **Disillusionment:** Moves from naive optimism to mature understanding. "The industry we thought was meritocratic turned out to run on relationships."
- **Maturation:** Moves from immature worldview to sophisticated worldview. "We stopped seeing our competitors as enemies and started seeing them as ecosystem partners."

**Morality:** The protagonist's ethical framework shifts. Sub-types:
- **Redemption:** Moves from selfishness to selflessness. "We stopped building features for ourselves and started building for the customer."
- **Testing:** The protagonist's morals are tested and they either hold firm or fall. "When the shortcut was available, we chose the harder path."
- **Corruption:** Moves from ethical to unethical (works for cautionary tales in marketing).

**Status (Internal):** The protagonist's self-perception changes. Not to be confused with external status — internal status is about identity, not social position. "We stopped calling ourselves a startup and started acting like the industry leader we had become."

### Leaf 3: Style

Style describes the tonal approach of the narrative:

**Drama:** Serious treatment with realistic emotional stakes. The default mode for most marketing content. Drama asks the audience to take the story seriously and invest emotionally in the outcome.

**Comedy:** Light treatment where the protagonist's flaws are exposed and corrected. Comedy in marketing is underused but powerful — it builds likability and lowers defenses. The key insight: comedy is not about jokes. It is about a protagonist who is flawed in a recognizable, relatable way and who grows past those flaws.

**Satire:** Critical treatment that exposes hypocrisy or absurdity. Satire in marketing is high-risk, high-reward. When it works (think of brands that mock their own industry's conventions), it creates fierce loyalty. When it misses, it alienates.

**Literary/Minimalist:** Restrained, understated treatment that trusts the audience to fill in emotional gaps. Useful for premium brands or sophisticated audiences that resist overt emotional manipulation.

### Leaf 4: Reality

Reality describes the rules of the story's universe:

**Realism:** The story operates under the rules of the real world. Causes have proportional effects. People behave as real people do. Most marketing content operates in realism, and it should — audiences need to believe the story could happen to them.

**Absurdism:** The story operates under exaggerated or illogical rules. Causes have disproportionate effects. Reality is bent for effect. In marketing, absurdism appears in viral content, surreal advertising, and brands that use exaggeration as a strategy (think of insurance commercials with impossible scenarios).

**Fantasy:** The story takes place in a world with different rules than our own. In marketing, fantasy appears in vision-casting: "Imagine a world where every customer interaction is personalized in real time." The rules of this imagined world must still be internally consistent.

**Factualism:** The story restricts itself to verifiable facts and documented events. In marketing, factualism is the mode of data-driven case studies, white papers, and research-based content. The constraint is the source of credibility.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify Your External Genre

Ask: What happens on the surface of this story? Is the protagonist fighting a threat (action), solving a puzzle (thriller), building a relationship (love), achieving excellence (performance), challenging a system (society), or rising in standing (status)?

Be honest. Many marketers default to "performance" when their actual story is "love/loyalty" (the customer's relationship with the brand) or "society" (the company's disruption of industry norms). Misidentification here cascades into every other structural decision.

### Step 2: Identify Your Internal Genre

Ask: What changes inside the protagonist? This is independent of the external events. A customer who switches from competitor A to your product might have an external performance story (they improved their metrics) and an internal worldview/revelation story (they discovered that the metric they had been tracking was the wrong one).

If the internal genre is unclear, the story will feel hollow. External events without internal meaning are spectacle — temporarily impressive but not memorable.

### Step 3: Identify Your Style

Ask: What is the tonal contract with the audience? Are we asking them to take this seriously (drama), to laugh and learn (comedy), to see through pretense (satire), or to appreciate subtlety (literary)?

The style must be consistent throughout the piece. A case study that opens with humor and then shifts to deadly serious drama confuses the audience. They do not know what emotional register to expect. Pick a style and honor it.

### Step 4: Identify Your Reality

Ask: What are the rules of this story's world? Is everything verifiable (factualism)? Is the world our world (realism)? Are we exaggerating for effect (absurdism)? Are we imagining a different world (fantasy)?

The reality dimension is the one most often violated unconsciously. A case study that claims to be factual (factualism) but includes unverifiable emotional claims has broken its reality contract. A product vision that claims to be realistic but describes capabilities that do not exist yet is operating in fantasy — and should own that.

### Step 5: List Obligatory Scenes and Conventions for Each Leaf

Once all four dimensions are identified, list the obligatory scenes and conventions for each. The external genre will have the most. The internal genre will have several. The style will have a few. The reality will have constraints rather than obligatory scenes.

Cross-reference the lists. Some obligatory scenes will be shared across dimensions. Some will be unique. The complete list is your structural contract with the audience — every item must be delivered.

### Step 6: Audit Your Content Against the Lists

Check your existing content (or your outline) against the obligatory scenes and conventions. Which ones are present? Which are missing? Which are present but weak? The missing and weak items are your structural priorities.

## Examples

### Example 1: B2B SaaS Case Study

**Genre Clover:**
- External: Performance (team achieves operational excellence)
- Internal: Worldview/Education (team learns a new way of working)
- Style: Drama (serious, stakes-driven)
- Reality: Factualism (verifiable data, named company, real metrics)

**Obligatory Scenes Audit:**
- Performance: Training montage? Yes, the implementation section covers this. Moment of doubt? Missing — the case study goes straight from adoption to results. Needs a section where the team questioned whether the tool was worth the disruption. Public performance? Yes, the quarterly review where the numbers were presented. Recognition? Weak — mentioned in passing. Should be expanded to show what the achievement meant to the team's reputation.
- Worldview/Education: Moment of ignorance? Present — the opening describes the team's blind spot. Encounter with truth? Present — the moment they saw the data for the first time. Integration of new knowledge? Missing — the case study does not show how the new understanding changed their daily decision-making beyond the tool itself.

**Prescription:** Add a "moment of doubt" scene between implementation and results. Expand the "recognition" beat. Add a paragraph showing how the worldview shift changed decisions beyond the specific tool.

### Example 2: Direct-to-Consumer Brand Campaign

**Genre Clover:**
- External: Love/Loyalty (customer discovers and commits to a brand)
- Internal: Status/Internal (customer's self-perception shifts from "budget shopper" to "conscious consumer")
- Style: Comedy (light, self-aware, relatable)
- Reality: Realism (everyday situations, recognizable moments)

**Obligatory Scenes Audit:**
- Love/Loyalty: First meeting? Present — the ad shows the customer discovering the brand. Proof of commitment? Missing — there is no scene where the brand or customer proves their commitment to each other beyond the purchase. Test of loyalty? Missing — no scenario where the customer is tempted by a cheaper alternative and chooses to stay. This is a major gap.
- Status/Internal: Low status starting point? Present — the customer's embarrassment about their old purchasing habits. Identity shift moment? Weak — mentioned but not dramatized. Should be a scene where the customer's self-perception visibly changes in a social context.
- Comedy conventions: Flawed protagonist? Present — the customer's relatable shopping habits. Correction of flaw? Present — the discovery of a better way. Social embarrassment? Missing — comedy often requires a moment of public awkwardness that leads to growth.

**Prescription:** Add a "temptation" scene where the customer encounters a cheaper alternative (test of loyalty). Dramatize the identity shift in a social context — perhaps friends commenting on the change. Add a comedic moment of social awkwardness that humanizes the customer.

## When to Use

- When planning a new brand narrative, campaign, or content series and you need to define the genre contract before writing
- When existing content feels "off" in a way that surface-level edits cannot fix — genre confusion is often the root cause
- When a brand is repositioning and needs to shift from one genre combination to another
- When multiple stakeholders have different visions for a piece of content — the Genre Clover provides a shared vocabulary for alignment
- When auditing competitor narratives to identify their genre positioning and find an unoccupied genre space
- When content needs to work across cultures, since genre expectations are relatively universal while cultural references are not

## When NOT to Use

- When the content is too short to operate in multiple genre dimensions. A tweet or tagline operates in one dimension at most. Use the Controlling Idea for ultra-short content.
- When the content is purely functional and non-narrative. A help article, an API reference, or a shipping notification does not have a genre. Do not force one onto it.
- When the creative team is already aligned on genre intuitively. Some teams have an instinctive genre sense, and the Clover framework would slow them down without adding value. Use it as a diagnostic when that intuition fails, not as a prerequisite for every project.
- When working in a genre-transcending format. Some experimental content deliberately blurs genre boundaries. The Clover can identify what is being blurred, which is useful, but it should not be used to force genre compliance on intentionally transgressive work.

## Integration

- **Coyne Story Grid:** The Genre Clover feeds directly into the Foolscap Global Story Grid. The external and internal genres identified in the Clover become the genre fields in the Foolscap. The obligatory scenes and conventions become the checklist items in the Foolscap. Without the Clover, the Foolscap is incomplete.
- **Coyne Five Commandments:** The genre determines what type of crises are appropriate at the scene level. An action crisis is about survival. A love crisis is about commitment. A performance crisis is about competence. The Clover tells the Five Commandments what flavor each element should take.
- **Coyne Controlling Idea:** The controlling idea must align with the primary genre. An action story's controlling idea is about life/death or victory/defeat. A love story's controlling idea is about union/separation. If the controlling idea contradicts the genre, either the idea or the genre identification is wrong.
- **Harmon Story Circle:** Different genre combinations emphasize different steps of the circle. Action genres emphasize steps 3-6 (the journey through the unknown). Love genres emphasize steps 2 and 8 (need and change). Performance genres emphasize steps 4-5 (search and find). The Clover tells you where to put the narrative weight on the circle.
- **Harmon Embryo Theory:** The genre clover influences what kind of seed will germinate into a healthy story. An action seed (a competitive threat) will not grow well if forced into a love story structure. The Clover helps ensure the seed matches the intended genre.

## Agent Routing

- **Use this framework** when the agent detects: brand positioning work, campaign planning, content strategy development, "why does this feel off" diagnostics where scene-level analysis (Five Commandments) has not found the problem, competitive narrative analysis, or cross-cultural content adaptation.
- **Pass to Coyne Story Grid** once the genre is identified and the obligatory scenes and conventions need to be tracked through a full scene-by-scene analysis.
- **Pass to Coyne Five Commandments** once the genre-specific crisis type is identified and individual scenes need to be written or analyzed with the correct crisis flavor.
- **Pass to Coyne Controlling Idea** once the genre is identified and the controlling idea needs to be formulated in alignment with the genre's core value.
- **Input required:** The brand or content to be analyzed, the target audience, and any existing positioning or brand guidelines that constrain genre choices.
- **Output:** A completed Genre Clover with all four dimensions identified, a list of obligatory scenes and conventions for each dimension, and an audit of existing content against those requirements.
