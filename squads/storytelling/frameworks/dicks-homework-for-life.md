# Dicks' Homework for Life
> A daily practice of recording one storyworthy moment per day, building an inexhaustible reservoir of authentic stories for content creation, social media, and founder documentation.

## Overview

Homework for Life is Matthew Dicks' signature daily practice and the engine that fuels his prolific storytelling output. The practice is radical in its simplicity: every night, before going to bed, reflect on the day and write down one storyworthy moment in a single sentence. Not a journal entry. Not a diary page. Not a paragraph. One sentence.

Dicks developed this practice after realizing that his 59 Moth StorySLAM victories were drawing from a finite reservoir of past experiences. He needed a system for continuously discovering new stories without relying on dramatic life events. Homework for Life is that system.

The practice works on two levels. On the surface, it produces a growing catalog of story candidates indexed by date. Below the surface, it rewires how you perceive your days. After weeks of doing Homework for Life, practitioners report that they begin noticing storyworthy moments in real time rather than only in retrospect. The practice trains the brain to see stories everywhere.

For marketing professionals, content creators, founders, and brand strategists, Homework for Life solves the most persistent content problem: "I don't know what to write about." After 90 days of practice, most people have more story material than they can use in a year.

## Core Methodology

### The Practice

**What you do:** Every evening, open a spreadsheet (or simple document) and record one storyworthy moment from the day. One sentence. Sometimes two. Never more.

**What counts as storyworthy:** Any moment that contained a change, a realization, a surprise, an emotion, or a shift in perspective. It does not need to be dramatic. Dicks emphasizes that most storyworthy moments are quiet:
- A conversation that changed how you think about something
- A moment when you noticed something you had previously ignored
- An interaction that revealed something about a relationship
- A small decision that felt significant in the moment
- A contrast between what you expected and what happened
- A feeling you could not explain
- A moment of connection or disconnection with another person

**What does NOT count:** Events without change. "I went to the grocery store" is not storyworthy. "I went to the grocery store and the cashier recognized me from high school and neither of us acknowledged it" is storyworthy because it contains a moment of unspoken recognition and a choice (to stay silent).

### The Format

Dicks recommends a spreadsheet with three columns:

| Date | Moment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-03-01 | Daughter asked me why I go to work if I don't like it. I didn't have an answer. | Career, parenting, identity |
| 2026-03-02 | Realized I've been eating lunch alone for six months and never noticed until someone asked to join me. | Isolation, habits, awareness |
| 2026-03-03 | Client said "you sound just like my old therapist" and I couldn't tell if it was a compliment. | Identity, professional role |

The Notes column is optional — some practitioners use it to tag themes, potential contexts, or connections to other moments. Keep it brief.

### Why a Spreadsheet

Dicks specifically recommends a spreadsheet over a notebook or journal for several reasons:
1. **The date column creates accountability.** Blank rows are visible. You can see your streaks and your gaps.
2. **It is searchable.** After a year, you can search for themes, people, or keywords.
3. **It prevents over-writing.** A spreadsheet cell discourages paragraphs. A notebook invites them.
4. **It is portable.** A Google Sheet or Excel file is accessible from any device.

### The Threshold Problem

The most common failure mode in Homework for Life is setting the threshold too high. People believe their days are not interesting enough to contain storyworthy moments. This belief is always wrong. It is a perception problem, not a reality problem.

Dicks' advice: lower the threshold radically. On days when nothing seems storyworthy, record the most human moment you can find. A look exchanged with a stranger. A thought you had while stopped at a red light. The feeling of putting on shoes that are slightly too tight. These moments may seem trivial, but many of Dicks' best Moth stories grew from moments exactly this small.

The threshold will naturally calibrate over time. Early entries may feel mundane. After 30 days, practitioners begin noticing richer moments. After 90 days, the practice becomes self-reinforcing: the act of recording moments trains the eye to spot them.

## Detailed Steps

### Getting Started

**Day 1-7: Build the Habit**
- Set a daily reminder (phone alarm, calendar event) for the same time each evening.
- Spend no more than 5 minutes on the practice.
- If nothing comes to mind, record the most vivid sensory memory from the day: what you saw, heard, smelled, or felt.
- Do not skip a day. A blank entry is better than a missed day.

**Day 8-30: Calibrate the Threshold**
- Review your first week's entries. Which ones produce a physical reaction when you reread them? (Stomach clench, smile, wince.) Those are your strongest candidates.
- Begin noticing moments in real time during the day. When you catch yourself thinking "that was interesting," make a mental note for the evening entry.
- Experiment with different parts of the day: morning moments, work moments, evening moments, interactions with strangers versus loved ones.

**Day 31-90: Harvest and Develop**
- Once a week, review the past 7 entries. Select the strongest one and spend 10 minutes expanding it: What was the 5-second moment? What would the opening and closing sentences be?
- Begin categorizing entries by theme: work stories, family stories, identity stories, customer stories, leadership stories.
- Notice patterns. Are certain themes recurring? Are there relationships or situations that consistently produce storyworthy moments? These patterns are your richest story veins.

**Day 91+: Systematic Content Production**
- The reservoir is now large enough to feed regular content production.
- Map entries to content needs: social media posts, newsletter stories, presentation anecdotes, case study seeds, podcast topics.
- Continue the daily practice. The reservoir should always be growing faster than you deplete it.

### Applying Homework for Life to Content Creation

**Social media content:**
Each Homework entry is a potential LinkedIn post, Twitter/X thread, or Instagram caption. The entry provides the seed; the Storyworthy methodology provides the craft. A single Homework entry can be developed into a 200-word social post in 15 minutes.

**Newsletter content:**
A weekly newsletter can be anchored by one developed Homework entry per week. Over a year, this produces 52 authentic, personal stories — a body of content that builds audience trust and differentiates from competitors who rely on industry commentary and listicles.

**Presentation anchoring:**
Before any presentation, review the Homework archive for entries that connect to the presentation's theme. A 2-minute personal story at the opening of a presentation is more engaging than any data slide. The Homework archive ensures you always have a relevant story available.

**Founder documentation:**
Founders who practice Homework for Life create an ongoing record of the company's human journey — the moments of doubt, insight, connection, and change that make the founder's story authentic. This archive becomes invaluable for investor narratives, brand storytelling, media interviews, and eventual memoir.

### Applying Homework for Life to Team Practice

Homework for Life can be adopted as a team practice for organizations that want to build a culture of storytelling.

**Weekly story share (15 minutes):**
Once per week, 2-3 team members share their strongest Homework entry from the past week. No development, no polish — just the one-sentence entry and a brief (60-second) explanation of why it felt storyworthy. This builds narrative literacy and trust across the team.

**Monthly story harvest (30 minutes):**
Once per month, the team reviews all shared entries and identifies moments that could become brand content: customer stories, team stories, values-in-action stories. These moments are added to a shared content reservoir.

**Quarterly story audit:**
Review the team's accumulated Homework entries and ask:
- What themes are emerging?
- Which stories have we told? Which are untold?
- Are there gaps in our story types? (Using Hall's 4 Story Types as a checklist)
- Which entries could be developed into major content pieces (keynotes, videos, long-form articles)?

## Examples

### Example 1: From Homework Entry to LinkedIn Post

**Homework entry (March 3):** "A junior designer pushed back on my feedback for the first time. I felt proud and threatened at the same time."

**Developed LinkedIn post:**
"Yesterday, a junior designer told me my feedback was wrong. Not 'I see it differently.' Not 'Can I share another perspective.' She said: 'That's wrong, and here's why.' She was right. I spent the rest of the afternoon thinking about the moment. I was proud of her. But I also felt something I didn't expect: threatened. Proud and threatened at the same time. That's when I realized: I had been hiring for people who would agree with me, not people who would challenge me. One sentence from a 24-year-old designer changed how I think about leadership. The best leaders don't hire agreement. They hire discomfort."

**Time to develop:** 12 minutes from Homework entry to published post.

### Example 2: From Homework Entry to Case Study Seed

**Homework entry (February 18):** "Customer called crying. Not because the product failed — because it worked. She said she hadn't been able to do this since her stroke."

**Development path:** This single entry became the seed for a 1,200-word customer case study. The team contacted the customer, conducted a 30-minute interview using the Storyworthy framework (finding the 5-second moment, establishing beginning/end opposites), and produced a narrative case study that outperformed every other piece of content that quarter in engagement and conversion.

### Example 3: From Homework Entry to Keynote Opening

**Homework entry (January 9):** "Realized I've given the same presentation 40 times and never once believed the opening line."

**Keynote opening (developed):**
"I have given this presentation forty times. And for forty presentations, I have opened with a statistic about the market. Today I'm going to tell you why I stopped believing that statistic six months ago, what I found when I went looking for the truth, and why it changes everything we thought we knew about our customers."

**Impact:** The keynote received the highest audience rating of the conference. Three attendees specifically mentioned the opening in their feedback.

## When to Use

- As an ongoing daily practice for anyone who creates content, gives presentations, or leads teams
- For founders who want to document their journey in real time rather than reconstructing it later
- For content teams that need a sustainable source of authentic story material
- For individuals building a personal brand through consistent, story-driven content
- For organizations that want to build a culture of storytelling from the inside out
- For speakers and thought leaders who need a reservoir of opening and closing stories
- As a prerequisite practice before running First/Last/Best/Worst workshops (practitioners spot better stories)

## When NOT to Use

- As a replacement for strategic narrative planning (Homework finds stories; it does not organize them strategically)
- When you need stories immediately (Homework is a long-game practice; for immediate story discovery, use First/Last/Best/Worst)
- As a journaling or diary practice (resist the urge to write more; the constraint is the point)
- For team members who are resistant to vulnerability (introduce the practice gradually, starting with observational moments rather than emotional ones)
- When the content need is for structured, strategic brand narratives rather than personal stories (use Howell's frameworks)

## Integration

- **Dicks' Storyworthy:** Homework for Life is the discovery layer of the Storyworthy methodology. Entries are raw material; Storyworthy is the craft that turns them into stories.
- **Dicks' First/Last/Best/Worst:** Homework is daily, passive discovery. First/Last/Best/Worst is concentrated, active discovery. Use both to build the deepest possible story reservoir.
- **Dicks' Beginning/End Rule:** Each Homework entry can be tested with the rule: "If this became a story, what would the opening and closing sentences be?" Entries that produce strong opposites are the best candidates for development.
- **Howell's ABT Framework:** A developed Homework entry can be compressed into an ABT for testing narrative tension before investing in full story development.
- **Hall's 4 Strategic Story Types:** Periodically categorize Homework entries by Hall's types (Value, Founder, Customer, Purpose) to ensure the reservoir covers all strategic needs.
- **Hall's Story Selection Matrix:** When a specific content need arises, use the matrix to identify which Homework entries are the best fit for the audience, objective, channel, and timing.
