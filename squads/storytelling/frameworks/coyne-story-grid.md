# Coyne Story Grid
> A spreadsheet-based analytical methodology for dissecting narrative structure scene by scene, tracking value charges, turning points, and polarity shifts to audit stories for structural integrity.

## Overview

The Story Grid was developed by Shawn Coyne, a veteran book editor with over 25 years of experience in Big Five publishing. Coyne's insight was that editors needed an objective, systematic way to diagnose why a story was not working — beyond the vague feeling that "something is off." His solution was to treat narrative analysis the way an accountant treats a balance sheet: track every transaction, categorize every entry, and let the data reveal the structural health of the whole.

The Story Grid operates at two levels. At the macro level, the Foolscap Global Story Grid captures the entire story's architecture on a single page — its genre, conventions, obligatory scenes, controlling idea, and the beginning-middle-end structure. At the micro level, the Scene-by-Scene Spreadsheet tracks every individual unit of story, recording what happens, what value changes, what type of turning point drives the change, and whether the polarity shifts from positive to negative or vice versa.

For marketing, the Story Grid solves a problem that most content teams do not even know they have: the inability to diagnose structural failure with precision. When a case study does not convert, when a sales page feels flat, when a webinar loses the audience at the midpoint, the usual response is to fiddle with surface elements — rewrite headlines, swap images, adjust CTAs. The Story Grid reveals whether the problem is structural: missing value shifts, absent turning points, monotonous polarity, or genre violations.

## Core Methodology

### The Foolscap Global Story Grid

The Foolscap is a one-page document that captures the macro architecture of any narrative. It forces you to answer the essential questions before you write a single scene:

**1. External Genre:** What type of story is this on the surface level? Action, thriller, love story, performance story, society story? In marketing, this translates to: Is this a conquest narrative (action)? A discovery narrative (performance)? A relationship narrative (love)? A transformation narrative (society)?

**2. Internal Genre:** What inner change does the protagonist undergo? Worldview (naive to sophisticated, or disillusioned to mature), morality (selfish to selfless), status (rise or fall)? In marketing: Does the customer's understanding change? Their values? Their position in their industry?

**3. Obligatory Scenes:** Every genre has scenes that the audience requires. A love story must have a "lovers meet" scene and a "proof of love" scene. An action story must have a "hero at the mercy of the villain" scene. In marketing, every brand narrative genre has its own obligatory scenes. A transformation case study must have a "moment of doubt" scene and a "proof of change" scene. A product launch must have a "problem revealed" scene and a "solution demonstrated" scene. Skip an obligatory scene and the audience feels cheated without knowing why.

**4. Conventions:** Genre conventions are the expected elements — not specific scenes but recurring patterns. A thriller convention is a "ticking clock." A love story convention is a "rival." In marketing, a case study convention is "quantifiable results." A founder story convention is "the origin moment." Conventions are not cliches — they are audience expectations that you must either fulfill or deliberately subvert.

**5. Controlling Idea:** A single sentence that captures the meaning of the entire story (see the dedicated Controlling Idea framework for depth).

**6. Beginning Hook, Middle Build, Ending Payoff:** The three-act macro structure, each with its own inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, and resolution.

### The Scene-by-Scene Spreadsheet

This is the micro-level tool. Every scene (or in marketing, every content unit — paragraph, email, slide, video segment) gets a row in the spreadsheet with the following columns:

**Scene Number:** Sequential ordering.

**Story Event:** What happens in this scene? Described as a single action with a subject and verb. "The client discovers their conversion rate has been miscalculated for six months."

**Value Charge:** What is the value at stake in this scene, and is it positive (+) or negative (-)? Values might include: success/failure, trust/suspicion, clarity/confusion, freedom/constraint, growth/stagnation. The value charge captures the emotional state at the end of the scene.

**Turning Point Type:** What type of event drives the scene's change? There are five types:
- **Action:** Something physical happens that changes the scene.
- **Revelation:** New information comes to light.
- **Decision:** A character makes a choice.
- **Coincidence:** An unexpected alignment of events (use sparingly).
- **Dilemma:** A character faces an impossible choice between two competing values.

**Polarity Shift:** Did the scene move from positive to negative (+ to -), negative to positive (- to +), positive to more positive (+ to ++), or negative to more negative (- to --)? A scene that does not shift polarity is not pulling its weight.

### Reading the Grid

Once the spreadsheet is complete, patterns emerge:

- **Monotonous polarity:** If every scene is positive, the story has no tension. If every scene is negative, the story is exhausting. Healthy stories alternate, with an overall trajectory that matches the genre (comedies trend upward, tragedies trend downward).

- **Missing turning points:** Scenes without clear turning points are filler. In marketing content, these are the paragraphs or slides that exist because the writer felt obligated to include them, not because they change anything.

- **Value confusion:** If different scenes are tracking different values without resolution, the story feels scattered. A case study that tracks "revenue growth" in one section and "team culture" in another without connecting them leaves the audience unsure what the story is about.

- **Genre violations:** If the Foolscap says the story is an action genre (conquest narrative) but the scenes are all internal worldview shifts, the story is violating its own genre promise.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Complete the Foolscap First

Before analyzing scenes, establish the macro architecture. For a marketing narrative:

1. Identify the external genre. What is the surface-level story type? "A small team defeats a massive competitor" is action. "A founder discovers a truth about their industry" is worldview/revelation. "A customer and brand build mutual loyalty" is love/loyalty.

2. Identify the internal genre. What changes inside the protagonist? Moving from ignorance to knowledge is worldview/education. Moving from selfishness to generosity is morality/redemption. Moving from obscurity to recognition is status/admiration.

3. List the obligatory scenes for both genres. These become your structural checkpoints.

4. List the conventions for both genres. These become your audience-expectation checkpoints.

5. Write the controlling idea as a single sentence.

6. Sketch the beginning hook, middle build, and ending payoff — each with its own five commandments (inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, resolution).

### Step 2: Build the Scene Spreadsheet

Break your content into its smallest meaningful units:
- For a case study: each section or major paragraph
- For an email sequence: each email, or each section within an email
- For a video: each scene or segment
- For a sales page: each scroll section or content block
- For a presentation: each slide or slide group

For each unit, fill in all columns: story event, value charge, turning point type, polarity shift.

### Step 3: Analyze the Pattern

Look at the spreadsheet as a whole:
- Chart the polarity shifts visually. You should see a wave pattern, not a flat line and not random noise.
- Check that every obligatory scene from the Foolscap appears somewhere in the spreadsheet.
- Check that all conventions are present.
- Identify scenes with no polarity shift — these are candidates for cutting or rewriting.
- Identify sequences of same-polarity scenes — these are where the audience gets bored or exhausted.

### Step 4: Diagnose and Prescribe

Common diagnoses:
- **"The middle sags":** The middle build has too many same-polarity scenes. Inject complications that reverse polarity.
- **"The ending feels unearned":** The polarity shift in the climax is too large relative to the progressive complications. The audience needed more incremental shifts to believe the final leap.
- **"It feels generic":** The obligatory scenes are present but the conventions are missing. The skeleton is right but the flesh is wrong for the genre.
- **"It is confusing":** Multiple values are tracked without hierarchy. Establish a primary value and make all others subordinate.

## Examples

### Example 1: Auditing a SaaS Case Study

**Foolscap:**
- External Genre: Performance (the team gets better at their job)
- Internal Genre: Worldview/Education (the team learns a new way of working)
- Controlling Idea: Operational excellence is achieved when a team replaces intuition-based decisions with data-driven systems.
- Obligatory Scenes: Inciting performance challenge, training/practice montage, moment of truth performance, public recognition of achievement.
- Conventions: Mentor figure, measurable metric, before/after contrast, skeptic who converts.

**Scene Spreadsheet (abbreviated):**

| # | Story Event | Value | Turning Point | Polarity |
|---|------------|-------|---------------|----------|
| 1 | Team misses Q3 targets for third consecutive quarter | Failure (-) | Revelation | neutral to - |
| 2 | New VP brings in analytics platform | Hope (+) | Action | - to + |
| 3 | Team resists the new tool, adoption stalls | Failure (-) | Decision | + to - |
| 4 | VP runs a pilot with 3 willing reps; they hit 120% of quota | Success (+) | Revelation | - to + |
| 5 | Skeptical senior rep sees pilot results, reluctantly tries it | Hope (+) | Decision | + to ++ |
| 6 | Full team adopts, Q4 target achieved for first time in a year | Success (++) | Action | ++ stays |
| 7 | Team wins company-wide performance award | Recognition (+++) | Action | ++ to +++ |

**Diagnosis:** Scenes 5-6-7 are all positive polarity with no reversal. The ending feels too easy. Prescription: Add a complication between scenes 5 and 6 — perhaps the skeptical senior rep discovers a flaw in the data that nearly derails the pilot, forcing a crisis of confidence that must be resolved before full adoption. This adds a negative polarity shift that makes the eventual success feel earned.

### Example 2: Auditing a Nonprofit Fundraising Video

**Foolscap:**
- External Genre: Society (community transformation)
- Internal Genre: Morality/Redemption (viewers move from apathy to action)
- Controlling Idea: Community transformation happens when individuals choose collective responsibility over personal comfort.

**Scene Spreadsheet (abbreviated):**

| # | Story Event | Value | Turning Point | Polarity |
|---|------------|-------|---------------|----------|
| 1 | Statistics about the community problem | Despair (-) | Revelation | neutral to - |
| 2 | More statistics, worse numbers | Deeper despair (--) | Revelation | - to -- |
| 3 | One family's personal story of suffering | Despair (--) | Revelation | -- stays |
| 4 | Organization's intervention begins | Hope (+) | Action | -- to + |
| 5 | Results and transformation | Joy (++) | Revelation | + to ++ |
| 6 | Call to action: donate now | Empowerment (+++) | Decision | ++ to +++ |

**Diagnosis:** Two problems. First, scenes 1-2-3 are monotonously negative with the same turning point type (all revelations). The audience is numbed, not engaged. Second, the jump from scene 3 (despair) to scene 4 (hope) is too abrupt — there is no character decision or crisis that bridges the gap. Prescription: Replace scene 2 with a scene where a specific person tries to solve the problem on their own and fails (action turning point, different from the revelation in scene 1). Make scene 3 the crisis — the person faces a dilemma between giving up and reaching out for help. Scene 4 then becomes the climax of that micro-arc: they choose to reach out, and the organization responds.

## When to Use

- When you need to diagnose why a piece of content is not working and surface-level fixes have not helped
- When auditing a large-scale narrative (webinar, video series, book, long-form sales page) for structural integrity
- When multiple stakeholders disagree about what is wrong with a piece of content — the grid provides objective evidence
- When planning a complex content piece and you need to ensure structural completeness before writing
- When training content teams to think structurally about narrative
- When you want to ensure genre expectations are being met without being formulaic

## When NOT to Use

- When the content is too short to benefit from scene-level analysis. A social media post or a short email does not need a spreadsheet — use the Five Commandments instead.
- When speed matters more than precision. Building a full Story Grid takes time. For quick content production, use the Story Circle or But/Therefore as faster diagnostics.
- When the content is not narrative. A product spec, a FAQ page, a pricing table — these are informational, not narrative, and the Story Grid does not apply.
- When the analysis will be done by someone unfamiliar with genre theory. The Story Grid requires a working knowledge of genre conventions and obligatory scenes. Without that knowledge, the Foolscap will be filled in incorrectly and the diagnosis will be wrong.
- When you are in the creative development phase. The Story Grid is an analytical tool, not a generative one. Use the Embryo Theory for creation and the Story Grid for post-creation analysis.

## Integration

- **Coyne Five Commandments:** The Five Commandments operate at the scene level; the Story Grid operates at the whole-story level. The spreadsheet should verify that each scene contains all five commandments. If a scene is missing its crisis, the polarity shift will feel arbitrary.
- **Coyne Genre Clover:** The Genre Clover defines the genres that feed into the Foolscap. Without a clear genre identification from the Clover, the Foolscap's obligatory scenes and conventions will be incomplete.
- **Coyne Controlling Idea:** The Controlling Idea is a required field in the Foolscap. It serves as the North Star for the entire grid — every scene should, in some way, test or advance the controlling idea.
- **Harmon Story Circle:** The Story Circle provides the macro shape; the Story Grid provides the micro analysis. You can map the 8 Story Circle steps onto the beginning hook, middle build, and ending payoff of the Foolscap. Steps 1-3 are roughly the beginning hook. Steps 4-6 are the middle build. Steps 7-8 are the ending payoff.
- **Harmon But/Therefore:** The polarity shift column in the spreadsheet should correlate with But/Therefore connections. A polarity reversal (+ to - or - to +) typically corresponds to a "but." A polarity continuation (+ to ++ or - to --) typically corresponds to a "therefore."

## Agent Routing

- **Use this framework** when the agent detects: content audit requests requiring detailed structural analysis, "why is this not converting" diagnostics, long-form content planning, genre-compliance checking, or training requests for structural narrative thinking.
- **Pass to Coyne Five Commandments** for scene-level analysis within the grid — when a specific scene needs deeper structural work.
- **Pass to Coyne Genre Clover** when genre identification is unclear or when the content operates in multiple genres that need to be separated and analyzed.
- **Pass to Coyne Controlling Idea** when the Foolscap reveals that the controlling idea is missing, muddled, or contradicted by the scene-level evidence.
- **Input required:** The complete content to be analyzed (or a detailed outline), the intended audience, and the intended outcome (what the content should make the audience think, feel, or do).
- **Output:** A completed Foolscap Global Story Grid, a scene-by-scene spreadsheet with value charges and polarity shifts, a diagnosis of structural issues, and specific prescriptions for fixing them.
