# But-Therefore Causality
> Trey Parker and Matt Stone's rule: every beat connects with "but" or "therefore," never "and then."

## Overview

In a 2011 NYU masterclass, South Park creators Trey Parker and Matt Stone revealed the simplest and most powerful test for narrative quality: take any two consecutive beats in your story and place a connector between them. If the connector is "and then," your story is a sequence of events. If the connector is "but" or "therefore," your story has causality.

"And then" = episodic. Things happen in order but don't cause each other.
"But" = complication. Something unexpected disrupts the expected path.
"Therefore" = consequence. Something happens as a direct result of what came before.

This distinction is the difference between a story and a list. A blog post where each section connects with "and also" will lose readers by the third section. A sales page where each section connects with "but" or "therefore" will pull readers forward because each section creates the need for the next.

The framework has roots deeper than comedy writing. Aristotle's concept of "necessity and probability" in *Poetics* makes the same argument: events in a plot should follow from each other, not merely follow after each other. Every screenwriting teacher from Syd Field to Blake Snyder to John Truby reinforces this principle. Parker and Stone simply gave it the catchiest formulation.

The test is deceptively simple. Applying it reveals that the majority of first-draft marketing content—especially case studies, email sequences, and brand timelines—is episodic rather than causal. Events are arranged chronologically ("We founded the company, then we raised money, then we launched, then we grew") rather than causally ("We founded the company on a specific thesis, which led us to raise from investors who shared that thesis, but the market responded differently than expected, therefore we had to pivot before launch"). Chronology is not story. Causality is story.

The power of this test is in its diagnostic speed. A writer can audit a 2,000-word draft in under five minutes by extracting beats and testing connectors. No other editing tool provides this much structural insight this quickly.

## Core Methodology

1. **Draft the Narrative**: Write the story in whatever form feels natural.
2. **Extract the Beat List**: Reduce each major beat to a single sentence.
3. **Apply the Connector Test**: Place "and then," "but," or "therefore" between each pair of consecutive beats.
4. **Flag "And Then" Connections**: Every "and then" is a structural weakness.
5. **Revise to Causality**: Convert each "and then" to a "but" or "therefore" by adding conflict, consequences, or causal links.
6. **Re-Test**: Run the connector test again until every transition is "but" or "therefore."
7. **Vary the Rhythm**: A story that is all "but" feels chaotic. A story that is all "therefore" feels predictable. Alternate them.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Draft Freely
- Write the first draft without worrying about causality. Let the story flow.
- Substep: This is important—applying the But-Therefore test during drafting creates paralysis. Use it as an editing tool, not a drafting constraint.

### Step 2: Extract the Beat List
- Substep: Read through the draft and mark every moment where something changes (a new piece of information, a new action, a new emotional state).
- Substep: Write each beat as a simple sentence: "The founder tried X." "The client said Y." "Revenue dropped to Z."
- Substep: Aim for 8-20 beats for most marketing narratives. Fewer than 8 suggests the story is too thin. More than 20 suggests it may need compression.

### Step 3: Apply the Connector Test
- Substep: Write the beats in a list. Between each pair, insert the most honest connector:
  - "The founder launched the product. [AND THEN / BUT / THEREFORE] The first reviews came in."
  - If the reviews were a direct consequence of the launch: "therefore."
  - If the reviews were unexpectedly negative: "but."
  - If the reviews happened chronologically but without causal link to the specific launch approach: "and then."
- Substep: Be ruthlessly honest. The temptation is to claim causality where there is only chronology.

### Step 4: Diagnose "And Then" Connections
- Each "and then" indicates one of three problems:
  - **Missing cause**: The events are causally connected but the narrative doesn't show it. Fix: add the causal link.
  - **Missing conflict**: The events proceed without complication. Fix: add an obstacle, surprise, or reversal between them.
  - **Irrelevant beat**: One of the two beats doesn't belong in this story. Fix: cut it.
- Substep: Classify each "and then" into one of these three categories before attempting to fix it.

### Step 5: Revise Each "And Then"
- **For missing cause**: Insert a sentence or paragraph that shows why Beat B follows from Beat A. "The launch targeted enterprise customers [therefore] the first reviews came from IT directors who judged the product against enterprise standards the team hadn't anticipated."
- **For missing conflict**: Add a "but" between the beats. "The founder launched the product. [But] the analytics showed that users were signing up and never returning after day one."
- **For irrelevant beats**: Remove the beat entirely. If the story still works without it, it was filler.
- Substep: When converting "and then" to "but," ensure the complication is genuine, not manufactured. Artificial obstacles feel manipulative.
- Substep: When converting "and then" to "therefore," ensure the consequence is specific. "Therefore things got worse" is lazy. "Therefore the team had to choose between fixing the onboarding flow and shipping the feature their biggest client had demanded" is specific.

### Step 6: Re-Test the Full Sequence
- Substep: After revisions, extract the new beat list and run the connector test again.
- Substep: New "and then" connections may have appeared where beats were added or restructured.
- Substep: Continue until every transition is "but" or "therefore."

### Step 7: Calibrate the But/Therefore Ratio
- Substep: Too many "buts" (complication after complication) creates exhaustion and a sense that nothing progresses.
- Substep: Too many "therefores" (consequence after consequence) creates a sense of inevitability that kills suspense.
- Substep: Ideal rhythm alternates: therefore-but-therefore-but, or clusters of 2-3 of one type before switching.
- Substep: The opening of the story benefits from "therefore" chains (building context and establishing logic). The middle benefits from "but" chains (introducing conflict). The ending benefits from a "therefore" chain that resolves the central tension causally.

### Step 8: Apply to Transitions Between Content Pieces
- Substep: The test works not just within a single piece but across a series.
- Substep: In an email sequence: Email 1 → Email 2 should connect with "but" or "therefore." If Email 2 could be read independently with no loss, the sequence lacks causality.
- Substep: In a content series: each installment should create the need for the next through consequence or complication.

## Examples

### Example 1: Weak vs. Strong Case Study

**Weak (And Then):**
"We founded the company in 2019. And then we raised a seed round. And then we launched our product. And then we got our first customers. And then we raised a Series A."

**Strong (But/Therefore):**
"We founded the company in 2019 with a clear thesis about remote team management. Therefore, we targeted distributed companies for our seed round, which closed in four months. But when we launched the product, our target customers told us our pricing model was wrong for their budget cycles. Therefore, we had to restructure our entire revenue model three months before our runway expired. But that constraint forced us to discover a usage-based pricing approach that actually accelerated adoption. Therefore, our Series A conversation started from a position of strength—not just growth metrics, but a pricing insight our competitors hadn't discovered."

### Example 2: Email Sequence Audit

**Email 1 → Email 2 connection:**
- Weak: "Yesterday I told you about the problem. Today I want to share a solution." (And then.)
- Strong: "Yesterday I showed you why the standard approach fails. But here's what confused me—if it fails, why does everyone still use it? Therefore, I spent three months researching the psychology behind the behavior. What I found changed everything." (But + therefore.)

**Email 3 → Email 4 connection:**
- Weak: "In the last email I shared case studies. Now let me tell you about our offer." (And then.)
- Strong: "You've seen the results three companies achieved with this framework. But those results required something most teams don't have—a structured implementation process. Therefore, we built one. And for the next 48 hours, it's available to you." (But + therefore.)

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Forcing False Causality
Not every sequence of events has a genuine causal connection. Forcing a "therefore" where the connection is coincidental or weak is worse than an honest "and then." If two events are genuinely unrelated, either cut one or restructure so they connect through an intermediate beat.

### Pitfall 2: All "But," No "Therefore"
A story where every transition is a complication ("but... but... but...") creates a sense of chaos without progress. The audience needs to feel the story moving forward, not just bouncing between obstacles. Alternate between "but" (complication) and "therefore" (consequence) to create forward motion with resistance.

### Pitfall 3: Using the Test Too Early
Applying But-Therefore during brainstorming or first-draft writing can create paralysis. The test is an editing tool. Draft freely, then diagnose with the test. Writers who try to maintain perfect causality in the first draft often produce stilted, overthought prose.

### Pitfall 4: Ignoring Emotional Causality
Not all causality is logical. Emotional causality is equally valid: "She got the promotion [therefore] she felt guilty about leaving her team behind [but] the guilt made her a more empathetic leader." The But-Therefore test should account for emotional consequences, not just practical ones.

### Pitfall 5: Applying to Non-Narrative Content
The test is designed for narrative content—stories with characters, events, and progression. Applying it to listicles, reference guides, or modular content creates confusion. In non-narrative formats, sections are deliberately independent, and "and then" connections between them are appropriate and expected.

## When to Use

- Editing and revising ANY narrative content—this is the single most efficient story diagnostic tool.
- Constructing email sequences where each email must motivate the open of the next.
- Building pitch decks where each slide must create the need for the next.
- Writing case studies, ad scripts, landing pages, and any content with sequential structure.
- Training writers and content creators—the test is simple enough to teach in five minutes and powerful enough to use for a career.

## When NOT to Use

- Listicle formats and modular content where sections are deliberately independent.
- Reference content (glossaries, resource libraries, FAQs) where sequential reading is not expected.
- During initial brainstorming—apply it in editing, not ideation.
- Poetry, artistic expression, or content where associative logic is intentionally preferred over causal logic.

## Integration

- **Story Beats and Scenes**: The But-Therefore test is applied to the beat list generated by beat analysis. Every beat transition must pass.
- **Three-Act Structure**: Act transitions are the highest-stakes "but" or "therefore" moments. Act I → Act II is typically a "but" (the inciting incident disrupts). Act II → Act III is typically a "therefore" (the climax is a consequence of everything in Act II).
- **Tension-Release Loop**: "But" connectors create tension. "Therefore" connectors can create either tension (negative consequence) or release (positive consequence).
- **Editing Compression Framework**: The But-Therefore test identifies beats that can be cut (those connected by "and then"). It's the first step of compression.
- **Stakes Escalation Ladder**: Each stakes escalation should be a "therefore" (consequence) or "but" (complication) from the previous level.

## Agent Routing

- **Assisting Agents**:
  - Story Architect Agent — uses the test during structural outlining to ensure causal architecture.
  - Copywriter Agent — applies the test at the sentence level for transitions and connective tissue.
  - Quality Assurance Agent — uses the test as a final check before content is approved.
  - Training Agent — teaches the framework to new team members and content creators.

## Quick Reference: The But-Therefore Audit Template

Use this template to audit any narrative content in under five minutes:

1. Extract each major beat as a one-sentence description.
2. Between each pair of consecutive beats, write the most honest connector:
   - **THEREFORE** = consequence (this happened as a direct result)
   - **BUT** = complication (this happened unexpectedly, disrupting the expected path)
   - **AND THEN** = chronology (this happened next, but without causal connection)
3. For each "AND THEN" connection, diagnose the problem:
   - [ ] Missing cause — the connection exists but is not shown
   - [ ] Missing conflict — the events proceed without complication
   - [ ] Irrelevant beat — one of the two beats does not belong
4. Apply the fix:
   - Missing cause → add a sentence showing the causal link
   - Missing conflict → add a "but" by introducing an obstacle or surprise
   - Irrelevant beat → cut it entirely
5. Re-test until every connection is "but" or "therefore."
6. Check the but/therefore ratio. Target: roughly 50/50 with natural variation.

This audit is the single fastest way to improve any piece of narrative content. Teach it to every writer on the team.
