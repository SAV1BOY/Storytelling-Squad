# Data Storytelling Framework
> Turn numbers into narratives through context, insight, implications, and action — making data drive decisions.

## Overview
Data without story is noise. Numbers on a slide do not change minds; narratives built on numbers do. This framework provides a systematic approach to transforming raw data into decision-driving narratives. The core principle is that data storytelling is not visualization — it is interpretation. A chart shows what happened. A data story explains why it matters and what to do about it. The framework moves through four beats: establishing why the data matters (context), revealing what the data shows (insight), explaining what it means for the audience (implications), and prescribing what to do about it (action). Every data story must answer the audience's silent question: "So what?"

## Core Methodology
The Data Storytelling Framework operates on four sequential beats:

1. **Context (Why This Data Matters)** — Before presenting any number, establish why the audience should care. Context is the narrative frame that transforms a statistic from trivia into information. Without context, data is meaningless. "Revenue grew 12%" means nothing. "Revenue grew 12% while the market grew 28%" is a crisis.

2. **Insight (What the Data Reveals)** — The specific, non-obvious finding that emerges from the data. An insight is not a data point; it is an interpretation. "Sales increased 15%" is a data point. "Sales increased 15% despite a 20% reduction in ad spend, suggesting organic demand is strengthening" is an insight.

3. **So What (Implications)** — What the insight means for the audience's strategy, operations, or decisions. Implications bridge the gap between understanding and action. "If organic demand is strengthening, we may be over-investing in paid acquisition."

4. **Action (What to Do About It)** — A specific, bounded recommendation derived from the data. Not "we should look into this" but "I recommend we reallocate 30% of Q3 paid budget to content marketing and measure organic pipeline growth over 90 days."

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Know Your Audience's Decision
- Before touching the data, identify the decision the audience needs to make
- Every data presentation exists to inform a decision: budget allocation, strategy shift, resource deployment, go/no-go
- Frame your analysis around that decision: "This analysis will help us decide whether to expand into the European market"
- If there is no decision to be made, the data presentation is informational, not a story — and it will be ignored
- Ask: "What would the audience do differently if they understood this data?" If the answer is "nothing," you have the wrong data or the wrong audience.

### Step 2: Establish Context
- **Historical context**: How does this data compare to previous periods? "Revenue is $2.1M this quarter, up from $1.8M last quarter and $1.2M a year ago."
- **Competitive context**: How does this compare to peers or the market? "Our NPS of 42 is above the industry average of 31 but below category leaders at 55+."
- **Aspirational context**: How does this compare to goals? "We targeted 500 new customers this quarter and acquired 380 — a 76% attainment rate."
- **Emotional context**: Why should the audience feel something about this data? "That 76% attainment means 120 families who needed our product did not find us."
- Choose 1-2 context frames. Too many contexts dilute focus.

### Step 3: Extract and Present Insights
- An insight is a pattern, anomaly, or relationship in the data that is not obvious from the raw numbers
- Techniques for finding insights: compare across segments, look for trends over time, identify outliers, calculate correlations, seek contradictions
- Present each insight as a narrative sentence, not a chart title: "While overall revenue grew 12%, our enterprise segment grew 31% and our SMB segment declined 4% — we are becoming a different company."
- Limit to 3 insights per presentation. More than 3 overwhelms the audience and dilutes the narrative.
- Sequence insights from most surprising to most actionable: surprise earns attention, action maintains it

### Step 4: Articulate the "So What"
- For each insight, state the implication in terms of the audience's priorities
- Use conditional language to invite buy-in: "If this trend continues..." or "This suggests that..."
- Connect implications to the audience's existing mental models: "This confirms what the sales team has been reporting anecdotally."
- Challenge assumptions when the data warrants it: "We assumed SMB was our growth engine. The data tells a different story."
- The "so what" is the emotional pivot of the data story — this is where the audience moves from understanding to urgency

### Step 5: Recommend Action
- Every data story should end with a specific recommendation
- Format: "Based on [insight], I recommend [action] within [timeframe], measured by [metric]"
- Include the cost of inaction: "If we do not adjust our SMB strategy, we project a further 8% decline next quarter, equivalent to $340K in lost revenue."
- Present 2-3 scenarios if appropriate: aggressive, moderate, conservative — each with data-supported projections
- Make the recommendation concrete enough to be implemented: "Reassign 2 SDRs from SMB outbound to enterprise inbound support" not "invest more in enterprise"

### Step 6: Design the Visual Narrative
- Every chart should have a narrative title, not a descriptive one: "Enterprise is driving all growth" instead of "Revenue by Segment"
- Use annotation to guide the audience's eye: highlight the data point that matters, de-emphasize the rest
- One chart per insight. Multi-insight charts confuse.
- Sequence visuals to match the narrative arc: context chart, insight chart, implication chart, action chart
- Use progressive reveal in presentations: show the axes, then the baseline, then the trend, then the annotation. Let the audience experience the insight unfolding.

## Examples

### Example 1: Quarterly Business Review — SaaS Company
- **Context**: "Last quarter, we set a goal of $2.4M in new ARR. We closed $2.1M — 87.5% attainment. Respectable. But the story underneath the number is more important."
- **Insight**: "73% of our new ARR came from 4 enterprise deals. Our pipeline had 40 deals; 4 of them carried the quarter. Meanwhile, our average deal size in the mid-market dropped 22% quarter over quarter."
- **So What**: "We are increasingly dependent on a small number of large deals. If even one of those 4 deals had slipped, we would have missed plan by $500K. This is a concentration risk, and our mid-market weakness is accelerating it."
- **Action**: "I recommend two moves: (1) Launch a dedicated mid-market sales motion with a dedicated team of 3 reps by end of Q2. Goal: 30 mid-market deals averaging $35K. (2) Expand enterprise pipeline to maintain at least 8 qualified opportunities at all times, reducing single-deal dependency."

### Example 2: Marketing Campaign Performance Report
- **Context**: "We spent $180K on paid acquisition in Q1 across three channels: Google ($80K), Meta ($60K), LinkedIn ($40K). Our target CPL was $45."
- **Insight**: "Blended CPL was $52 — over target. But channel-level data reveals a split: Google CPL was $38 (below target), Meta CPL was $48 (slightly over), and LinkedIn CPL was $112 (2.5x target). LinkedIn also generated the highest SQL conversion rate at 18%, versus Google at 6% and Meta at 4%."
- **So What**: "LinkedIn is expensive per lead but produces the highest quality leads. When we calculate cost per SQL instead of cost per lead, LinkedIn is actually our most efficient channel at $622/SQL versus Google at $633/SQL and Meta at $1,200/SQL."
- **Action**: "I recommend shifting $20K from Meta to LinkedIn in Q2. We project this will generate 15 additional SQLs at the same total budget. I also recommend eliminating CPL as our primary metric and replacing it with cost per SQL, which better reflects actual business impact."

## When to Use
- Quarterly business reviews and board presentations
- Campaign performance reports and marketing analytics
- Strategy presentations where data informs the recommendation
- Any presentation where the audience must make a decision informed by data
- Financial reviews, investor updates, and operational dashboards

## When NOT to Use
- When the audience needs raw data for their own analysis (provide a data export, not a narrative)
- When the data is preliminary and not yet reliable enough for interpretation
- When the data does not actually support a meaningful insight (do not force a narrative on inconclusive data)
- When the audience has explicitly requested a data dump without interpretation

- **Structure Agent**: Maps the four beats and sequences insights for narrative impact
- **Drafting Agent**: Writes the narrative, chart titles, and recommendation
- **Design Agent**: Creates data visualizations aligned with the narrative arc
- **Editing Agent**: Ensures the narrative is rigorous, the data supports the claims, and the recommendation is actionable
- Route to this framework when the task involves presenting data in a way that drives a specific decision or action
