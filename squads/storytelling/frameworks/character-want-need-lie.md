# Character Want-Need-Lie
> The engine of character depth: external want, internal need, and the false belief holding them back.

## Overview

Every compelling character operates on three levels simultaneously. The Want is what they consciously pursue—the external goal they can articulate. The Need is what they actually require for genuine fulfillment—the internal transformation they cannot yet see. The Lie is the false belief that keeps them pursuing the Want instead of addressing the Need.

This triad originates from narrative psychology and has been refined by screenwriters (Craig Mazin, K.M. Weiland) and novelists for decades. It explains why flat characters fail to engage audiences and why the deepest stories resonate: they dramatize the gap between what people say they want and what they actually need.

In marketing storytelling, this framework is transformative. Every customer avatar has a Want (the stated problem they're trying to solve), a Need (the deeper transformation they're actually seeking), and a Lie (the false belief that has kept them stuck). A product that only addresses the Want is a commodity. A brand that addresses the Need through the lens of the Lie becomes irreplaceable.

This framework is the difference between "we sell project management software" (Want) and "we help teams reclaim the creative energy that bureaucracy stole from them" (Need, addressing the Lie that more process equals more control).

## Core Methodology

1. **Identify the External Want**: What does the character (customer) say they're looking for? What would they type into a search bar?
2. **Identify the Internal Need**: What transformation would genuinely improve their life? What do they need that they haven't articulated?
3. **Identify the Lie**: What false belief prevents them from pursuing the Need directly? What assumption are they making about themselves, their industry, or the world?
4. **Map the Gap**: The distance between Want and Need is the character's arc. The Lie is what maintains that distance.
5. **Design the Shattering Moment**: At what point in the story does the Lie crack? What evidence or experience makes it untenable?
6. **Resolve Want Through Need**: The most satisfying stories deliver the Want as a consequence of addressing the Need, not as a separate achievement.
7. **Embed in Brand Positioning**: The brand's unique value proposition lives in the gap between Want and Need.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Surface the External Want
- Substep: Interview customers or analyze search data, support tickets, and sales conversations. What language do they use to describe their problem?
- Substep: The Want is always concrete and measurable. "I want more leads." "I want to lose 20 pounds." "I want my team to ship faster."
- Substep: Write the Want as a character would say it—in first person, in their vocabulary, without abstraction.
- Substep: Validate the Want against market data. Is this what a significant audience actually wants?

### Step 2: Excavate the Internal Need
- Substep: Ask "why?" five times. "I want more leads." Why? "To grow revenue." Why does that matter? "So I can prove this business is viable." Why does that matter? "Because I left a safe career and everyone said I was crazy." Why does that matter? "Because I need to know I made the right choice."
- Substep: The Need is always emotional, identity-based, or relational. It's about who the character wants to become, not what they want to acquire.
- Substep: Common Needs in marketing contexts: validation, autonomy, belonging, competence, legacy, security, freedom, meaning.
- Substep: The Need should resonate at a level deeper than the product category. The Need for someone buying accounting software might be "I need to feel like a competent business owner, not a fraud."

### Step 3: Name the Lie
- Substep: The Lie is the false belief that bridges the gap between Want and Need in the character's mind.
- Substep: Formula: "I believe that [achieving the Want] will give me [the Need] because [the Lie]."
- Substep: Example: "I believe that getting more leads will make me feel like my business is viable because I believe that revenue is the only valid measure of success."
- Substep: Common marketing Lies:
  - "More is always better" (more tools, more features, more effort).
  - "If I just work harder, it will work" (effort as the only variable).
  - "The problem is [external factor]" when the problem is [internal approach].
  - "I'm not [enough]" (not smart enough, not experienced enough, not connected enough).
  - "Success requires suffering" (hustle culture Lie).
  - "This is just how our industry works" (learned helplessness).
- Substep: Validate the Lie by checking if customers exhibit behaviors consistent with it. Do they keep buying solutions that address the Want without achieving the Need?

### Step 4: Map the Arc
- Substep: Plot the character's journey from Lie-driven pursuit of Want to Need-aware transformation.
- Substep: The arc has phases:
  1. **Living the Lie**: Character pursues the Want using Lie-based strategies.
  2. **Lie Under Pressure**: Circumstances challenge the Lie but the character doubles down.
  3. **Lie Cracking**: Evidence accumulates that the Lie is false. A mentor, an event, or a failure makes the Lie visible.
  4. **Lie Shattering**: The character confronts the Lie directly and chooses the Need over the Want.
  5. **Integration**: The character pursues the Need and, often, achieves the Want as a byproduct.
- Substep: In short-form marketing, this arc may be compressed to a single paragraph. In long-form, it can span the entire piece.

### Step 5: Design the Shattering Moment
- Substep: This is the story's most important beat. It must be dramatic, specific, and emotionally resonant.
- Substep: The shattering moment works when it is both external (an event happens) and internal (the character realizes something).
- Substep: In marketing, the shattering moment is often the discovery of the product, methodology, or approach—but framed as a worldview shift, not a purchase decision.
- Substep: The shattering moment should be the point in the story where the audience feels a shift in their own beliefs, not just the character's.

### Step 6: Resolve Through Integration
- Substep: Show the character achieving the Want as a natural consequence of addressing the Need.
- Substep: "She didn't just get more leads. She built a business she was proud of. And the leads came because that pride was visible in everything she created."
- Substep: The resolution should make the audience feel that the Lie was the real obstacle all along.

### Step 7: Translate to Brand Positioning
- Substep: The brand's role in the story is as the catalyst for the shattering moment—not as the hero.
- Substep: Position statement formula: "We help [character] who wants [Want] discover that [Lie is false] so they can [achieve Need] and, as a result, [achieve Want in a sustainable way]."
- Substep: Test the positioning: does it address the Want (so customers recognize themselves), challenge the Lie (so they feel understood), and promise the Need (so they feel inspired)?

## Examples

### Example 1: Productivity App
- **Want**: "I want to get more done in less time."
- **Need**: To feel in control of their life and present for the moments that matter.
- **Lie**: "If I optimize every minute, I'll finally feel caught up."
- **Story**: A product manager tracks every hour, uses six productivity tools, and still feels behind. The shattering moment: her daughter draws a picture of "Mommy at work" and it's identical to "Mommy at home"—both show her on a laptop. She realizes optimization was the problem, not the solution. She needs fewer things done with full presence, not more things done with partial attention. The app is positioned not as a productivity tool but as a "priority clarity" tool.

### Example 2: B2B Consulting Firm
- **Want**: "I want to scale my company past $10M."
- **Need**: To build an organization that doesn't depend entirely on the founder.
- **Lie**: "I'm the only one who can maintain the quality, so I need to be involved in everything."
- **Story**: A founder works 80-hour weeks and revenue plateaus at $3M because he's the bottleneck. He hires consultants to optimize processes (Want-driven). Marginal improvement. The shattering moment: a key employee quits and says, "I came here to build something, but you won't let me." He realizes the Lie—his control isn't protecting quality, it's preventing growth. The consulting firm helps him build systems that transfer his judgment to the team. Revenue hits $10M, but more importantly, he takes his first real vacation in five years.

## When to Use

- Customer avatar development and audience research synthesis.
- Brand positioning and messaging strategy.
- Case study and testimonial story construction.
- Sales page narrative design—the Want/Need/Lie maps perfectly to problem/agitate/solve structures.
- Content strategy—the Lie is an inexhaustible source of content topics.
- Ad creative—the most powerful ads name the Lie.

## When NOT to Use

- Purely transactional contexts where the customer knows exactly what they need (commodity purchases, repeat orders).
- Technical documentation or how-to content where character depth is unnecessary.
- Audiences in emergency situations—they need solutions, not psychology.
- When the Want and Need are genuinely aligned and there is no Lie—some purchases are straightforward and don't benefit from forced depth.

## Integration

- **Theme-Premise-Controlling Idea**: The Lie is the counter-argument to the Controlling Idea. The story's argument is that the Lie is false and the Need is real.
- **Three-Act Structure**: The Lie is established in Act I, challenged in Act II, and shattered in Act III.
- **Stakes Escalation Ladder**: The Lie raises stakes because it ensures the character's approach will fail at higher and higher levels until confronted.
- **Logline Formula**: The Want is the "goal" in the logline. The Lie is embedded in the "obstacle."
- **Story Brief Framework**: The "core belief to change" field maps directly to the Lie.
- **Contrast and Juxtaposition**: Want vs. Need is one of the most powerful contrast pairs in storytelling.
