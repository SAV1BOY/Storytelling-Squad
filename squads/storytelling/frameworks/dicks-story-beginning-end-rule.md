# Dicks' Story Beginning/End Rule (Rule of Opposites)
> The first sentence and the last sentence of a story should be as opposite as possible — ensuring maximum transformation contrast and emotional arc.

## Overview

Matthew Dicks' Beginning/End Rule is one of the most practical and immediately applicable principles in narrative craft. The rule is simple: the first sentence of your story and the last sentence of your story should be as opposite as possible. This opposition creates a transformation arc that audiences feel in their bodies, even if they cannot articulate why the story worked.

The rule emerges from Dicks' observation that the best stories at The Moth, in literature, in film, and in everyday conversation share a common structure: the protagonist at the end of the story is fundamentally different from the protagonist at the beginning. The greater the distance between those two states, the more powerful the story feels.

This is not a rule about happy endings or sad beginnings. The opposition can move in any direction. A story can begin in joy and end in sorrow, begin in ignorance and end in knowledge, begin in isolation and end in connection, or any combination. What matters is the distance between the poles.

The rule also functions as a diagnostic tool. If you cannot write an opening and closing that are genuinely opposite, the story may not contain enough transformation to be worth telling. The rule forces honesty about whether a narrative actually goes somewhere.

## Core Methodology

### The Principle

Every story is fundamentally about change. The Beginning/End Rule ensures that the change is visible, felt, and structurally embedded in the narrative. It is not enough for the protagonist to "learn a lesson" or "grow as a person" — those are abstractions. The rule demands concrete, specific opposition between two states.

### Types of Opposition

**Emotional Opposition**
- Beginning: "I was terrified of public speaking."
- End: "I stood on stage and forgot the audience was there."

**Circumstantial Opposition**
- Beginning: "I had $47 in my bank account and a lease I couldn't pay."
- End: "I wrote a check for the full year's rent without checking the balance."

**Belief Opposition**
- Beginning: "I believed that asking for help was weakness."
- End: "I called three people before breakfast and asked each of them for a favor."

**Relational Opposition**
- Beginning: "My father and I hadn't spoken in four years."
- End: "He answered on the first ring, like he'd been waiting."

**Identity Opposition**
- Beginning: "I introduced myself as a marketing consultant."
- End: "I introduced myself as a storyteller."

**Status Opposition**
- Beginning: "I was the most junior person in the room, sitting against the wall."
- End: "The CEO asked me to stay after everyone else left."

**Knowledge Opposition**
- Beginning: "I thought I understood what my customers wanted."
- End: "I realized I had never once asked them."

### The Rule as Diagnostic Tool

Before investing time in crafting a full story, apply this test:

1. Write the opening sentence that establishes the protagonist's state.
2. Write the closing sentence that establishes the protagonist's transformed state.
3. Place them side by side.
4. Ask: "Are these genuinely opposite? Is the distance between them meaningful?"

If the distance is small, you have three options:
- **Dig deeper:** The opposition exists but you have not found it yet. Ask "What was really different?" until you reach a genuine contrast.
- **Expand the timeframe:** Sometimes the story needs to cover more ground to capture the full transformation.
- **Abandon the story:** Not every experience contains a narrative arc. Some moments are interesting but not storyworthy. The rule helps you identify this early, saving you the effort of crafting a narrative that goes nowhere.

### Writing the Opening

The opening sentence must do three things:
1. **Establish the "before" state** with specificity and sensory detail
2. **Ground the audience** in a time, place, or situation
3. **Create curiosity** that earns attention for the next sentence

Dicks emphasizes that the opening should be active, not expository. Begin in the middle of a moment, not with background information. "I was sitting in a plastic chair in a hospital hallway, counting ceiling tiles" is better than "Let me tell you about the time I learned something important at a hospital."

The opening should feel like the reader has walked into a room where something is already happening. Context can be filled in later. The opening's job is to anchor the "before" state and create forward momentum.

### Writing the Closing

The closing sentence must do two things:
1. **Land on or immediately after the 5-second moment** (the instant of transformation)
2. **Demonstrate the "after" state** through action, image, or dialogue — not explanation

Critical rule: do not explain the transformation. Do not tell the audience what they should feel. Do not add a moral, a lesson, or a reflection after the closing. The opposition between the opening and closing IS the meaning. Trust the audience to feel it.

Dicks calls this "landing the plane." The worst thing a storyteller can do is circle the runway after landing. Once the transformation is visible, stop.

**Anti-pattern — the post-landing circle:**
"I stood on stage and forgot the audience was there. And that's when I realized that my fear of public speaking was really a fear of being seen, and that being seen is actually a gift, and I've carried that lesson with me ever since."

**Correct landing:**
"I stood on stage and forgot the audience was there."

Stop. The opposition with the opening ("I was terrified of public speaking") does all the work. The audience feels the arc without being told to feel it.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify the Transformation

Before writing anything, answer: "How is the protagonist different at the end of this story than at the beginning?" Be specific. "They grew" is not an answer. "They went from believing they were unqualified to realizing their unconventional background was their greatest asset" is an answer.

### Step 2: Name the Poles

Write two short phrases:
- **Before:** [specific state, belief, emotion, circumstance]
- **After:** [opposite state, belief, emotion, circumstance]

Check: are these genuinely opposite? Could someone who read both phrases feel the distance between them?

### Step 3: Draft the Opening Sentence

Convert the "Before" pole into a concrete, grounded, active sentence. Include sensory detail. Place the protagonist in a specific moment, not in a general state.

**Weak:** "I used to be insecure about my writing."
**Strong:** "I deleted the email seventeen times before hitting send, and even then I closed my laptop like it was evidence."

### Step 4: Draft the Closing Sentence

Convert the "After" pole into a concrete, grounded sentence that demonstrates (not explains) the transformation. The closing should feel like a door clicking shut — definitive and quiet.

**Weak:** "I learned to be confident in my writing."
**Strong:** "I hit send without rereading it. I didn't need to."

### Step 5: Build the Middle

With the opening and closing locked, the middle of the story becomes clear: it is the journey between two opposite poles. Every beat in the middle should either move the protagonist closer to the "after" state or pull them back toward the "before" state. Beats that do neither are candidates for cutting.

### Step 6: Verify the Arc

Read the opening, then the closing, then the opening again. Ask:
- Does the closing feel earned?
- Is the distance between them surprising?
- Would an audience who heard only these two sentences understand that a transformation occurred?
- Is the transformation shown rather than told?

## Examples

### Example 1: Case Study for a Leadership Development Firm

**Before pole:** A newly promoted VP who cannot make decisions without checking with her former peers.
**After pole:** A VP who makes a difficult decision alone and stands behind it when challenged.

**Opening:** "Three weeks into my new role, I walked into the break room and asked my former teammate if she thought we should approve the Henderson proposal. She stared at me and said, 'You're my boss now. Why are you asking me?'"

**Closing:** "When the board questioned the Henderson decision, I said, 'I approved it because it was right.' No one else spoke. No one needed to."

**Application:** This case study, structured with the Beginning/End Rule, demonstrates transformation in a way that data alone cannot. The reader feels the VP's growth. The leadership development firm's value is proven through narrative contrast, not through testimonials or metrics.

### Example 2: Brand Transformation Story for an Annual Report

**Before pole:** A company that defined success by revenue growth alone.
**After pole:** A company that measures success by customer outcome metrics.

**Opening:** "In January, our all-hands started the way it always did: the CFO put up the revenue slide. The room cheered. No one asked what those numbers meant for the people who paid them."

**Closing:** "In December, the all-hands started with a video from a customer whose business we saved. The revenue slide came later. No one noticed."

**Application:** This story structure transforms a dry annual report narrative into an emotionally resonant account of organizational change. The opposition between the two all-hands meetings makes the transformation visceral.

### Example 3: Testimonial for an Online Education Platform

**Before pole:** A student who sees herself as "not a math person."
**After pole:** A student who teaches math concepts to her coworkers.

**Opening:** "I was the person who said 'I'll handle the words, you handle the numbers' in every meeting, every project, every partnership for fifteen years."

**Closing:** "Last Thursday, a colleague asked me to explain the variance analysis. I drew it on a whiteboard. I didn't even pause."

## When to Use

- Crafting any narrative that needs an emotional arc: case studies, testimonials, founder stories, brand narratives
- Testing whether a story candidate has enough transformation to be worth telling
- Editing existing stories that feel flat or aimless — apply the rule to find what is missing
- Structuring presentations that need a clear "before and after" throughline
- Writing ad copy or campaign creative that relies on transformation narrative
- Coaching speakers, writers, or content creators on narrative structure
- Video testimonial scripting where the "before" and "after" states need to be visually and emotionally distinct

## When NOT to Use

- Informational content that does not require narrative arc (product specs, FAQs, how-to guides)
- Stories where the transformation is ambiguous or ongoing (some stories do not have clean endings, and forcing opposition would be dishonest)
- When you are still in the story-discovery phase (use First/Last/Best/Worst or Homework for Life first)
- Strategic narrative planning where the goal is a framework, not a single story (use Howell's Canvas or Story Cycle)
- When the story is about a community or system rather than an individual protagonist (the rule works best with a single character)

## Integration

- **Dicks' Storyworthy:** The Beginning/End Rule is built into the Storyworthy methodology. The 5-second moment defines the "after" state, and the opposite of that moment defines the opening.
- **Dicks' First/Last/Best/Worst:** After discovering potential stories through the prompt exercise, apply the Beginning/End Rule to test which stories have the strongest transformation arc.
- **Dicks' Homework for Life:** Each Homework entry can be tested with the rule: "If I built a story around this moment, what would the opening and closing sentences be?"
- **Howell's ABT Framework:** The AND clause often captures the "before" state, and the THEREFORE clause captures the "after" state. The Beginning/End Rule ensures the distance between them is maximized.
- **Hall's Gap-Bridge Framework:** Hall's "Normal" maps to the opening state, and "New Normal" maps to the closing state. The Beginning/End Rule ensures these two normals are genuinely different.
- **Hall's Story Formula:** The "Significant Moment" in Hall's formula is the pivot point between the two opposite poles. The Beginning/End Rule provides the structural frame; Hall's formula provides the emotional criteria.
