# Ganz Public Narrative
> The complete framework for turning personal experience into collective action through Story of Self, Story of Us, and Story of Now.

## Overview

Public Narrative is a leadership practice developed by Marshall Ganz, Senior Lecturer in Leadership, Organizing, and Civil Society at Harvard Kennedy School. Ganz developed the framework from his experience as a civil rights organizer, farm worker union organizer with Cesar Chavez, and decades of teaching at Harvard. The framework was notably deployed by the Obama 2008 campaign, where it became the structural backbone of the campaign's grassroots organizing training — teaching thousands of ordinary volunteers to tell compelling stories that mobilized communities to action.

Public Narrative is not a storytelling technique in the entertainment sense. It is a leadership practice that uses narrative to mobilize others toward shared purpose. Its central insight is that leadership requires enabling others to act under conditions of uncertainty, and people act based on emotion, not information. The leader's job is to connect deeply held values to specific emotions that motivate specific actions.

The framework has three interlocking components:
- **Story of Self**: Why I am called to this work (personal values made visible through choice)
- **Story of Us**: Why WE are called to this work (shared values and identity)
- **Story of Now**: Why we must act NOW (urgent challenge demanding collective response)

Each component follows the same internal structure: challenge, choice, outcome. A character faces a challenge relevant to shared values, makes a choice that reveals those values, and experiences an outcome that teaches something about what those values mean in practice.

The power of Public Narrative is that it is not one person telling others what to do. It is one person sharing why they care, inviting others to recognize shared values, and articulating a moment that demands collective action. The audience doesn't follow the narrator — they act on their own values, which the narrator has made visible.

## Core Methodology

### The Architecture: Self + Us + Now

Public Narrative works as a single, integrated story told in three movements. Each movement builds on the previous one:

**Story of Self** establishes the narrator's moral authority. Not credentials. Not resume. Moral authority — the lived experience that reveals why this person cares deeply about this particular issue. The audience learns: "This person's commitment is real because it comes from genuine experience."

**Story of Us** expands from individual to collective. It identifies the shared values, experiences, and hopes that bind the narrator to the audience as a community. The audience shifts from "I understand why THEY care" to "I recognize why WE care."

**Story of Now** creates urgency and demands action. It identifies the specific threat to shared values, articulates why the moment demands response, and proposes a concrete action the community can take together. The audience shifts from "I understand why we care" to "I know what we must do."

The transition between these movements is critical:
- Self → Us: "And I learned that I wasn't alone in this. Many of you have faced the same..."
- Us → Now: "And everything we've built together is now at risk because..."

### The Internal Structure: Challenge, Choice, Outcome

Within each movement, the narrative follows a consistent pattern:

**Challenge**: A moment of difficulty, conflict, or uncertainty. The challenge must be specific (not abstract) and must connect to shared values (not just personal inconvenience). The challenge creates emotional engagement because the audience wonders: "What would I do?"

**Choice**: The protagonist makes a choice in response to the challenge. This is the moral center of the story. The choice reveals values — not because the narrator says "I value X" but because the action demonstrates it. The audience sees values enacted, not just proclaimed.

**Outcome**: The result of the choice. The outcome teaches something about what happens when those values are acted upon. It does not need to be positive — stories of failure, sacrifice, or partial victory can be more powerful than stories of unqualified success, because they demonstrate the cost of commitment.

### Values and Emotional Mapping

Ganz emphasizes that specific values connect to specific emotions, and specific emotions motivate specific actions:

- **Community** → feelings of belonging, solidarity → actions of mutual support
- **Justice** → feelings of outrage, moral clarity → actions of confrontation and advocacy
- **Hope** → feelings of possibility, agency → actions of initiative and creation
- **Empathy** → feelings of connection, compassion → actions of care and service
- **Dignity** → feelings of self-respect, defiance → actions of resistance and standing firm
- **Urgency** → feelings of anxiety transformed into determination → immediate action

The narrator's job is to identify which values are at stake, evoke the corresponding emotions through story, and channel those emotions toward a specific action.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Identify Your Core Values Story
Before constructing the full narrative, identify the specific moment in your life when you chose to commit to this work, cause, or mission. This is not your whole biography. It is a single moment — or a small number of moments — where a challenge forced a choice that revealed your values.

Questions to find this moment:
- When did you first realize this mattered to you?
- What experience made this personal, not just professional?
- What did you have to give up or risk to pursue this?
- What moment would you point to and say, "That's when I knew"?

### Step 2: Craft the Story of Self
Structure the moment as challenge → choice → outcome:
- Set the scene with enough detail to be vivid (where were you, who was there, what was at stake?)
- Describe the challenge as you experienced it (not in retrospect — capture the uncertainty of the moment)
- Describe the choice you made and why
- Describe what happened as a result — and what you learned about your own values

Keep it to 2-3 minutes in spoken form. Longer is not better. The audience needs enough detail to feel the moment, not a comprehensive memoir.

### Step 3: Identify the Shared Story
Map the connection between your personal story and the community's experience:
- What values from your Story of Self are shared by this audience?
- What experiences has this community had that parallel yours?
- What stories, symbols, or reference points does this community already share?
- What binds this group together as "us" rather than a collection of individuals?

### Step 4: Craft the Story of Us
Articulate the shared identity through challenge → choice → outcome at the collective level:
- "We" have faced challenges that revealed our shared values
- "We" have made choices together that defined who we are as a community
- "We" have experienced outcomes that strengthened our identity

This is not about telling the audience what they have in common. It is about reflecting their shared experience back to them in a way that makes it visible and meaningful.

### Step 5: Define the Urgent Challenge
Identify the specific threat or opportunity that demands action NOW:
- What threatens the values we've just articulated?
- Why is this moment different from other moments?
- What happens if we don't act?
- What is the cost of delay?

The urgency must be genuine. Manufactured urgency is detected and destroys trust.

### Step 6: Craft the Story of Now
Structure the call to action as challenge → choice → outcome:
- The challenge: here is the specific threat to our shared values
- The choice: here is the specific action we can take together
- The outcome: here is what becomes possible if we act (and what we lose if we don't)

The action must be concrete, specific, and achievable. "We must change the world" is not a call to action. "We need 500 people to show up at the city council meeting on Tuesday" is.

## Examples

### Example 1: Nonprofit Fundraising Campaign
A climate-focused nonprofit used Public Narrative for their annual fundraising campaign:

**Story of Self**: The executive director told the story of growing up near a river that flooded her family's home twice during her childhood. The second flood destroyed her grandmother's house permanently. She described the moment of standing in the mud, watching her grandmother cry over lost photo albums, and deciding that no family should lose their home to a preventable disaster. Challenge: environmental destruction hitting home — literally. Choice: dedicating her career to climate resilience. Outcome: founding the organization.

**Story of Us**: "I know many of you have your own version of that river. Maybe it was a wildfire season that turned your sky orange. Maybe it was a drought that killed your family's farm. Maybe it was watching the news and feeling that knot in your stomach when another disaster hits. We're here because we share a conviction that communities deserve protection, that resilience is a right, not a privilege."

**Story of Now**: "This year, three communities in our region face FEMA flood map changes that will leave 12,000 families without affordable insurance by January. We have nine months to fund and deploy resilience infrastructure in all three. We need $2 million by September. Last year, this community raised $1.4 million. This year, the need is greater and the deadline is real. Will you be part of the 2,000 donors who make this possible?"

Result: The campaign raised $2.3 million — 60% above goal — because the narrative connected personal values to collective identity to urgent, specific action.

### Example 2: Brand Community Launch
A sustainable fashion brand used Public Narrative to launch their community program:

**Story of Self (Founder)**: "I worked in fast fashion for seven years. I saw the factories. I signed the purchase orders that I knew were priced too low for safe working conditions. The moment I quit was the day I visited a factory in Dhaka and met a woman my age doing work I had commissioned, for wages that couldn't feed her family. I couldn't unsee it."

**Story of Us**: "You're reading this because you've had your own version of that moment. Maybe you watched a documentary. Maybe you checked a label and Googled the factory. Maybe you just felt the disconnect between the clothes in your closet and the values in your heart. We're a community of people who decided that what we wear should reflect what we believe."

**Story of Now**: "The industry is at an inflection point. New regulations in the EU are forcing transparency. For the first time, brands can't hide their supply chains. But regulation without consumer demand just creates compliance theater. We need 10,000 consumers to sign the Transparency Pledge this month — showing brands that we'll buy from those who open their doors and walk away from those who don't."

Result: 14,000 pledge signatures in three weeks. The community program became the brand's primary acquisition channel, with pledge signers converting to customers at 5x the rate of paid acquisition.

## When to Use

- Cause marketing and purpose-driven campaigns
- Nonprofit fundraising and donor mobilization
- Community building around a brand or movement
- Internal culture change and organizational transformation
- Political and advocacy campaigns
- Launch campaigns that require collective participation
- Employer branding and talent attraction for mission-driven organizations
- Any context where collective action, not individual purchase, is the goal

## When NOT to Use

- Product marketing with no genuine social dimension (forced purpose feels performative)
- Transactional sales contexts where emotional narrative creates mismatched expectations
- Situations where the "Self" story doesn't exist (no authentic connection to the cause)
- Audiences that are hostile to the values being articulated (Public Narrative assumes shared values exist; it doesn't create them)
- Contexts where urgency is manufactured rather than genuine
- Brand communications where the company's practices contradict the narrative (audiences detect and punish hypocrisy)

## Integration

- **With Ganz's Story of Self / Us / Now**: The individual component frameworks provide detailed guidance for each movement. Public Narrative is the architecture; the component frameworks are the detailed building instructions.
- **With Klaff's Frame Control**: Public Narrative IS a frame — the moral frame. It positions the narrator and audience within a shared moral universe. Frame control principles help maintain this frame against cynicism or distraction.
- **With Klaff's Crocodile Brain**: The Story of Self passes the croc brain filter through emotional, concrete, personal storytelling. The Story of Now creates the urgency the croc brain responds to.
- **With Johnstone's Impro**: The Story of Self benefits from spontaneous, unpolished delivery. Over-rehearsed personal stories lose their emotional truth.
- **With Johnstone's Status Transactions**: Public Narrative involves deliberate status leveling — the narrator lowers themselves through vulnerability (Story of Self) then raises the community (Story of Us) then invites collective action from a position of shared power (Story of Now).

## Agent Routing

Route to the Ganz Public Narrative framework when:
- The user needs to build a movement, community, or collective identity
- The request involves cause marketing or purpose-driven campaigns
- Fundraising or donor mobilization narrative is needed
- The user describes a need to "inspire action," "build a movement," or "rally a community"
- The brief involves connecting personal story to organizational or brand purpose
- The user mentions Marshall Ganz, Public Narrative, or the Obama campaign's storytelling approach
- Internal culture change requires narrative-driven buy-in

Do NOT route here when:
- The need is individual persuasion (use Klaff frameworks)
- The context is product marketing with no genuine social dimension
- The request is for creative technique (use Johnstone frameworks)
- The audience is a single decision-maker, not a community
- There is no authentic connection between the narrator and the cause
