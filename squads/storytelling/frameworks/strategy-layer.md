# Strategy Layer
> Determine which story, for whom, why, where, and when — story selection, audience matching, channel strategy, and narrative prioritization.

## Overview
Discovery tells you what stories are available. Strategy tells you which stories to tell, in what order, to whom, through what channel, and for what purpose. The Strategy Layer is the decision-making engine of the storytelling stack. It transforms raw material from the Discovery Layer into a prioritized narrative plan. Without strategy, storytelling becomes reactive — producing whatever content seems urgent today without considering how it fits into the larger narrative arc. Strategy ensures that every story told serves a specific audience at a specific stage of their journey, through the channel most likely to reach and move them.

## Core Methodology
The Strategy Layer operates through five decision frameworks:

1. **Story Selection** — Which stories from the story bank should be told now? Selection is based on business priority, audience need, and narrative readiness (do we have enough material to tell this story well?).

2. **Audience Matching** — Which audience segment needs this story? A single product can require five different stories for five different audiences. Strategy ensures the right story reaches the right audience.

3. **Channel Strategy** — Where should this story be told? Each channel has different narrative constraints, audience behaviors, and performance expectations. Strategy matches stories to channels based on format fit and audience presence.

4. **Narrative Prioritization** — In what order should stories be deployed? Some stories must precede others to build the necessary context, trust, or awareness. Strategy sequences narratives for maximum cumulative impact.

5. **Timing and Cadence** — When should each story be told? Timing considers market conditions, competitive activity, seasonal patterns, and the audience's readiness to receive the message.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Story Selection
- Review the story bank from the Discovery Layer and categorize by type: brand stories, customer stories, product stories, founder stories, thought leadership stories, proof stories
- Score each story on three dimensions:
  - **Business impact** (1-5): How directly does this story support current business priorities? A case study for the segment you are targeting scores higher than one for a segment you are not.
  - **Audience need** (1-5): How strongly does the target audience need this story? Stories that address the top objection score higher than stories that address a minor concern.
  - **Narrative readiness** (1-5): Do you have enough material to tell this story well? A customer who gave a detailed interview scores higher than one who provided a single quote.
- Multiply the three scores. Stories scoring 60+ are immediate priorities. Stories scoring 30-59 go into the pipeline. Stories below 30 are archived for future consideration.
- Select 5-8 priority stories per quarter. More than 8 dilutes focus and quality.

### Step 2: Audience Matching
- Map each selected story to its primary audience segment
- Define the audience in behavioral terms, not just demographics: "Marketing directors at B2B SaaS companies with 50-200 employees who are currently evaluating analytics tools" is more useful than "Marketing leaders"
- For each story-audience pair, answer:
  - What does this audience currently believe? (from the belief map)
  - What do we want them to believe after this story?
  - What action do we want them to take?
  - What objection does this story address?
- If a story serves multiple audiences, create audience-specific versions — do not try to serve everyone with one version. The same customer story might lead with ROI for a CFO audience and lead with ease of use for an end-user audience.

### Step 3: Channel Strategy
- For each story-audience pair, identify the primary and secondary channels:
  - **Email**: Best for serialized narratives, personalized stories, and direct CTAs. Audience owns the relationship.
  - **Blog/Website**: Best for long-form stories, SEO-driven discovery, and evergreen proof. The audience finds you.
  - **Social media**: Best for micro-stories, social proof, and real-time engagement. The audience encounters you.
  - **Video**: Best for emotional stories, demonstrations, and personality-driven content. The audience experiences you.
  - **Paid ads**: Best for high-reach story distribution to targeted segments. The audience is interrupted by you.
  - **Sales enablement**: Best for one-to-one proof stories tailored to specific prospect situations.
  - **PR/Media**: Best for credibility stories and third-party validation.
- Match stories to channels based on format fit: data-heavy stories work better in blogs and presentations; emotional stories work better in video and social.
- Map audience presence to channels: where does this specific segment spend their attention?

### Step 4: Narrative Prioritization and Sequencing
- Not all stories can launch simultaneously. Sequence them for maximum cumulative impact.
- **Foundation stories first**: Brand story, founder story, and core product narrative should be established before campaign-level stories
- **Awareness before proof**: Introduce the problem before presenting the solution. Market education precedes product marketing.
- **Broad before narrow**: Start with stories that reach the widest relevant audience, then narrow to segment-specific stories
- **Proof follows claims**: After a bold claim (thought leadership, product promise), immediately follow with proof (case study, data story)
- Create a narrative calendar that shows which stories launch when and how they build on each other
- Identify dependencies: Story B requires Story A as context. Story C requires the proof from Story B.

### Step 5: Timing and Cadence
- **Market timing**: Align stories with industry events, seasonal patterns, budget cycles, and competitive activity
- **Launch timing**: Coordinate story releases with product launches, feature updates, or company milestones
- **Cadence by channel**:
  - Email: 1-2 story-driven emails per week during campaigns, 1 per week during maintenance
  - Blog: 1-2 story-driven posts per week
  - Social: 3-5 story posts per week (mix of micro-stories, proof, and engagement)
  - Video: 1-2 per month (higher production effort requires lower frequency)
  - Ads: Refresh creative every 2-4 weeks to prevent fatigue
- Build buffer: plan stories 4-6 weeks ahead to allow for proper development through the Structure, Drafting, and Editing layers

### Step 6: Strategy Documentation and Review
- Produce a quarterly story strategy document that includes:
  - Priority stories with scores and rationale
  - Audience-story-channel maps
  - Narrative calendar with sequencing and dependencies
  - Success metrics for each story (what does "this story worked" look like?)
  - Resource allocation: who develops each story, with what budget and timeline
- Review monthly: What stories are performing? What stories are not? What new stories have emerged from customer conversations or market changes?
- Adapt quarterly: The strategy should evolve based on performance data from the Measurement Layer

## Examples

### Example 1: Quarterly Story Strategy for B2B SaaS
- **Priority Stories**: (1) New case study from enterprise customer in healthcare vertical. (2) Thought leadership piece challenging conventional wisdom about marketing attribution. (3) Product narrative update for new analytics dashboard feature. (4) Founder story refresh for upcoming conference season. (5) Customer micro-stories for social proof campaign.
- **Audience Matching**: Healthcare case study → VP Marketing at health systems. Attribution thought leadership → CMOs and marketing directors. Dashboard narrative → current customers and mid-funnel prospects. Founder story → conference attendees and LinkedIn followers. Micro-stories → broad awareness audience.
- **Channel Strategy**: Case study → blog + email + sales enablement. Thought leadership → LinkedIn article + conference keynote + social thread. Dashboard → product email + blog + in-app notification. Founder → LinkedIn + conference + YouTube. Micro-stories → social (all platforms).
- **Sequencing**: Week 1-2: Thought leadership launches (establishes authority). Week 3-4: Healthcare case study (provides proof). Week 5-6: Dashboard narrative (product-level storytelling). Week 7-8: Founder story (conference timing). Micro-stories: continuous throughout.

### Example 2: Launch Strategy for New Service Offering
- **Priority Stories**: (1) Problem awareness — the market gap this service addresses. (2) Founder insight — why we decided to build this. (3) Beta customer result — first proof of concept. (4) Full case study — comprehensive proof from pilot program. (5) Customer testimonials — social proof across segments.
- **Sequencing**: Weeks 1-2 (anticipation): Problem awareness stories across blog and social. Week 3 (pre-launch): Founder insight via email and LinkedIn. Week 4 (launch): Full reveal with beta customer result. Weeks 5-6 (proof): Case study and testimonials. Weeks 7-8 (urgency): Deadline-driven campaign.

## When to Use
- At the beginning of every quarter to plan narrative priorities
- Before any major campaign, launch, or initiative
- When multiple stories are competing for resources and you need to prioritize
- When current storytelling feels scattered or reactive
- When entering a new market segment and need a narrative entry strategy

### Strategy Pitfalls
- **Strategy without discovery**: Making story selection decisions based on internal preferences rather than audience research. The CEO's favorite customer story may not be the most effective story for the target audience.
- **Over-planning, under-executing**: Spending weeks on a perfect strategy document while competitors publish daily. Strategy should take days, not months. A good strategy executed quickly beats a perfect strategy executed slowly.
- **Channel obsession**: Choosing channels based on where the company wants to be present rather than where the audience actually spends time. If your audience is not on TikTok, a TikTok strategy is a waste.
- **Narrative fragmentation**: Assigning stories to so many channels that no single channel gets enough content to build narrative momentum. Better to dominate one channel than to be invisible on five.
- **Ignoring measurement feedback**: Planning the next quarter's strategy without reviewing the previous quarter's performance data. Every strategy cycle should begin with a retrospective.

## When NOT to Use
- When you have not completed the Discovery Layer — strategy without discovery is assumptions dressed as plans
- When the task is a single, standalone story that does not require strategic context
- When rapid response is needed (crisis communication, trending topic) — act first, strategize later
- When the team lacks the resources to execute on the strategy — a plan without capacity is a wish list

## Integration
- Receives inputs from the **Discovery Layer**: story bank, belief maps, objection catalog, competitive landscape
- Outputs to the **Structure Layer**: which stories to build and in what format
- Coordinates with the **Delivery Layer**: channel specifications and format requirements
- Informed by the **Measurement Layer**: performance data from previous story campaigns
- Aligns with every application framework to determine which framework applies to which story

## Agent Routing
- **Strategy Agent**: Owns the quarterly planning process, scoring, and sequencing
- **Research Agent**: Provides updated Discovery Layer inputs for strategy decisions
- **Channel Agent**: Advises on platform-specific opportunities and constraints
- **Calendar Agent**: Manages the narrative calendar and identifies scheduling conflicts or dependencies
- Route to this layer when making decisions about which stories to tell, when, where, and to whom — before any content creation begins
