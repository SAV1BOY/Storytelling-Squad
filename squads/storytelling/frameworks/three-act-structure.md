# Three-Act Structure
> The foundational narrative architecture that divides any story into Setup, Confrontation, and Resolution.

## Overview

The Three-Act Structure is the oldest and most universal story framework in existence. Its roots trace to Aristotle's *Poetics* (335 BCE), where he argued that every complete story has a beginning, middle, and end. Syd Field codified the modern version in *Screenplay* (1979), establishing the proportional breakdown that Hollywood and marketers alike rely on today.

Why it matters for storytelling squads: every narrative task—whether a 30-second ad, a sales page, or a keynote—benefits from this skeleton. It provides the minimum viable architecture for audience engagement. Without it, content drifts into exposition without tension, or tension without payoff.

The structure is not a formula. It is a map of how human attention naturally processes change over time. Audiences unconsciously expect a setup, a complication, and a resolution. Violating this expectation without deliberate craft results in disengagement.

## Core Methodology

The three acts follow a 25% / 50% / 25% proportion by default:

1. **Act I — Setup (25%)**: Establish the world, introduce the protagonist (or customer avatar), define the status quo, and plant the Inciting Incident that disrupts equilibrium.
2. **Act II — Confrontation (50%)**: The protagonist pursues a goal against escalating obstacles. This is the engine room of tension. It contains the midpoint shift and the "all is lost" moment.
3. **Act III — Resolution (25%)**: The climax resolves the central tension. The new equilibrium is established. The call to action lives here.

### Key Structural Beats

- **Opening Image**: The first impression that sets tone and context.
- **Inciting Incident**: The event that forces the protagonist out of the status quo. Occurs at the boundary of Act I.
- **First Plot Point**: The protagonist commits to the journey. No turning back.
- **Midpoint**: A shift in the protagonist's approach—from reactive to proactive, or a major revelation that reframes the conflict.
- **All Is Lost / Dark Night**: The lowest point. Everything seems impossible.
- **Climax**: The decisive confrontation with the central obstacle.
- **Resolution / New Normal**: The transformed state. In marketing, this is the world after the product, service, or idea has been adopted.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Define the Protagonist and Their Status Quo (Act I)
- Identify who the story is about. In marketing, this is almost always the customer, not the brand.
- Describe the "before" state with specificity. Generic pain points create generic stories. Use concrete details: the 3 AM alarm, the spreadsheet with 47 tabs, the missed dinner.
- Substep: Establish at least one sympathetic trait so the audience roots for this character.
- Substep: Ground the setting in a recognizable context the target audience shares.

### Step 2: Introduce the Inciting Incident (Act I → Act II Transition)
- This is the disruption that makes the status quo untenable. In marketing, it could be a market shift, a personal breaking point, or a discovery.
- The inciting incident must be specific and concrete, not abstract. "Revenue was declining" is weak. "The biggest client called on a Tuesday afternoon and said they were leaving" is strong.
- Substep: The incident should create a question in the audience's mind—"What will they do now?"

### Step 3: Escalate the Confrontation (Act II, First Half)
- The protagonist takes initial action but faces obstacles. Each obstacle should be harder than the last.
- Substep: Introduce at least two failed attempts or complications before any progress.
- Substep: Use the "yes, but" principle—every small win should come with a new complication.

### Step 4: Deliver the Midpoint Shift (Act II, Center)
- Something changes the protagonist's understanding of the problem. New information, a mentor's insight, or a realization.
- In marketing, this is often where the product or methodology is introduced—not as a sales pitch, but as a genuine turning point in the character's journey.
- Substep: The midpoint should feel like a revelation, not an advertisement.

### Step 5: Push to the "All Is Lost" Moment (Act II, Second Half)
- Even with the new approach, things get worse before they get better. This is critical for credibility.
- Substep: The "all is lost" moment should challenge the core belief the story is trying to establish. If the story argues that "simplicity wins," the darkest moment should be when complexity seems inevitable.

### Step 6: Build the Climax (Act III, Opening)
- The protagonist applies everything learned to face the central challenge decisively.
- Substep: The climax should feel both surprising and inevitable—surprising in its specific execution, inevitable in its thematic logic.
- In marketing, this is the transformation moment. The result, the breakthrough, the outcome.

### Step 7: Resolve and Establish the New Normal (Act III, Close)
- Show the "after" state in concrete detail that mirrors the "before" state from Act I.
- Substep: The resolution should answer the question raised by the inciting incident.
- Substep: Include the call to action as a natural extension of the story's logic, not a bolted-on afterthought.

### Step 8: Audit the Proportions
- Count your words, slides, or seconds. If Act I exceeds 30%, you are over-explaining. If Act II is under 40%, you lack tension. If Act III is over 30%, you are over-selling.

## Examples

### Example 1: SaaS Case Study (Blog Post)
- **Act I (Setup)**: "When DataFlow hit 50 employees, their onboarding process broke. New hires spent their first two weeks lost in a maze of Notion docs, Slack channels, and outdated wikis. Three senior engineers quit in Q2, citing 'chaos' in their exit interviews."
- **Act II (Confrontation)**: They tried building an internal tool (failed—took 4 months and still didn't work). They tried hiring a dedicated onboarding manager (helped, but didn't scale). Then the CTO discovered structured knowledge-base software. But adoption was rocky—engineers resisted another tool. The team had to rethink their entire documentation culture.
- **Act III (Resolution)**: "Six months later, new-hire ramp time dropped from 14 days to 3. The engineers who almost left became the system's biggest advocates. DataFlow grew to 120 employees without adding a single onboarding headcount."

### Example 2: Personal Brand Keynote
- **Act I**: Speaker describes their life as a corporate consultant—successful on paper, empty underneath. The inciting incident: a client asks, "Do you even believe in what you're selling?"
- **Act II**: Speaker quits, tries to start a business, fails twice. Burns through savings. Spouse is worried. Discovers a methodology through a chance meeting. Tests it, gets early traction, then hits a wall when a major partnership falls through.
- **Act III**: Speaker refines the approach, lands a breakthrough client, and builds the business that now serves the audience in the room. Ends with: "The question my client asked me is the same one I want to ask you tonight."

## When to Use

- Any narrative under 2,000 words or 10 minutes where simplicity is an advantage.
- Sales pages, landing pages, pitch decks, short video scripts.
- Case studies, testimonials, and customer success stories.
- When the audience is unfamiliar with the brand and needs a clear, linear journey.
- When time is limited and you need the simplest effective structure.

## When NOT to Use

- Long-form content (webinars, books, documentary-length video) where three acts feel too compressed. Use Five-Act Structure instead.
- Content that is purely instructional or informational with no narrative element.
- When the audience already knows the story and you need to subvert expectations—consider starting in Act II (In Medias Res opening).
- Listicle or modular content formats where non-linear structure works better.

## Integration

- **Story Beats and Scenes**: Use beat analysis to ensure each act contains the right number of value shifts.
- **Tension-Release Loop**: Layer tension-release cycles within Act II to maintain engagement across the longest section.
- **Stakes Escalation Ladder**: Map escalating stakes to the three acts—personal stakes in Act I, relational in Act II, universal in Act III.
- **Emotional Arc Mapping**: Overlay the emotional arc to verify that the energy of each act matches the intended audience experience.
- **Story Opening Patterns**: Select the Act I opening pattern from the Story Opening Patterns framework.
- **Story Closing Patterns**: Select the Act III closing pattern from the Story Closing Patterns framework.
- **But-Therefore Causality**: Audit every transition between beats to ensure causal connection.

## Agent Routing

- **Primary Owner**: Story Architect Agent — responsible for selecting and applying the three-act structure as the foundational architecture for any narrative task.
- **Assisting Agents**:
  - Character Developer Agent — builds out the protagonist and supporting characters for Act I setup.
  - Tension & Conflict Agent — designs the escalation pattern for Act II.
  - Editor Agent — audits proportions and trims excess from any act that runs long.
  - CTA Strategist Agent — ensures Act III resolution flows naturally into the desired audience action.
