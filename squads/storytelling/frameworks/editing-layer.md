# Editing Layer
> Cut, compress, clarify, and strengthen stories through disciplined subtraction — "kill your darlings."

## Overview
Editing is where good stories become great. The Drafting Layer produces raw material — alive with energy but also riddled with excess, unclear passages, weak transitions, and self-indulgent detours. The Editing Layer applies discipline: cutting what does not serve the narrative, compressing what takes too long, clarifying what confuses, and strengthening what almost works. William Faulkner's famous advice — "kill your darlings" — is the soul of this layer. Every sentence must earn its place. Every paragraph must advance the story. Every word must carry weight. Editing is not about making content shorter; it is about making content stronger.

## Core Methodology
The Editing Layer operates through four passes, each targeting a different level of the narrative:

1. **Structural Edit (Macro)** — Does the story arc work? Are the beats in the right order? Does tension build? Is the payoff earned? Does the piece achieve its strategic purpose? This pass may require rewriting sections or reorganizing the structure.

2. **Narrative Edit (Mid-Level)** — Does each section work? Are scenes vivid? Is dialogue authentic? Are transitions smooth? Does proof land at the right moment? This pass refines the storytelling craft.

3. **Line Edit (Micro)** — Is each sentence clear, concise, and purposeful? Are there filler words, passive constructions, jargon, or cliches? This pass polishes the prose.

4. **Proof and Fact Check** — Are all claims accurate? Are quotes correctly attributed? Are metrics verifiable? Are names, titles, and companies correct? This pass ensures credibility.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Structural Edit — The Story Arc
- Read the entire draft without making any changes. Note your emotional response at each point: engaged, bored, confused, skeptical, moved.
- Check against the structure document: Does the draft follow the planned beats? Where did it deviate, and was the deviation an improvement or a drift?
- Evaluate the opening: Does it earn the reader's attention in the first 2-3 sentences? If not, find the most compelling moment in the draft and consider moving it to the opening.
- Evaluate tension: Does the story build tension progressively? Identify any sections where tension drops unnecessarily.
- Evaluate the turning point: Is the moment of change clear and dramatic? Is it placed at approximately the two-thirds mark of the narrative?
- Evaluate the resolution: Is it satisfying? Does it connect back to the opening? Does it lead naturally to the CTA?
- Evaluate proof placement: Does proof appear at moments of peak skepticism? Is there enough proof to be convincing?
- Structural edits may require significant rewriting. This is normal and expected.

### Step 2: Narrative Edit — The Craft
- Read each section individually. For each section, ask: "Is this a scene or a summary? Should it be the other?"
- **Strengthen scenes**: Add specific details where scenes feel generic. Add dialogue where scenes feel distant. Add sensory details where scenes feel abstract.
- **Compress summaries**: If a section summarizes when it should narrate, either convert it to a scene or cut it to its essential information.
- **Fix transitions**: Every beat should connect to the next through a bridge sentence or paragraph. If you feel a "bump" between sections, the transition needs work.
- **Check dialogue**: Read all quotes aloud. Do they sound like a human speaking? Remove any quotes that sound like marketing copy. If a quote does not add narrative value, cut it.
- **Verify emotional progression**: The reader should feel increasingly invested as the story progresses. If you feel the story plateau at any point, inject a new tension, a surprising detail, or a proof element to re-engage.

### Step 3: Line Edit — The Prose
- **Cut filler words**: Remove "very," "really," "just," "actually," "basically," "literally," and "in order to" on every pass. These words add length without adding meaning.
- **Activate passive voice**: Change "The report was created by the team" to "The team created the report." Passive voice distances the reader from the action.
- **Eliminate throat-clearing**: The first paragraph of many drafts is the writer warming up. The real opening is often in paragraph 2 or 3. Cut the warm-up.
- **Kill redundancy**: If you say the same thing twice in different words, keep the stronger version and cut the other. If two examples make the same point, keep the better one.
- **Replace jargon**: Every industry term should be replaced with plain language unless the audience is expert-level. "Optimize the customer journey" becomes "make it easier for customers to buy."
- **Shorten sentences at key moments**: Short sentences create impact. At the turning point, the proof reveal, and the CTA — use short sentences. Long sentences are fine for context and exploration.
- **Read the draft aloud**: Your ear catches what your eye misses. If you stumble over a sentence while reading aloud, the reader will stumble too. Rewrite it.
- Target: cut 15-25% of the word count from the first draft without losing any meaning.

### Step 4: Proof and Fact Check
- Verify every quantitative claim: percentages, dollar amounts, timeframes, user counts
- Confirm every quote is accurately attributed and has not been altered beyond removing verbal tics
- Check every name, title, and company name for spelling and accuracy
- Verify that any referenced case studies, research, or third-party sources are correctly cited
- Confirm that no confidential information has been included without approval
- For customer stories: verify the customer has approved the final version for publication
- For data stories: verify the data source, the methodology, and the interpretation

### Step 5: Consistency Check
- **Voice consistency**: Does the piece sound like the same narrator throughout? Watch for sections that shift tone (often because they were written at different times or by different people).
- **Narrative consistency**: Does the proof support the claims? Do the examples align with the argument? Are there contradictions between sections?
- **Brand consistency**: Does the piece align with the brand narrative established in the Brand Story Framework? Are promises, enemy definitions, and proof consistent with other published content?
- **Format consistency**: Are headers, subheaders, pull quotes, and CTAs formatted consistently? Are visual styles consistent?

### Step 6: Final Read — The Reader's Experience
- Read the entire edited piece one final time, as a reader, not an editor
- Ask: Would I keep reading if I were the target audience? At every point?
- Ask: Am I convinced by the argument? Where am I still skeptical?
- Ask: Do I know exactly what to do next? Is the CTA clear?
- Ask: Would I share this? What would I say about it to a colleague?
- If the answers are not satisfying, identify the weakest section and apply one more round of editing to that section only
- The piece is ready when you can read it straight through without wanting to change anything

## Examples

### Example 1: Structural Edit Intervention
- **Draft problem**: The case study opens with three paragraphs about the company's history and industry before any tension appears. The reader has no reason to care.
- **Edit**: Move the most compelling quote from paragraph 6 to the opening: "'I was spending more time managing the tool than the tool was saving me.' That is how David Chen described his first six months with his previous analytics platform." Then provide context. Then build tension.
- **Result**: The reader is immediately engaged because they recognize the frustration.

### Example 2: Line Edit Example
- **Before**: "In order to really truly understand the full extent of the problem that the team was actually facing on a daily basis, it is important to note that there were basically a number of different factors that were contributing to the overall situation."
- **After**: "Three factors created the problem."
- **Words cut**: 38 → 5. Meaning preserved. Impact increased.

### Example 3: Narrative Edit — Dialogue
- **Before**: "The customer stated that the product had a significant positive impact on their team's ability to execute projects within established timeframes."
- **After**: "'We stopped missing deadlines,' Sarah said. 'That had not happened in two years.'"
- **Improvement**: The quote is now human, specific, and emotionally resonant.

## When to Use
- After every first draft before publication
- When an existing piece of content is underperforming and needs improvement
- When adapting content from one format to another (the adapted version needs editing for the new format)
- When combining contributions from multiple writers into a cohesive piece
- When a piece has been through multiple revisions and needs a final polish

### Editing Pitfalls
- **Over-editing**: Polishing until the voice is gone. Editing should sharpen, not sanitize. If the edited version sounds like a committee wrote it, you have gone too far.
- **Editing for length, not impact**: Cutting to hit a word count rather than cutting to strengthen the narrative. Some pieces need to be longer because the story demands it. Cut what is weak, not what is long.
- **Ignoring the arc**: Making line-level improvements while missing a structural problem. Always do the structural pass first. Perfect sentences in a broken arc are wasted effort.
- **The committee review**: Routing the draft through too many reviewers. Each reviewer adds their preferences, and the piece becomes a compromise that satisfies no one. Limit review to 2-3 people maximum.
- **Skipping the read-aloud**: Relying on silent reading to catch problems. Reading aloud catches awkward phrasing, run-on sentences, and tonal inconsistencies that the eye misses.
- **Losing the customer's voice**: Cleaning up customer quotes until they sound like marketing copy. Authenticity lives in imperfect language. Preserve the customer's personality.

## When NOT to Use
- During the drafting process — editing while drafting kills momentum and produces neither a good draft nor a good edit
- For content that is intentionally rough or informal (live social posts, casual internal messages)
- When the structural problems are so severe that editing cannot fix them — return to the Structure Layer instead
- When the piece needs a complete rewrite rather than refinement — send it back to the Drafting Layer

- Outputs publication-ready content to the **Delivery Layer**

## Agent Routing
- **Editing Agent**: Performs all four editing passes (structural, narrative, line, proof)
- **Fact-Check Agent**: Verifies quantitative claims, quotes, and attributions
- **Voice Agent**: Ensures consistency with brand voice standards
- **Quality Agent**: Performs the final read and approves for publication
- Route to this layer after a first draft is complete and before any content enters production or publication
