# Ganz Story of Us
> The shared story of community — what values, experiences, and hopes bind "us" together as a collective identity.

## Overview

Story of Us is the second movement of Marshall Ganz's Public Narrative framework. If Story of Self establishes the narrator's moral authority ("why I care"), Story of Us establishes collective identity ("why WE care"). The shift from "I" to "we" is the shift from personal testimony to community building — and it is where individual commitment becomes collective power.

Story of Us answers the question: What makes us a "we"? What shared values, experiences, and aspirations bind this group of individuals into a community capable of acting together? Without a Story of Us, you have an audience of individuals who may each privately agree with the narrator. With a Story of Us, you have a community that recognizes itself and its shared capacity for action.

Ganz draws a critical distinction between aggregation and community. An aggregation is a collection of individuals who happen to share a characteristic (customers of a brand, employees of a company, residents of a city). A community is a group that recognizes shared values, shared history, and shared responsibility for each other. Aggregations consume. Communities act.

Story of Us is what transforms an aggregation into a community. It names the shared values, makes the shared history visible, and creates the collective identity that enables coordinated action. This is why it is essential for brand communities, movements, organizational culture, employer branding, and any context where collective identity drives behavior.

## Core Methodology

### Shared Values as the Foundation

A community is not defined by shared demographics, shared purchases, or shared geography. It is defined by shared values — the principles that members hold deeply enough to act on even when acting is costly.

Identifying shared values requires looking beyond surface commonalities to the underlying commitments:
- A community of runners is not defined by the act of running but by shared values of discipline, personal growth, and the belief that limits are meant to be tested
- A community of open-source developers is not defined by coding but by shared values of transparency, collaboration, and the belief that knowledge should be free
- A brand community is not defined by product usage but by shared values the product represents

The Story of Us must name these values explicitly — not as marketing language but as lived commitments. "We believe in sustainability" is a slogan. "We're the people who check labels, who choose inconvenience over compromise, who believe what we buy is a vote for the world we want" is a Story of Us.

### Shared Experience as Evidence

Values become real through shared experience. The Story of Us draws on experiences the community has had together — or experiences so common among members that they function as shared even when lived individually.

**Types of shared experience**:

*Founding moments*: The event or experience that brought the community together. For a startup, it might be the first product launch. For a movement, the first protest. For a brand community, the experience that made early adopters realize they were part of something.

*Challenges overcome together*: Moments of collective difficulty that the community survived or transcended. These are bonding experiences that prove the community's strength.

*Rituals and traditions*: Repeated practices that reinforce identity. The annual conference. The weekly check-in. The shared vocabulary. The inside jokes. These seem trivial but they are the connective tissue of community.

*Shared enemies or obstacles*: Communities are often defined as much by what they oppose as by what they support. A shared adversary — an industry practice, a social problem, a common frustration — creates solidarity.

*Shared heroes and stories*: The stories the community tells about itself. The founding myth. The member who went above and beyond. The moment that defined who "we" are.

### The "We" That Includes, Not Excludes

Ganz emphasizes that Story of Us must be inclusive within its defined community. It should draw the circle as widely as possible around the shared values, not as narrowly as possible around demographics or identity markers.

The question is not "Who is like us?" but "Who shares these values?" This is particularly important for brand communities, where the temptation is to define "us" narrowly (our power users, our biggest fans) rather than broadly (everyone who cares about what we care about).

However, a "we" that includes everyone means nothing. The Story of Us must have boundaries — values that define who is in and who is out. The key is that these boundaries are based on values and choices, not on identity characteristics. "We are the people who choose X over Y" is an inclusive boundary (anyone can make that choice). "We are people who look like Z" is an exclusive boundary that limits the community's moral power.

### Challenge-Choice-Outcome at the Collective Level

Like Story of Self, Story of Us follows the challenge-choice-outcome structure, but at the collective level:

**Challenge**: What shared challenge has this community faced? What tested our values? What forced us to decide who we were?

**Choice**: How did we respond as a community? What did we do together that revealed our shared values? What collective choices define us?

**Outcome**: What happened as a result? What did we build, achieve, or learn together? What did our collective action make possible?

This structure is essential because it transforms passive identity ("we are a group of people who like X") into active identity ("we are a group of people who chose X when it was hard, and here's what happened").

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Define the Community
Who is the "we" you are addressing? Be specific:
- Is it your customers? Which customers — all of them, or a specific segment?
- Is it your employees? Your team? Your industry peers?
- Is it a social or political community? Defined by what shared commitment?
- Is it a brand community? Defined by what shared values beyond product usage?

### Step 2: Identify Shared Values
For the defined community, identify 2-3 core shared values. These should be:
- Actually shared (not aspirational values you wish they had)
- Deep enough to motivate action (not surface preferences)
- Connected to the Story of Self values (the narrator's values must resonate with the community's values)
- Expressible through story (abstract values must have concrete manifestations)

Methods: Community surveys, customer interviews, analysis of community-generated content, observation of what community members celebrate and what they reject.

### Step 3: Gather Shared Experience Evidence
Collect the stories, moments, and experiences that demonstrate these values in action at the community level:
- What do community members talk about when they talk about why they're part of this group?
- What moments do they reference as defining experiences?
- What challenges has the community faced together?
- What are the community's rituals, traditions, and shared reference points?

### Step 4: Craft the Story of Us
Structure the narrative:
1. **Name the shared value** (through demonstration, not declaration): "We're the kind of people who..."
2. **Provide evidence through shared experience**: "Remember when we..." or "Every one of us has had the experience of..."
3. **Apply challenge-choice-outcome**: "When [shared challenge], we chose [shared response], and it showed us [shared lesson]"
4. **Affirm the collective identity**: "That's who we are. That's what holds us together."

### Step 5: Bridge from Self to Us
The transition from Story of Self to Story of Us must feel organic:
- "I learned this about myself, and when I looked around, I realized I wasn't alone."
- "My experience is not unique. Many of you have faced your own version of this."
- "What brought me here is the same thing that brought you here."

### Step 6: Test with Community Members
Share the Story of Us with actual community members and ask:
- "Does this feel true to your experience?"
- "Would you say this describes who we are?"
- "Is there anything missing?"

If the community recognizes itself in the story, it's working. If they don't, the story is based on the narrator's projection rather than the community's reality.

## Examples

### Example 1: Developer Tool Community
A developer tools company needed to transform their user base into an active community:

**Shared Values Identified**: Craftsmanship (caring about code quality, not just shipping), generosity (helping other developers solve problems), and pragmatism (choosing tools that work over tools that are trendy).

**Story of Us**: "We're the developers who care about the code other developers will maintain after us. We write tests not because we're told to but because we remember what it felt like to inherit untested code at 2 AM on a production issue. We help strangers on Stack Overflow because someone once helped us. We chose this tool not because it was the flashiest option but because it was the one that actually worked when the stakes were high.

Last year, when [major security vulnerability] hit, this community patched and documented the fix before the vendor even acknowledged the problem. 400 pull requests in 48 hours. Not because anyone asked. Because that's what we do — we fix things.

That's the community you're part of. Not users. Not customers. Builders who care about what they build."

Result: Community engagement metrics increased 4x. Organic contribution (documentation, plugins, tutorials) tripled. Support ticket volume decreased as community self-service improved.

### Example 2: Sustainable Consumer Brand
A sustainable household products brand used Story of Us to launch their community program:

**Shared Values Identified**: Stewardship (leaving things better than we found them), skepticism (questioning marketing claims and demanding proof), and practical optimism (believing individual choices matter without being naive about systemic issues).

**Story of Us**: "We're the people who read the ingredients list. Not because we're paranoid — because we believe we deserve to know what's in the products that touch our families. We chose to pay more for less packaging. We chose inconvenience over ignorance. Every one of us has had that moment in a store aisle where we put something back because the label didn't add up.

When we launched the transparency database last year, 12,000 of you uploaded product photos and ingredient lists in the first month. You didn't do that for a discount code. You did it because information should be free, and companies that hide ingredients don't deserve our money.

We're not a customer base. We're a quality standard. And we're raising the bar for every brand that wants our business."

Result: The community self-identified with the narrative so strongly that "we read labels" became an organic social media tagline, generating user-created content that outperformed paid campaigns.

### Example 3: Internal Company Culture Narrative
A mid-stage startup used Story of Us to articulate culture during a period of rapid hiring:

**Story of Us**: "We're the people who joined a company with 30 employees, a product that half-worked, and a mission that fully mattered. We chose uncertainty over stability because we believed that [problem] deserved a better solution and we could build it.

When we lost our biggest customer last Q3, nobody pointed fingers. Fifteen people voluntarily stayed late for three weeks to rebuild the integration. Not because anyone mandated it, but because this team doesn't ship work we're not proud of.

If you're new here, that's the culture you've joined. We hire for conviction. We promote for ownership. We celebrate the person who says 'this isn't good enough' more than the person who says 'this is fine.' Welcome to the team that cares too much."

Result: New hire ramp time improved, cultural alignment in 90-day reviews increased, and the narrative became a touchstone referenced in team meetings and retrospectives.

## When to Use

- Community building around a brand, product, or cause
- Brand narrative where collective identity matters more than individual benefit
- Employer branding and internal culture articulation
- Movement building for social causes or industry change
- Customer community programs (loyalty, advocacy, user groups)
- Fundraising where donor identity drives giving
- Coalition building where diverse groups need to recognize shared purpose
- Onboarding new members into an existing community or organization

## When NOT to Use

- Individual persuasion contexts (pitches to a single decision-maker)
- When no genuine community exists yet (Story of Us reflects existing identity; it doesn't create identity from nothing)
- Product marketing focused on individual benefit rather than collective identity
- When the "shared values" are actually the brand's values projected onto the audience
- Contexts where the audience is too diverse for meaningful shared identity
- When the narrator is not a genuine member of the community being addressed (outsiders telling a community's story feels appropriative)

## Integration

- **With Ganz's Public Narrative**: Story of Us is the second movement. It bridges the personal (Story of Self) and the active (Story of Now).
- **With Ganz's Story of Self**: The values revealed in Story of Self must be the same values that define the Story of Us community. The narrator's individual story becomes the entry point to the collective story.
- **With Ganz's Story of Now**: Story of Us creates the collective identity that Story of Now mobilizes. Without a strong "we," the call to action has no subject.
- **With Ganz's Values and Action**: Story of Us is where values become shared — the prerequisite for collective emotion and collective action.
- **With Johnstone's Status Transactions**: Story of Us involves status leveling — the narrator descends from the elevated position of Story of Self to join the community as an equal.
- **With Klaff's Frame Control**: The "we" frame is one of the strongest frames available. Once established, it is difficult for outside forces to break because it is distributed across a community rather than residing in a single person.

## Agent Routing

Route to the Ganz Story of Us framework when:
- The user needs to build or articulate community identity
- The request involves "we" messaging for brands, organizations, or movements
- Customer community programs need narrative infrastructure
- Employer branding or internal culture requires articulation
- The user describes a need to "bring people together" or "create belonging"
- The audience is a community, not an individual decision-maker
- The user asks about collective identity, shared values, or community storytelling

Do NOT route here when:
- The need is for the full Public Narrative architecture (use the complete framework)
- The focus is on personal storytelling (use Story of Self)
- The request is about urgency and calls to action (use Story of Now)
- The context is individual persuasion (use Klaff frameworks)
- No genuine community exists to address
