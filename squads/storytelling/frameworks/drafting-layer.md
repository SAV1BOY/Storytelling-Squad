# Drafting Layer
> Write the first version — voice, dialogue, sensory details, scenes, and the craft of turning structure into prose.

## Overview
Drafting is where structure becomes story. The beat sheet from the Structure Layer provides the skeleton; the Drafting Layer adds muscle, skin, and breath. This is the craft phase — where voice is established, scenes are rendered, dialogue is written, sensory details are placed, and the abstract architecture of narrative becomes the concrete experience of reading, watching, or listening. The Drafting Layer operates on a single essential principle: the first draft exists to be written, not to be perfect. Speed and completeness matter more than polish. The Editing Layer handles refinement. The drafter's job is to get the story out of the structure document and into words.

## Core Methodology
The Drafting Layer operates through five craft dimensions that together produce a compelling first draft:

1. **Voice** — The personality of the narrative. Voice determines whether the story sounds like a trusted friend, a wise mentor, a passionate founder, or a credentialed expert. Voice must be consistent within a piece and across a brand's content.

2. **Scenes** — The building blocks of narrative. A scene is a specific moment in time and space where something happens. Scenes replace abstractions with experience. "We had communication problems" is an abstraction. "It was 6 AM and the Slack channel had 47 unread messages, all from the same thread" is a scene.

3. **Dialogue** — The words people actually said. Dialogue is the fastest way to create intimacy and authenticity. A customer's exact quote carries more emotional weight than any amount of description.

4. **Sensory Detail** — Concrete, specific details that activate the reader's imagination. Not "the office was busy" but "the printer was jammed, two phones were ringing, and someone had burned microwave popcorn." Sensory details make stories feel real.

5. **Pacing** — The rhythm of the narrative. Short sentences create urgency. Long sentences create reflection. Paragraph breaks create emphasis. Pacing controls how the reader moves through the story and where they pause.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Establish Voice Before Writing
- Review the brand's voice guidelines if they exist. If they do not, define voice along four dimensions:
  - **Tone**: Formal ↔ Casual. Where on this spectrum does the brand sit?
  - **Authority**: Expert ↔ Peer. Does the brand speak from above or alongside the audience?
  - **Emotion**: Reserved ↔ Passionate. How much feeling does the brand express?
  - **Humor**: Serious ↔ Playful. Is humor appropriate for this audience and topic?
- Write three sentences in the target voice before beginning the draft. This calibrates your internal voice setting.
- For customer stories, the voice should lean toward the customer's tone, not the brand's. If the customer is casual and direct, the case study should feel casual and direct.
- Voice should flex with channel: LinkedIn allows more formality, Twitter demands more personality, email can be more intimate.

### Step 2: Draft the Opening Scene
- The opening is the most important paragraph. It earns or loses the reader in seconds.
- **Scene opening**: Drop the reader into a specific moment. "Maria was staring at a spreadsheet at 11 PM on a Tuesday, and she was fairly certain that the numbers were lying to her."
- **Contrast opening**: Present two realities side by side. "Three months ago, this team was working 60-hour weeks and missing deadlines. Today, they leave at 5 and ship early."
- **Question opening**: Pose the question the story will answer. "What happens when a 200-person company discovers that 40% of its revenue depends on a single customer?"
- **Data opening**: Lead with a surprising number. "It takes the average marketing team 11 hours to produce a report that nobody reads."
- Never open with background, history, or context. Open with a moment, then provide context as needed.

### Step 3: Write Scenes, Not Summaries
- For every key beat in the structure document, ask: "Can I write this as a scene?"
- A scene has: a specific time, a specific place, at least one character, and something happening
- Convert summaries to scenes:
  - Summary: "The team struggled with communication." → Scene: "The Monday standup had become a 90-minute argument. Two engineers would talk over each other while the designer sketched rectangles on her notepad, waiting for it to end."
  - Summary: "The customer was impressed by the results." → Scene: "David pulled up the report, scrolled to the bottom line, and paused. He leaned back in his chair. 'Run that number again,' he said. We did. Same result. He started laughing."
- Not every beat requires a full scene. Use scenes for the highest-impact moments: the turning point, the first result, the moment of decision. Use summary for transitions and context.

### Step 4: Integrate Dialogue and Quotes
- Use exact quotes from interviews wherever possible. Paraphrased dialogue loses authenticity.
- Format dialogue to maximize impact:
  - Short quotes mid-paragraph for pace: As Sarah put it, "We were flying blind."
  - Block quotes for longer, more powerful statements that deserve their own space
  - Dialogue pairs for conversational energy: "I asked her what changed. She said, 'I stopped dreading Monday mornings. That was the first thing I noticed.'"
- Clean up verbal tics (um, like, you know) but preserve the speaker's vocabulary and rhythm
- If you do not have a direct quote for a key moment, return to the interview recordings or contact the source. Do not fabricate dialogue.

### Step 5: Add Sensory and Specific Details
- Replace generic descriptions with specific ones:
  - Generic: "The company was growing fast." → Specific: "They hired 12 people in January and ran out of desks."
  - Generic: "The tool was easy to use." → Specific: "She connected her first data source in 6 minutes without opening the documentation."
  - Generic: "Revenue increased." → Specific: "Revenue went from $1.2M to $1.8M in one quarter — a number she had to show the board twice because they did not believe it."
- Details should do double duty: they make the story feel real AND advance the narrative. A detail that is just decoration slows the story.
- Use the senses: what did the person see, hear, feel? "The notification sound became her favorite part of the morning — it meant another sale had come in while she slept."

### Step 6: Draft the Full Piece at Speed
- Set a timer. Write the complete draft without stopping to edit.
- Follow the structure document beat by beat. Do not deviate from the structure during drafting.
- If you get stuck on a section, write "[NEEDS WORK]" and move to the next beat. Momentum matters more than perfection.
- Write 20-30% more than the target word count. It is easier to cut than to add.
- After completing the draft, walk away for at least 30 minutes before reading it. Fresh eyes catch what tired eyes miss.
- The first draft is a conversation with yourself. The edited version is a conversation with the audience.

## Examples

### Example 1: Drafting a Case Study Opening
- **Structure note**: "Beat 1 — Context: Greenfield Marketing, 15-person agency, reporting problem."
- **Bad draft**: "Greenfield Marketing is a 15-person digital marketing agency that was experiencing challenges with their reporting process."
- **Good draft**: "Every Monday morning at Greenfield Marketing started the same way: four analysts, six open tabs each, and a shared Google Sheet that nobody trusted. The sheet was supposed to reconcile data from six ad platforms. Instead, it reconciled everyone's frustration into a single location. 'I used to joke that the spreadsheet had more formulas than our campaigns had conversions,' said Tom Chen, Greenfield's operations lead. Nobody laughed."

### Example 2: Drafting a Turning Point Scene
- **Structure note**: "Beat 3 — The moment the new tool worked. Tom's reaction."
- **Bad draft**: "After implementing the new platform, Tom was pleased with the results. The data was now automated and accurate."
- **Good draft**: "Tom connected the first client account on a Tuesday afternoon. By Thursday morning, the dashboard was populated — six platforms, one view, numbers that matched. He checked them twice. Then he checked the old spreadsheet. The numbers were the same, down to the penny, except the dashboard had taken 4 hours instead of 16. He screenshotted it and sent it to his team with one message: 'No more Monday morning spreadsheet party.' The reply from his lead analyst was a single emoji: a prayer hands."

## When to Use
- After the Structure Layer has produced a beat sheet and before the Editing Layer begins refinement
- When creating any long-form content: case studies, blog posts, scripts, presentations, email sequences
- When converting interview transcripts, notes, or data into narrative prose
- When adapting an existing story from one format to another (the new format needs a fresh draft)

### Drafting Pitfalls
- **Editing while drafting**: Stopping every paragraph to perfect the prose. This kills momentum and produces neither a good draft nor a good edit. The drafting pass is for getting words on the page. The editing pass is for making them better.
- **Ignoring the structure document**: Deviating from the beat sheet because "inspiration struck." Inspiration is welcome, but the structure was designed for a reason. Note the inspiration, follow the structure, and evaluate the deviation after the draft is complete.
- **Writing for yourself**: Using vocabulary, references, or humor that the writer enjoys but the audience would not. Every sentence should serve the reader's experience, not the writer's self-expression.
- **The empty quote problem**: Reaching a point in the draft where a customer quote should go and writing a placeholder that never gets filled. If the quote does not exist, go back to the interview recordings or contact the source. Fabricated dialogue is never acceptable.
- **Fear of specificity**: Writing in generalities because specific details feel risky. "A company" is forgettable. "A 15-person agency in Portland managing $2M in ad spend" is a story. Specificity is the currency of narrative credibility.

## When NOT to Use
- For short-form content where drafting and editing are a single step (social posts, subject lines, ad copy)
- When the content is purely functional (API documentation, legal copy, compliance materials)
- When a polished piece already exists and needs only minor updates (use the Editing Layer directly)
- When the structure document has not been approved — drafting from an unstable structure wastes effort

## Agent Routing
- **Drafting Agent**: Writes the first draft following the structure document, voice guidelines, and craft principles
- **Research Agent**: Provides additional quotes, details, or data points when the drafter identifies gaps
- **Voice Agent**: Ensures consistency with brand voice across all drafted content
- Route to this layer when a structure document is ready and the next step is producing a written, scripted, or narrated first draft

### Drafting Quality Signals
- The opening paragraph drops the reader into a scene or moment, not a summary or definition
- At least 3 direct customer or subject quotes appear in the draft, using their actual words
- Specific details outnumber general descriptions by at least 2:1
- The emotional arc is discernible even in the rough draft — tension builds, the turn lands, the resolution satisfies
- The draft is 20-30% over target word count, leaving material for the Editing Layer to refine
- The voice is consistent throughout and matches the brand's established tone
