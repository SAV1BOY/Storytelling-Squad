# Klaff Pitch Anything — The STRONG Method
> Oren Klaff's complete pitch methodology for commanding attention, controlling the room, and closing the deal.

## Overview

Oren Klaff's *Pitch Anything* (2011) introduced a neuroscience-informed methodology for pitching that broke from conventional wisdom. Where most pitch advice focuses on content — better slides, clearer value propositions, more data — Klaff argued that the mechanics of how a pitch is delivered matter more than what the pitch contains. The reason: every message must first pass through the audience's primitive brain before it reaches the analytical neocortex, and the primitive brain responds to status, novelty, and tension, not to logic and data.

The STRONG method is Klaff's complete pitch architecture. Each letter represents a phase of the pitch, and each phase serves a specific neurological function. The method works because it is designed to match the sequence in which the human brain processes new information: first threat assessment, then social positioning, then emotional engagement, and finally analytical evaluation.

STRONG stands for: Setting the frame, Telling the story, Revealing the intrigue, Offering the prize, Nailing the hookpoint, Getting the decision.

Klaff developed this method through thousands of pitch meetings in investment banking and venture capital, where he raised over $400 million. The methodology has since been adapted for sales presentations, partnership proposals, internal budget requests, keynote addresses, and any high-stakes communication where the audience holds decision-making power.

## Core Methodology

### S — Setting the Frame

Every social interaction takes place within a frame — a set of assumptions about what is happening, who holds power, and what the rules are. The person who controls the frame controls the interaction. Before you deliver a single word of content, the frame has already been established by context: who called the meeting, who entered whose office, who is asking for what.

Frame setting happens in the first 30-90 seconds. Key actions:
- Establish your status as a peer, not a supplicant (do not thank them excessively for their time)
- Define the time constraint ("I have about 20 minutes before my next commitment")
- Set the agenda from a position of authority ("Here's what I want to cover and why it matters to you")
- Disrupt any default frame that positions you as lower status

If you enter a pitch as someone asking for something, you have already lost the frame. You must enter as someone offering something valuable — and evaluating whether the audience deserves access to it.

### T — Telling the Story

Once the frame is set, deliver your narrative. But this is not a data dump or a feature walkthrough. Klaff's storytelling phase follows a specific structure designed to engage the primitive brain:

1. **Introduce yourself through a brief, high-status origin story** — not a resume recitation, but a moment that establishes why you are uniquely positioned for this opportunity.
2. **Describe the world as it is** — the problem, the market reality, the pain point. Make it vivid and specific.
3. **Describe the world as it could be** — the vision, the transformation, the opportunity. Create contrast between "now" and "possible."
4. **Show how you bridge the gap** — your solution, product, or idea as the bridge between the current painful reality and the better future.

The story phase should take no more than 5-7 minutes. Longer narratives lose the primitive brain's attention.

### R — Revealing the Intrigue

Intrigue is Klaff's term for the moment in the pitch where you introduce something the audience didn't expect — a plot twist that reframes the opportunity. This is not a gimmick. It is a genuine piece of information or perspective that creates a "lean forward" moment.

Effective intrigue tactics:
- Reveal a counterintuitive insight ("Everyone assumes X, but the data shows Y")
- Share a behind-the-scenes story that humanizes the opportunity
- Introduce a constraint or challenge that makes the opportunity more interesting, not less
- Present a "what if" scenario that expands the audience's imagination

The intrigue phase serves a neurological function: novelty triggers dopamine release, which heightens attention and creates a positive association with your pitch. Without intrigue, the pitch is forgettable.

### O — Offering the Prize

This is the critical pivot that separates Klaff's method from conventional pitching. In a standard pitch, the presenter positions themselves as the seller and the audience as the buyer. Klaff inverts this dynamic.

In the prize phase, you position yourself (and your offer) as the prize. The audience should feel that getting access to your product, partnership, or investment opportunity is a privilege, not a given. Key behaviors:
- Mention other interest or demand casually (not desperately)
- Set criteria for who you work with ("We're selective about our partners because...")
- Express your own evaluation of the audience ("What I'm trying to determine is whether this is the right fit")
- Subtly withdraw attention — lean back, slow down, signal that you're assessing them

This phase leverages the scarcity principle and the fundamental human desire to obtain what seems difficult to get. When done well, the audience shifts from evaluating you to trying to qualify themselves.

### N — Nailing the Hookpoint

The hookpoint is the moment where the audience transitions from passive listening to active engagement. You know you've hit the hookpoint when the audience starts asking questions, leaning forward, or making future-oriented statements ("So how would this work if we...").

You cannot force the hookpoint, but you can create conditions for it:
- Stack multiple engagement triggers (novelty + tension + status + vision)
- Pause after a powerful statement and let the silence create space for response
- Ask a direct question that requires the audience to commit a position
- Present a clear decision point with real stakes

If you reach the end of your pitch without hitting a hookpoint, the pitch has failed regardless of how good the content was. The hookpoint is the evidence that you have crossed from the primitive brain to the neocortex — the audience is now thinking analytically about your proposition.

### G — Getting the Decision

Klaff is emphatic that a pitch must end with a clear ask and a clear next step. Ambiguous endings waste everything that came before. The decision phase includes:
- A direct, specific ask ("We're raising $2M at a $10M valuation and allocating $500K to strategic partners")
- A time constraint on the decision ("We're closing the round by March 15")
- A next step that is concrete and immediate ("I'll send the term sheet today and we can schedule a follow-up for Thursday")
- Graceful handling of "not now" responses that preserve the relationship and the frame

The decision phase must maintain the prize frame. You are not begging for a response. You are offering a clear path forward and giving the audience the opportunity to take it.

## Detailed Steps

### Preparation Phase (Before the Pitch)
1. Research the audience's frames — what do they believe about your industry, your company size, your position?
2. Identify the default frame and plan your disruption
3. Craft a 60-second origin story that establishes status without arrogance
4. Develop a clear "world as it is / world as it could be" narrative arc
5. Identify 2-3 genuine intrigue elements (not manufactured surprises)
6. Define your prize positioning — what makes you selective?
7. Prepare your specific ask and next-step proposal
8. Rehearse the full pitch to fit within 20 minutes maximum

### Execution Phase (During the Pitch)
1. Enter the room with ownership energy — do not apologize for existing
2. Set the frame in the first 90 seconds (time constraint, agenda, status)
3. Deliver the story arc in 5-7 minutes — vivid, specific, emotionally engaging
4. Drop the intrigue element at the peak of attention
5. Pivot to prize positioning — shift from presenting to evaluating
6. Watch for hookpoint signals and amplify them when they appear
7. Close with a specific ask, timeline, and next step
8. Leave before you overstay — exit at peak energy, not after it fades

## Examples

### Example 1: SaaS Startup Investor Pitch
A cybersecurity SaaS startup used the STRONG method to raise a Series A.

**Frame**: The founder opened with "I've got about 15 minutes before a call with [major tech company's] CISO, so let me get right to it." This set a time frame (urgency) and status frame (she's in demand) without being overt.

**Story**: "Right now, 70% of mid-market companies have less security monitoring than the average home with a Ring doorbell. They're not dumb — the enterprise tools cost $200K+ and take 6 months to deploy. We built what should have existed five years ago: enterprise-grade monitoring that deploys in 48 hours at $2K/month."

**Intrigue**: "Here's what nobody in this space is talking about: the next wave of attacks isn't targeting IT infrastructure — it's targeting the APIs between SaaS tools. We're the only platform monitoring that surface, and we've already detected three zero-days that our competitors missed."

**Prize**: "We've got term sheets from two funds already. What I'm trying to determine is whether your fund adds strategic value beyond capital — specifically, your portfolio company network for distribution."

**Hookpoint**: The lead partner leaned forward and asked, "Which SaaS integrations are you monitoring?" — a technical, future-oriented question that signaled genuine engagement.

**Decision**: "We're allocating $3M in this round and closing in three weeks. If you want a position, I need a partner meeting by Friday."

### Example 2: Internal Budget Proposal for Marketing Team
A marketing director used the STRONG method to secure a $500K budget increase for content marketing.

**Frame**: Instead of presenting to the CFO as a cost center requesting funds, she opened with: "I want to show you something that changes how we think about our acquisition cost structure."

**Story**: "Our paid acquisition cost has increased 40% year-over-year. Every competitor is bidding on the same keywords. But our three best-performing blog posts from last year generated $1.2M in pipeline at a cost of $4,000 to produce. That's a 300:1 return that actually improves over time."

**Intrigue**: "I pulled data from six public companies in our space. The two that invested in content marketing in 2022 are now spending 35% less on total customer acquisition than the four that didn't. Content isn't a cost — it's the hedge against paid channel inflation."

**Prize**: "I've had two agencies pitch me to run this program externally at $800K. I think we can do it better in-house for $500K. But I only want to do this if leadership is committed to a 12-month horizon — content compounds, and measuring it quarterly will kill it."

**Decision**: "I need a decision by end of month so we can post the two content roles and have the team ramped by Q2."

## When to Use

- Investor pitches (seed through growth stage)
- Enterprise sales presentations where a single meeting determines the deal
- Partnership proposals where you need to establish mutual value
- Internal budget or resource requests to executive leadership
- Board presentations where strategic direction is being set
- Keynote or conference presentations where you need to stand out
- Any high-stakes meeting where the audience holds decision-making power and attention is scarce

## When NOT to Use

- Casual or relationship-building conversations (STRONG can feel aggressive in informal contexts)
- Consultative selling where discovery should precede any pitch
- Existing customer conversations focused on retention or expansion (too adversarial)
- Community or movement-building contexts (Ganz frameworks are more appropriate)
- Situations where genuine collaboration is needed rather than persuasion
- Cultural contexts where directness and frame control are perceived as disrespectful
- Written content where the real-time dynamics of frame control don't apply

## Integration

- **With Klaff's Frame Control**: STRONG is the overarching architecture; Frame Control provides the detailed mechanics for the "S" phase and for maintaining frame throughout.
- **With Klaff's Crocodile Brain**: The crocodile brain theory explains WHY the STRONG sequence works in this specific order — each phase addresses a different layer of brain processing.
- **With Klaff's Push/Pull**: Push/Pull dynamics operate within the "O" (Offering the prize) and "N" (Nailing the hookpoint) phases.
- **With Johnstone's Status Transactions**: Klaff's frame-setting and prize-offering are status transactions. Johnstone's behavioral specifics (stillness, speech pace, eye contact) are the implementation tools.
- **With Ganz's Story of Now**: The "T" (Telling the story) phase can incorporate Ganz's urgency structure for pitches that require collective action.
