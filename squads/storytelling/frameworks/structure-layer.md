# Structure Layer
> Choose the framework, map the beats, design the arc, and place proof — the architecture phase of story creation.

## Overview
Structure is the skeleton of story. Before a single word is written, the narrative must be architected: which framework applies, what beats the story will follow, where the arc peaks, and where proof lands for maximum impact. The Structure Layer bridges strategy and drafting. It takes the strategic decision ("tell this story, to this audience, through this channel") and produces a detailed narrative blueprint that the Drafting Layer will bring to life. Structuring is a separate skill from writing — some of the best writers are poor structurers, and their stories wander. Some of the best structurers are poor writers, and their outlines come to life through collaborators. This layer treats structure as its own discipline.

## Core Methodology
The Structure Layer operates through four sequential steps:

1. **Framework Selection** — Choose the narrative framework that best fits the story type, audience, and channel. A customer story uses the Case Study Story Framework. A data presentation uses the Data Storytelling Framework. The correct framework provides the narrative pattern; the wrong framework forces the story into an unnatural shape.

2. **Beat Mapping** — Identify the specific story beats (narrative moments) within the chosen framework and assign them to sections of the content. Each beat serves a narrative function: setup, tension, turn, proof, resolution, or CTA.

3. **Arc Design** — Shape the emotional trajectory of the story. Where does tension peak? Where does relief arrive? Where does the audience lean in and where might they check out? Arc design ensures the story moves the audience emotionally, not just informationally.

4. **Proof Placement** — Determine where in the narrative evidence appears for maximum impact. Proof placed too early feels unearned. Proof placed too late loses the skeptic. Optimal proof placement follows a claim at the moment of highest audience skepticism.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Select the Framework
- Review the strategic brief from the Strategy Layer: what is the story, who is the audience, what is the channel?
- Match the story type to the appropriate framework:
  - Customer transformation → Case Study Story Framework
  - Company origin → Brand Story Framework or Founder Story Framework
  - Sales conversation → Sales Story Framework or Pitch Story Framework
  - Data presentation → Data Storytelling Framework
  - Product announcement → Product Narrative Framework or Launch Narrative Framework
  - Educational content → Content Marketing Story Framework
  - Video content → Video Script Narrative Framework
  - Ad creative → Ad Storytelling Framework
  - Email campaign → Email Sequence Narrative Framework
- If the story spans multiple types (e.g., a case study presented as a video), choose the primary framework and adapt elements from the secondary
- Document the framework choice and the rationale — this prevents mid-draft pivots that waste time

### Step 2: Map the Beats
- Open the selected framework and review its narrative beats
- For each beat, assign specific content from the story material gathered during discovery
- **Example for a Case Study using Case Study Story Framework**:
  - Beat 1 (Context): Company background, industry, team size, initial situation
  - Beat 2 (Friction): The specific problem — quote from interview: "We were losing 3 hours per person per day"
  - Beat 3 (Action): The decision to change, the implementation process, the first-week experience
  - Beat 4 (Result): Quantified outcomes — time saved, revenue impact, team satisfaction
  - Beat 5 (Proof): Direct quotes, screenshots, third-party validation
- For each beat, note the estimated word count or time allocation (for video/presentations)
- Identify which beats have strong material and which need additional research
- If a beat lacks material, either return to the Discovery Layer or adjust the framework to work with what you have

### Step 3: Design the Arc
- Plot the emotional trajectory of the story using a simple curve:
  - **Baseline**: The opening state — neutral or mildly curious
  - **Rising tension**: Each beat increases emotional investment through stakes, conflict, or curiosity
  - **Peak tension**: The moment of maximum uncertainty or emotional intensity — usually right before the turning point
  - **Resolution**: The turning point and its aftermath — relief, satisfaction, or inspiration
  - **New baseline**: The closing state — higher than the opening, with forward momentum toward the CTA
- Identify potential "dead zones" where the audience might disengage:
  - Technical explanations without emotional context
  - Proof sections that are too dense without narrative relief
  - Transitions between beats that feel abrupt
- Design interventions for dead zones: a surprising detail, a callback to an earlier moment, a rhetorical question, a change of pace
- For longer content (blog series, webinars, video), map mini-arcs within the larger arc — each section should have its own setup-tension-resolution cycle

### Step 4: Place Proof Strategically
- Identify the 3-5 proof elements available: customer quotes, metrics, case study excerpts, data points, demonstrations, third-party endorsements
- For each proof element, determine the narrative moment where the audience's skepticism peaks:
  - After a bold claim → place the strongest proof immediately
  - After describing a transformation → place the quantitative result
  - After introducing the product → place the customer quote about the experience
  - Near the CTA → place the risk-reversal proof (guarantee, trial, pilot results)
- Proof layering rules:
  - Never stack multiple proof types without narrative breathing room between them
  - Alternate between emotional proof (quotes, stories) and rational proof (data, metrics)
  - The first proof should be the most emotionally compelling, not the most data-heavy
  - The final proof before the CTA should address the audience's most common objection

### Step 5: Create the Structure Document
- Produce a beat sheet: a simple document listing each beat, its content, its word count/time allocation, and its proof elements
- Include transition notes: how each beat connects to the next
- Include arc annotations: mark the emotional peak, the dead zones and their interventions, and the CTA moment
- The structure document should be readable by anyone on the team and provide enough detail that a different writer could draft from it
- Format example:
  - Beat 1 — Context (150 words): Introduce the customer and their industry. Tone: neutral, factual. Transition: "But underneath the surface, something was breaking."
  - Beat 2 — Friction (200 words): The specific problem. Quote from interview. Tone: empathetic, tense. Proof: time-loss metric. Transition: "That is when they decided to try something different."

### Step 6: Review and Validate Structure
- Test the structure against the strategic brief: Does it serve the right audience? Does it address the right objection? Does it lead to the right CTA?
- Test the arc: Read the beat sheet aloud. Does the emotional trajectory feel natural? Does tension build? Is there a satisfying resolution?
- Test proof placement: Is proof appearing at moments of peak skepticism? Is there enough proof to be convincing without being overwhelming?
- Share the structure with the team for feedback before moving to the Drafting Layer. Structural problems are 10x easier to fix in a beat sheet than in a full draft.

## Examples

### Example 1: Structure for a Case Study Blog Post
- **Framework**: Case Study Story Framework
- **Beat 1 — Context (150 words)**: Greenfield Marketing, 15-person agency, $2M monthly ad spend. Professional, growing, but struggling with reporting.
- **Beat 2 — Friction (200 words)**: Monday morning scramble. Analysts pulling from 6 platforms. 40% of time on reporting. Three analysts quit. Quote: "We were paying strategy salaries for spreadsheet work."
- **Beat 3 — Action (175 words)**: Tom Chen runs a 2-week pilot. Integration takes 4 hours per client. Team skepticism. Quote: "I expected a month of setup."
- **Beat 4 — Result (175 words)**: Reporting time drops from 16 hours to 2 per client per month. 340 analyst hours reallocated. Client retention up 18%. Quote: "We finally have time to do the work clients actually pay us for."
- **Beat 5 — Proof (100 words)**: Before/after time audit chart. Client satisfaction survey. Tom's recommendation to peers.
- **Total**: ~800 words. Arc peaks at Beat 2 (frustration) and resolves at Beat 4 (transformation).

### Example 2: Structure for a Webinar
- **Framework**: Webinar Narrative Framework
- **Teach Beat 1 (10 min)**: Manual SEO audit technique. Valuable standalone. Reveals tension: most sites have 40+ issues.
- **Teach Beat 2 (10 min)**: Prioritization framework. Valuable standalone. Reveals tension: fixing manually takes 8+ hours.
- **Teach Beat 3 (5 min)**: Metrics to track. Valuable standalone. Reveals tension: issues recur monthly.
- **Tension Crystallization (3 min)**: Summary of all three tensions. "You now know how to audit, prioritize, and measure. The challenge is doing it consistently."
- **Offer (10 min)**: Demo of automated audit tool. Customer proof. Pricing.
- **CTA (5 min)**: Free trial. Q&A.

## When to Use
- After the Strategy Layer has determined which story to tell and before the Drafting Layer begins writing
- When restructuring an existing piece of content that is not performing
- When adapting a story from one format to another (blog to video, case study to social)
- When multiple team members will contribute to a single piece and need a shared blueprint

## When NOT to Use
- When the content is purely spontaneous (live social media, real-time responses)
- When the story is short enough that structure is self-evident (a single social post, a subject line)
- When the writer is highly experienced and prefers to discover structure through drafting (some writers structure in their heads)

## Integration
- Receives story assignments from the **Strategy Layer**
- Selects from all application frameworks (Brand, Case Study, Founder, Pitch, Sales, etc.)
- Outputs structure documents to the **Drafting Layer**
- Coordinates proof placement with the **Discovery Layer** story bank
- Informs the **Editing Layer** about intended arc for quality control

## Agent Routing
- **Structure Agent**: Owns framework selection, beat mapping, arc design, and proof placement
- **Strategy Agent**: Provides the brief that initiates the structuring process
- **Research Agent**: Supplies additional material when beats lack content
- Route to this layer after strategic decisions are made and before any writing begins
