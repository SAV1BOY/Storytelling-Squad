# Snyder's Save the Cat Beat Sheet
> A precise 15-beat narrative structure that maps the emotional turning points of any story, giving marketers a scene-by-scene blueprint for compelling content.

## Overview

Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat!* (2005) revolutionized screenwriting by codifying a 15-beat structure that reliably produces emotionally satisfying stories. The "Save the Cat" moment itself refers to the early scene where the protagonist does something likable—demonstrating why the audience should root for them. In marketing, this translates to the moment where a brand demonstrates authentic empathy or generosity before asking for anything in return.

The beat sheet provides more granular structure than three-act models. Where the Hero's Journey tells you *what* must happen, Save the Cat tells you *when* it must happen—including specific percentage-based page (or time) markers. This precision makes it invaluable for tightly timed content like video scripts, webinar presentations, and multi-part campaigns.

## Core Methodology

Snyder's system rests on three principles:

1. **Structure is not the enemy of creativity** — Predictable emotional beats create a container within which surprise and originality thrive.
2. **Every beat serves a function** — No beat exists for tradition's sake. Each one performs a specific psychological operation on the audience: establishing empathy, raising stakes, delivering catharsis, etc.
3. **The midpoint matters most** — The midpoint is not filler between setup and climax. It is the story's axis—the moment where "fun and games" ends and real consequences begin. Most weak marketing narratives fail because their midpoint is empty.

The 15 beats divide into three acts with precise timing:

- **Act I (0-25%):** Setup — establish the world, the hero, and the stakes
- **Act II First Half (25-50%):** Promise of the Premise — deliver what the audience came for
- **Act II Second Half (50-75%):** Bad Guys Close In — escalate pressure and complicate the situation
- **Act III (75-100%):** Finale — resolve the conflict through transformation

## Detailed Steps

1. **Opening Image (0-1%)** — A visual or verbal snapshot of the hero's "before" state. This image will be mirrored by the Final Image to show transformation. In marketing: the first frame of a video, the hero section of a landing page, the subject line of an email.

2. **Theme Stated (5%)** — Someone (not the hero) articulates the story's central truth, often indirectly. The hero doesn't understand it yet. In marketing: a provocative question or counterintuitive insight placed early.

3. **Setup (1-10%)** — Introduce the hero's world, supporting characters, and the "six things that need fixing." In marketing: paint the audience's current reality with enough specificity that they feel seen.

4. **Catalyst (10%)** — The inciting incident. Life will never be the same. In marketing: the trigger event—a product launch, a market shift, a customer's breaking point.

5. **Debate (10-20%)** — The hero questions whether to act. Should I? Can I? What if I fail? In marketing: acknowledge the audience's hesitations honestly. This builds trust.

6. **Break into Two (20-25%)** — The hero makes a *proactive* choice to enter the new world. Not pushed—they choose. In marketing: the commitment moment. "Here's what we decided to do differently."

7. **B Story (22%)** — A secondary storyline begins, often a relationship that will carry the theme. In marketing: introduce a parallel narrative—a customer's perspective, a team member's insight, a historical analogy.

8. **Fun and Games (20-50%)** — The "promise of the premise" delivered. This is what the audience came for. If the premise is "an AI that writes your emails," this section shows the AI writing emails in delightful ways. In marketing: product demonstrations, feature showcases, success montages.

9. **Midpoint (50%)** — A false victory or false defeat. Stakes raise from personal to universal. The "fun" phase ends; real consequences begin. In marketing: the pivot from features to stakes—"But here's what happens when you don't get this right."

10. **Bad Guys Close In (50-75%)** — External pressures mount while internal doubts grow. The team fractures. The plan fails. In marketing: competitive threats, market challenges, implementation hurdles. Show that the journey isn't easy.

11. **All Is Lost (75%)** — The lowest point. Something or someone is "dead" (literally or metaphorically). In marketing: the cautionary tale—what happens to those who don't adapt.

12. **Dark Night of the Soul (75-80%)** — The hero mourns, reflects, and—crucially—discovers something they missed. In marketing: the moment of insight that reframes the entire problem.

13. **Break into Three (80%)** — Armed with new understanding, the hero commits to the final battle. A and B stories merge. In marketing: the synthesis moment—where product capability meets human insight.

14. **Finale (80-99%)** — The hero executes the plan, defeats the antagonist, and proves the transformation is real. In marketing: the proof section—results, metrics, testimonials, case outcomes.

15. **Final Image (99-100%)** — Mirror of the Opening Image, showing how far the hero has come. In marketing: the aspirational end state. The bookend that completes the transformation arc.

## Examples

### Example 1: Product Launch Video (3 minutes)
- **Opening Image (0:00):** Frustrated marketer staring at a dashboard showing declining engagement.
- **Theme Stated (0:09):** Colleague says, "Maybe the problem isn't the audience. Maybe it's the message."
- **Setup (0:00-0:18):** Quick montage of the marketer's daily struggle—A/B tests that flop, meetings about meetings.
- **Catalyst (0:18):** An email arrives: "Your competitor just went viral."
- **Debate (0:18-0:36):** "Do we need another tool? Or do we need a different approach?"
- **Break into Two (0:36-0:45):** "We decided to try [Product]."
- **Fun and Games (0:45-1:30):** Rapid showcase of the product in action—generating content, testing variations, hitting send.
- **Midpoint (1:30):** First campaign results come in: 3x engagement. But then—"Our biggest client said the tone felt off."
- **Bad Guys Close In (1:30-2:15):** Refinement process, team debates, competitive pressure.
- **All Is Lost (2:15):** "We almost went back to the old way."
- **Break into Three (2:20):** "Then we realized we'd been using it wrong. We needed to feed it our brand voice, not generic prompts."
- **Finale (2:20-2:50):** Results: 5x engagement, client retention up 40%, team working fewer hours.
- **Final Image (2:50-3:00):** Same marketer, same dashboard—but now the numbers are green and they're leaving the office at 5 PM, smiling.

### Example 2: Annual Report Narrative Arc
A nonprofit structures its annual report as a Save the Cat beat sheet, with the "hero" being a specific beneficiary whose story threads through the data sections, creating emotional momentum that makes the statistics land with impact.

## When to Use

- Video scripts of any length (the percentage system scales)
- Webinar and presentation scripts where timing is critical
- Multi-chapter content (blog series, podcast seasons, email sequences)
- Campaign narratives that need precise emotional pacing
- Any format where you control the audience's experience sequentially

## When NOT to Use

- Non-linear content where the audience chooses their own path (websites, interactive tools)
- Purely data-driven content where narrative pacing is irrelevant
- Very short formats (under 30 seconds) where only 3-5 beats can fit (use Condensed Monomyth)
- When the audience expects academic or journalistic objectivity rather than narrative craft
- Content that needs to feel spontaneous or conversational rather than structured

## Integration

Save the Cat integrates with:
- **Campbell's Hero's Journey** — Save the Cat provides the timing and granularity that Campbell's model lacks
- **Snyder's 10 Genres** — Each genre has characteristic ways the 15 beats manifest
- **Coyne's Five Commandments** — Apply Coyne's scene-level analysis to each beat to ensure it's working
- **Duarte's Sparkline** — Overlay the emotional map of the sparkline onto the beat timing for presentation design
- **Dicks' Story Beginning-End Rule** — Use Dicks' technique to craft the Opening Image and Final Image as deliberate mirrors

## Agent Routing

Route to Save the Cat when:
- The user needs precise timing and pacing for a narrative (video, webinar, presentation)
- The user mentions "beats," "beat sheet," or "Save the Cat" explicitly
- The content is sequential and the creator controls the audience's pace
- The user needs more granularity than a three-act structure provides
- The format is 2+ minutes of video or 1000+ words of narrative prose

Do NOT route here when:
- The content is interactive or non-linear (use Hall's framework for story selection)
- The user needs a quick structure for a pitch (use Klaff or Miller One-Liner)
- The focus is on character identity rather than plot structure (use Campbell Archetypes)
- The user needs genre-level guidance before structure (route to Snyder 10 Genres first)
