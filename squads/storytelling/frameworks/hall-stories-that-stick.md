# Hall's Stories That Stick — The 4 Strategic Story Types
> Four categories of business stories that every organization needs: Value Story, Founder Story, Customer Story, and Purpose Story — when to use each and how they work together.

## Overview

Kindra Hall's "Stories That Stick" methodology identifies four strategic story types that every business needs in its narrative arsenal. While other frameworks focus on how to tell stories (structure, craft, technique), Hall's contribution is answering a more fundamental question: which story should you tell?

Most organizations default to one story type — usually the Value Story (product/service pitch) — and neglect the other three. This creates a one-dimensional narrative presence that fails to connect with audiences at different stages of the relationship. Hall's framework ensures strategic coverage across all four types, creating a narrative ecosystem that serves awareness, conversion, loyalty, and culture simultaneously.

The four types are not mutually exclusive. A single narrative can contain elements of multiple types. But each type has a primary function, a primary audience, and a primary moment in the relationship where it is most effective. Understanding these distinctions prevents the most common storytelling mistake in business: telling the right story at the wrong time.

## Core Methodology

### The 4 Strategic Story Types

**1. The Value Story**
**Function:** Demonstrates the value of a product, service, or solution.
**Primary audience:** Prospects in the consideration or decision stage.
**Primary question it answers:** "Why should I choose this?"

The Value Story shows the product or service in action, but not through features and specifications. It shows the transformation that the product enables for a real person in a real situation. The protagonist is not the product; the protagonist is the person whose life or work changed because of the product.

Key elements:
- A specific person in a specific situation (not a composite or archetype)
- A clear "before" state that the audience can relate to
- The moment the product/service enters the story (not as a savior, but as a tool)
- A concrete "after" state that is measurably or observably different
- The emotional dimension of the change, not just the functional dimension

Common failure mode: The Value Story becomes a feature demo. The product takes center stage instead of the person. When this happens, the story loses its emotional power and becomes a pitch.

**2. The Founder Story**
**Function:** Explains why the company exists and what drives the people behind it.
**Primary audience:** Anyone evaluating whether to trust the brand — prospects, employees, investors, partners, media.
**Primary question it answers:** "Why does this company exist?"

The Founder Story is the origin narrative. It connects the company's existence to a human experience — usually a problem the founder personally experienced, a moment of insight that could not be ignored, or a conviction so strong that building a company was the only adequate response.

Key elements:
- The founder as a real, flawed human being (not a visionary superhero)
- The specific moment or experience that sparked the company
- The emotional stakes: what the founder stood to lose by starting this company
- The connection between the founder's personal experience and the customer's experience
- The ongoing relevance: why the founding motivation still drives the company today

Common failure mode: The Founder Story becomes a résumé or a self-congratulatory origin myth. The founder is presented as prescient and destined for success. This version of the story is unbelievable and unrelatable.

**3. The Customer Story**
**Function:** Provides proof of transformation through a real customer's experience.
**Primary audience:** Prospects who need social proof, especially those in the decision stage.
**Primary question it answers:** "Will this work for someone like me?"

The Customer Story is the most powerful sales tool in the narrative arsenal because it is told about (and ideally by) someone who is not the brand. The prospect sees themselves in the customer's "before" state and is drawn toward the customer's "after" state. The brand is present but not dominant.

Key elements:
- A customer who is demographically and psychographically similar to the target audience
- A "before" state that the prospect recognizes as their own
- The specific challenge or pain point (not a generic problem statement)
- The decision to try the product/service (including hesitations and objections)
- The transformation: concrete, specific, and multidimensional (functional, emotional, social)
- The customer's voice: authentic language, not marketing copy

Common failure mode: The Customer Story is sanitized into a testimonial. All struggle is removed, all nuance is flattened, and the result reads like an advertisement. The best Customer Stories include the customer's doubts, the imperfect parts of the experience, and the unexpected outcomes.

**4. The Purpose Story**
**Function:** Articulates the bigger "why" — the impact the company seeks to have on the world beyond its products.
**Primary audience:** Everyone — but especially employees, long-term customers, and the broader culture.
**Primary question it answers:** "What does this company stand for?"

The Purpose Story transcends the transaction. It connects the brand to a cause, a belief, or a vision of the future that is larger than the company itself. The Purpose Story is what turns customers into advocates and employees into missionaries.

Key elements:
- A clear articulation of the world the brand is trying to create (not just the problems it solves)
- Specific evidence that the brand is actively pursuing this purpose (not just claiming it)
- The tension between the current state of the world and the brand's vision
- Real people affected by the brand's purpose work
- An invitation for the audience to participate in the purpose

Common failure mode: The Purpose Story becomes performative. The brand claims a purpose that is disconnected from its actual operations, products, and culture. Audiences detect this instantly. Purpose must be embedded in operations, not added to marketing.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Audit Your Current Story Portfolio

Before creating new stories, inventory what you already have:

| Story Type | Do We Have It? | Is It Current? | Is It Effective? | Gap? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Value Story | | | | |
| Founder Story | | | | |
| Customer Story | | | | |
| Purpose Story | | | | |

Most organizations will find they have multiple Value Stories (product pitches), possibly a Founder Story (on the About page), few genuine Customer Stories (testimonials are not stories), and no Purpose Story.

### Step 2: Prioritize by Need

Which story type will have the most impact right now?

- **If conversion is the problem:** Prioritize Customer Stories. Prospects need proof.
- **If awareness is the problem:** Prioritize the Purpose Story. Give people a reason to pay attention before they need your product.
- **If trust is the problem:** Prioritize the Founder Story. People trust people before they trust companies.
- **If differentiation is the problem:** Prioritize the Value Story. Show what your product actually does for real people in a way competitors cannot replicate.

### Step 3: Gather Raw Material

For each priority story type, identify the raw material:

- **Value Story:** Interview 3-5 customers who had the most dramatic transformation. Look for the customer whose "before" state most closely matches the target prospect.
- **Founder Story:** Interview the founder(s) using the First/Last/Best/Worst exercise. Look for the 5-second moment that sparked the company.
- **Customer Story:** Identify customers who are willing to share their experience in their own voice. Prepare interview questions using the Storyworthy methodology.
- **Purpose Story:** Gather evidence of purpose-in-action: community impact, policy changes, employee initiatives, customer advocacy. The Purpose Story must be built on facts, not aspirations.

### Step 4: Craft Using Narrative Frameworks

Apply the appropriate craft framework to each story type:

- **Value Story:** Use Hall's Story Formula (Character + Emotion + Moment). Ensure the product enters the story as a tool, not a hero.
- **Founder Story:** Use Dicks' Storyworthy methodology to find and develop the 5-second moment. Use the Beginning/End Rule to ensure maximum transformation contrast.
- **Customer Story:** Use Dicks' Storyworthy for craft, Hall's Gap-Bridge for structure, and Howell's ABT for compression.
- **Purpose Story:** Use Howell's Story Cycle for Brands to build the full arc. The brand's purpose is the Moral (Step 9) of its own story cycle.

### Step 5: Deploy Strategically

Each story type belongs in specific channels and moments. Use Hall's Story Selection Matrix for precise deployment.

General deployment guidelines:
- **Value Story:** Sales conversations, product pages, demo follow-ups, comparison content
- **Founder Story:** About page, investor decks, press kits, keynotes, employee onboarding
- **Customer Story:** Case study pages, email nurture sequences, social proof sections, conference talks
- **Purpose Story:** Brand campaigns, annual reports, culture decks, community content, recruitment

## Examples

### Example 1: Software Company Story Portfolio

**Value Story:** "Sarah was spending 20 hours a week manually reconciling invoices across three systems. After implementing [Product], reconciliation happens automatically and Sarah spends those 20 hours on strategic analysis that led to $400K in cost savings her first quarter."

**Founder Story:** "In 2017, our founder was the Sarah in this story. She was a controller at a mid-market company, drowning in manual reconciliation, and she told her CEO the fix would cost $2M in consulting fees. He said no. She quit and built the fix herself for $200/month."

**Customer Story:** "When Sarah's company evaluated our platform, she was skeptical. She had been burned by two previous vendors who promised automation and delivered complexity. She almost did not take the demo. What changed her mind was talking to another controller — someone she found in our community forum — who described the exact problem she was having and exactly how it got solved."

**Purpose Story:** "There are 500,000 controllers and accounting managers at mid-market companies in North America. Most of them are spending 30-50% of their time on work that machines should do. We exist to give them that time back — not so they can do more accounting, but so they can do the strategic work they were trained for and never get to do."

### Example 2: Consumer Brand Story Portfolio

**Value Story:** A new parent who was overwhelmed by conflicting nutrition advice until she found the brand's simple, evidence-based meal plans. Functional change: her child eats better. Emotional change: she stopped feeling guilty about every meal.

**Founder Story:** The founder was a pediatric nutritionist who watched parents cry in her office because they felt like they were failing. She realized the problem was not the parents — it was the information environment. She built the brand to be the calm, clear voice in a noisy market.

**Customer Story:** A father who initially signed up to prove to his ex-wife that he could handle meal planning during his custody weeks. Over time, cooking for his kids became the most meaningful part of his week. He did not expect the product to change his relationship with his children, but it did.

**Purpose Story:** Every child deserves to be fed well, regardless of their parents' income, education, or time. The brand donates a meal plan for every plan purchased and partners with community health organizations to make nutrition guidance accessible to families who cannot afford it.

## When to Use

- Building a comprehensive brand narrative strategy
- Auditing existing story assets and identifying gaps
- Preparing content for multiple stages of the customer journey
- Training sales teams on which stories to tell at which stage
- Developing a content calendar organized by story type
- Investor communications that need multiple narrative angles
- Brand launches or relaunches that need a full narrative ecosystem

## When NOT to Use

- When you need to craft a single specific story (use Storyworthy or Story Formula)
- When you need structural guidance for how to tell a story (use ABT, Gap-Bridge, or Story Cycle)
- When you need to choose between multiple existing stories for a specific moment (use Story Selection Matrix)
- When the need is for personal storytelling rather than brand storytelling (use Dicks' frameworks)

## Integration

- **Hall's Story Selection Matrix:** After identifying which story types you have, use the matrix to deploy them in the right contexts.
- **Hall's Gap-Bridge Framework:** Use Gap-Bridge as the structural template for crafting each story type.
- **Hall's Story Formula:** Use the formula as the quality test for each story type.
- **Dicks' Storyworthy:** The best methodology for crafting Founder Stories and Customer Stories with emotional depth.
- **Dicks' First/Last/Best/Worst:** Use this exercise to discover raw material for all four story types.
- **Howell's Story Cycle:** The four story types can be mapped to different steps of the Story Cycle. Value Stories live in the Journey and Victory steps. Founder Stories live in the Backstory and Disruption steps. Customer Stories live in the Hero and Transformation steps. Purpose Stories live in the Moral and Ritual steps.
- **Howell's Story Strategy Canvas:** The canvas's Proof element should contain at least one story of each type.
