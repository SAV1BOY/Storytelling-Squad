# Hall's Story Selection Matrix
> A decision framework for choosing the RIGHT story for the context — preventing the costly mistake of telling the wrong story at the right time.

## Overview

Kindra Hall's Story Selection Matrix solves a problem that most storytelling frameworks ignore: selection. Most organizations do not lack stories — they lack the discipline to choose the right story for the right audience, in the right channel, at the right moment. The matrix provides that discipline.

The insight behind the matrix is that storytelling failures in business are rarely craft failures. The story is usually well-told. The failure is strategic: a founder story is told when a customer story was needed. A purpose story is deployed on a product page where a value story would convert. A deeply emotional narrative is shared on a channel where the audience has a 3-second attention span.

The matrix operates on four axes: Audience (who is receiving this story), Objective (what do we want them to do after hearing it), Channel (where will they encounter it), and Timing (where are they in their relationship with the brand). By plotting these four variables, the matrix narrows the field of possible stories to the one or two that will be most effective in the specific context.

This framework assumes you already have a reservoir of stories (built through Hall's 4 Story Types, Dicks' exercises, or organizational knowledge). The matrix does not create stories; it deploys them.

## Core Methodology

### The Four Axes

**Axis 1: Audience (Who)**
Not just demographics but psychographic state. The same person is a different audience depending on their current mindset, needs, and relationship to the brand.

Audience states to consider:
- **Unaware:** Does not know the brand or the problem it solves. Needs stories that create recognition and resonance, not stories that sell.
- **Problem-aware:** Knows they have a problem but does not know solutions exist. Needs stories that validate their experience and introduce possibility.
- **Solution-aware:** Knows solutions exist but has not chosen one. Needs stories that differentiate and build trust.
- **Product-aware:** Knows this specific brand/product but has not committed. Needs stories that reduce risk and provide proof.
- **Most aware:** Existing customers or advocates. Needs stories that deepen loyalty, reinforce identity, and provide shareable narratives.

Each audience state calls for a different story type, tone, and level of detail.

**Axis 2: Objective (What)**
What specific action or change do we want the audience to take or experience after encountering this story?

Common objectives:
- **Awareness:** We want the audience to know we exist and understand what we stand for.
- **Consideration:** We want the audience to include us in their evaluation set.
- **Conversion:** We want the audience to take a specific action (purchase, sign up, schedule a call).
- **Retention:** We want the audience to stay, renew, or deepen their engagement.
- **Advocacy:** We want the audience to recommend us to others or share our story.
- **Recruitment:** We want potential employees to see themselves at this company.
- **Alignment:** We want internal teams to understand and embody the brand narrative.

Each objective demands a different narrative approach. A conversion story must be specific and proof-heavy. An awareness story must be emotionally broad and identity-driven.

**Axis 3: Channel (Where)**
Where will this story be encountered? Channel constraints determine length, format, tone, and structure.

Channel categories:
- **High-attention channels:** Keynote stages, long-form articles, podcasts, webinars, in-person meetings. These channels allow full narrative development with setup, tension, and resolution.
- **Medium-attention channels:** Email newsletters, LinkedIn posts, blog posts, case study pages. These channels require tighter structure but still allow narrative arc.
- **Low-attention channels:** Social media feeds, display ads, banner ads, homepage headlines. These channels demand compression — the story must be captured in a single image, sentence, or 15-second clip.
- **Conversational channels:** Sales calls, customer success check-ins, networking events. These channels require stories that can be told naturally in conversation, adjustable in length, and responsive to audience cues.

**Axis 4: Timing (When in the Relationship)**
Where is the audience in their relationship with the brand? Timing is distinct from awareness level — a customer can be highly aware of the brand but at a critical decision point (renewal, upsell, churn risk) that changes which story is needed.

Timing moments:
- **First contact:** The audience encounters the brand for the first time.
- **Evaluation:** The audience is actively comparing options.
- **Decision point:** The audience is about to commit (or not).
- **Onboarding:** The audience has just committed and needs reassurance.
- **Milestone:** The audience has achieved a significant result or reached a relationship milestone.
- **Risk point:** The audience is at risk of disengaging, churning, or leaving.
- **Advocacy moment:** The audience is in a position to recommend or share.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Define the Context

Before consulting the matrix, answer four questions with specificity:

1. **Who exactly is the audience?** Not "marketers" but "VP of Marketing at a B2B SaaS company with 200-500 employees who has been in role for less than a year and is under pressure to show results quickly."
2. **What exactly do we want them to do?** Not "engage with our content" but "schedule a demo call within 48 hours."
3. **Where exactly will they encounter this?** Not "digital" but "a LinkedIn sponsored post appearing in their feed during a Monday morning scroll."
4. **When exactly in the relationship are they?** Not "prospect" but "they downloaded our whitepaper two weeks ago, attended one webinar, but have not responded to the SDR's email."

### Step 2: Consult the Matrix

Cross-reference the four axes to identify the optimal story type and approach.

**Primary Matrix (Audience State x Objective):**

| | Awareness | Consideration | Conversion | Retention | Advocacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Purpose Story | - | - | - | - |
| **Problem-aware** | Purpose Story, Founder Story | Value Story | - | - | - |
| **Solution-aware** | - | Value Story, Customer Story | Customer Story | - | - |
| **Product-aware** | - | - | Customer Story, Value Story | Founder Story | - |
| **Most aware** | - | - | - | Purpose Story, Founder Story | Customer Story, Purpose Story |

**Channel Modifier:**
- High-attention channel: Use full narrative arc (Gap-Bridge structure, Storyworthy craft)
- Medium-attention channel: Use compressed narrative (ABT structure with one expanded element)
- Low-attention channel: Use story fragment (a single striking image, quote, or moment from a larger story)
- Conversational channel: Use modular stories that can expand or contract based on audience response

**Timing Modifier:**
- First contact: Lead with the most universal, emotionally accessible story. Minimize product specifics.
- Evaluation: Lead with differentiation. What story can only THIS brand tell?
- Decision point: Lead with proof. Customer stories with specific outcomes.
- Onboarding: Lead with belonging. Founder story and purpose story create identity.
- Risk point: Lead with value reinforcement. Remind them why they chose this.
- Advocacy moment: Give them a story they can retell. Simplicity and memorability above all.

### Step 3: Select and Adapt

Based on the matrix output, select the specific story from your reservoir and adapt it to the context:

1. **Select the story** that best matches the matrix recommendation.
2. **Adapt the length** to the channel. A Customer Story for a keynote might be 5 minutes. The same story for LinkedIn might be 200 words. The same story for a display ad might be one sentence and an image.
3. **Adapt the emphasis** to the objective. If the objective is conversion, emphasize the proof and outcome. If the objective is awareness, emphasize the emotion and universality.
4. **Adapt the entry point** to the timing. A first-contact audience needs the full setup (Normal). A product-aware audience can skip the setup and start at the Explosion.

### Step 4: Test the Selection

Before deploying, run three tests:

**The "Wrong Story" Test:** Imagine telling a completely different story type in this context. Does it feel wrong? If swapping a Customer Story for a Purpose Story feels equally valid, the context definition is not specific enough. Go back to Step 1.

**The "Retell" Test:** If the audience encounters this story and then tells someone about it, what would they say? If the retelling naturally includes the brand's key message, the selection is right. If the retelling is emotionally powerful but disconnected from the brand, the selection may be wrong.

**The "So What" Test:** After encountering this story, will the audience be closer to the objective defined in Step 1? If the story is beautiful but does not move the audience toward the desired action, it is the wrong story for this context.

## Examples

### Example 1: Selecting a Story for a Conference Keynote

**Context:**
- Audience: 200 CMOs at a marketing technology conference. Solution-aware. Skeptical of vendor claims.
- Objective: Move from awareness to consideration. Get 30+ demo requests.
- Channel: High-attention (45-minute keynote with Q&A).
- Timing: First contact for most attendees.

**Matrix output:** Customer Story (solution-aware x consideration) delivered in full narrative arc (high-attention channel) with universal entry point (first contact timing).

**Selection:** A detailed customer story about a CMO who was equally skeptical, evaluated multiple solutions, chose this brand, and achieved specific measurable results. The story should mirror the audience's current state (skepticism) and show the journey to the new state (confidence and results). Craft using Storyworthy methodology for maximum emotional impact.

### Example 2: Selecting a Story for a Churn-Risk Email

**Context:**
- Audience: Customer who has not logged into the platform in 45 days. Was previously active. Product-aware. At risk of churning.
- Objective: Retention. Re-engage before they cancel.
- Channel: Medium-attention (email).
- Timing: Risk point.

**Matrix output:** Value Story (product-aware x retention) in compressed format (medium-attention channel) emphasizing re-engagement (risk point timing).

**Selection:** A brief Value Story about another customer who almost left, what brought them back, and what they would have missed. The story should be short (150-200 words), focus on a specific feature or use case the at-risk customer previously engaged with, and end with a low-friction CTA ("Just log in and see what's new").

### Example 3: Selecting a Story for Employee Onboarding

**Context:**
- Audience: New employee, first week. Excited but uncertain about culture and expectations.
- Objective: Alignment. Help them understand and embody the brand narrative.
- Channel: High-attention (onboarding presentation or welcome packet).
- Timing: Onboarding.

**Matrix output:** Founder Story + Purpose Story (alignment objective, onboarding timing). Full narrative arc (high-attention channel).

**Selection:** The founder's story told in full, using Gap-Bridge structure, followed by the Purpose Story that connects the founding motivation to the company's current mission. End with an invitation: "This is the story you are now part of. Your chapter starts today."

## When to Use

- Any time you have multiple stories and need to choose the right one for a specific context
- Content calendar planning — mapping story types to channels and timing across a quarter
- Sales enablement — equipping reps with the right story for each stage of the sales process
- Campaign planning — ensuring each touchpoint uses the optimal story type
- Multi-channel storytelling — adapting stories across channels without losing strategic alignment
- Event and conference preparation — selecting the right story for the audience and moment

## When NOT to Use

- When you do not yet have a reservoir of stories to choose from (use First/Last/Best/Worst or Stories That Stick to build the reservoir first)
- When you need to craft a story from scratch (use Storyworthy, Gap-Bridge, or Story Cycle)
- When the question is "how do I tell this story?" rather than "which story should I tell?" (use craft frameworks)
- When the communication does not require narrative (data presentations, technical documentation, status updates)

## Integration

- **Hall's Stories That Stick:** The 4 story types are the categories that populate the matrix. Build the portfolio first (Stories That Stick), then deploy strategically (Story Selection Matrix).
- **Hall's Gap-Bridge Framework:** Once a story is selected, use Gap-Bridge to structure it for the specific channel and audience.
- **Hall's Story Formula:** Use the formula to verify that the selected story has the right human elements for the context.
- **Howell's ABT Framework:** When the matrix recommends a story for a low-attention channel, use ABT to compress it to its essence.
- **Howell's Story Strategy Canvas:** The canvas's Master ABT and story elements provide the raw material that the matrix deploys.
- **Dicks' Storyworthy:** When the matrix selects a story that needs maximum emotional craft (keynotes, long-form content, video), use Storyworthy for development.
- **Dicks' First/Last/Best/Worst:** When the matrix reveals a gap — a context where no suitable story exists — use this exercise to discover one.

## Agent Routing

Use this framework when:
- The user has a specific context (audience, channel, timing) and needs to choose the right story
- The request involves content planning, campaign planning, or sales enablement
- The user asks "which story should I tell?" or "what type of story works here?"
- The task involves deploying stories across multiple channels or funnel stages
- The user mentions "story selection," "right story," "which story," or "story deployment"

Route to `hall-stories-that-stick` if the user does not yet have a story portfolio to select from.
Route to `hall-gap-bridge-framework` once a story is selected and needs structuring.
Route to `dicks-storyworthy` once a story is selected and needs maximum emotional craft.
Route to `howell-abt-framework` when the selected story needs compression for a low-attention channel.
