# Editing Compression Framework
> "Kill your darlings" methodology—identifying and removing everything that does not serve the story, and the art of saying more with less.

## Overview

William Faulkner's advice to "kill your darlings" is perhaps the most quoted and least practiced principle in writing. The Editing Compression Framework operationalizes it into a systematic process for tightening narrative content without losing its power.

Compression is not about making content shorter for its own sake. It is about increasing the ratio of meaning to words. A 3,000-word piece compressed to 1,500 words is not half the content—it is twice the density. Every sentence carries more weight. Every paragraph earns its place.

The framework draws from journalism's inverted pyramid discipline, poetry's economy of language, Hemingway's iceberg theory (the dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water), and direct-response copywriting's ruthless focus on conversion.

In marketing storytelling, compression is especially critical because attention is finite and expensive. Every unnecessary sentence is a leak in the attention funnel. The best marketers are not those who write the most—they are those who cut the most and keep only what is essential.

Compression targets vary by format: social media posts should be compressed to near-maximum density, while long-form thought leadership can afford more breathing room. The framework provides specific ratios and techniques for each context.

## Core Methodology

1. **Complete the First Draft**: Write generously. Get everything out. Compression is an editing act, not a drafting constraint.
2. **Identify the Spine**: Determine the one essential throughline the piece cannot survive without.
3. **Apply the Three-Pass Edit**: Remove in layers—structural cuts first, then paragraph-level, then sentence-level.
4. **Test Each Element**: For every remaining section, paragraph, and sentence, ask: "Does this serve the spine?"
5. **Measure Compression Ratio**: Compare first draft length to final length. Target ratios by format.
6. **Preserve Voice and Rhythm**: Compression should not flatten the writing. Density and music can coexist.
7. **Final Read-Aloud Test**: The compressed version should flow better, not worse, than the original.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Complete the First Draft
- Substep: Write without self-editing. Allow repetition, tangents, and overexplanation. The first draft is raw material, not finished product.
- Substep: Many writers resist this—they want to write clean first drafts. But clean first drafts are often thin. Generous first drafts give the editor (even if you are your own editor) more material to sculpt.
- Substep: Mark sections where you felt most energized while writing. These often contain the strongest material.

### Step 2: Identify the Spine
- Substep: State the piece's core purpose in one sentence. This is the spine.
- Substep: If using the Theme-Premise-Controlling Idea framework, the Controlling Idea IS the spine.
- Substep: If using the Logline Formula, the logline IS the spine.
- Substep: Write the spine at the top of the document. Every editing decision references it.

### Step 3: First Pass — Structural Cuts
- Substep: Read the entire piece at normal speed. Do not stop to edit sentences.
- Substep: Mark entire sections that do not serve the spine. Be honest—does this section advance the argument, deepen the character, or provide essential proof? If not, cut the section.
- Substep: Look for structural redundancy—sections that make the same point in different ways. Keep the strongest version, cut the rest.
- Substep: Look for throat-clearing—introductory sections that exist because the writer needed a warm-up. The real beginning is often 2-3 paragraphs into the draft.
- Substep: Structural cuts typically remove 20-40% of a first draft.

### Step 4: Second Pass — Paragraph Cuts
- Substep: Within each surviving section, examine each paragraph.
- Substep: Cut paragraphs that repeat information already established.
- Substep: Cut paragraphs that provide context the audience already has.
- Substep: Cut paragraphs that hedge, qualify, or apologize for the piece's own argument.
- Substep: Merge short paragraphs that address the same point into single, stronger paragraphs.
- Substep: Paragraph cuts typically remove 10-20% of remaining content.

### Step 5: Third Pass — Sentence and Word Cuts
- Substep: Eliminate adverbs that do not change the meaning: "very," "really," "extremely," "quite," "somewhat."
- Substep: Replace weak verb + adverb combinations with strong verbs: "walked slowly" becomes "crept." "Said loudly" becomes "shouted."
- Substep: Cut prepositional phrase chains: "In terms of the perspective of the customer" becomes "from the customer's perspective" or simply "customers see."
- Substep: Remove hedging language: "I think that," "It seems like," "In my opinion." State claims directly.
- Substep: Cut meta-commentary: "In this section, we will discuss..." Just discuss it.
- Substep: Eliminate cliche phrases: "at the end of the day," "move the needle," "low-hanging fruit," "game-changer." Replace with specific language.
- Substep: Sentence-level cuts typically remove 10-15% of remaining content.

### Step 6: Measure Compression Ratio
Target ratios by format:
- **Social media posts**: 3:1 to 5:1 (write 300 words, publish 60-100 words)
- **Email copy**: 2:1 to 3:1 (write 1,000 words, publish 350-500 words)
- **Blog posts**: 1.5:1 to 2:1 (write 3,000 words, publish 1,500-2,000 words)
- **Sales pages**: 1.3:1 to 1.5:1 (write 4,000 words, publish 2,700-3,000 words)
- **Long-form thought leadership**: 1.2:1 to 1.5:1 (write 5,000 words, publish 3,300-4,200 words)
- **Video scripts**: 2:1 to 3:1 (scripts must be tight because production is expensive)

### Step 7: Preserve Voice and Rhythm
- Substep: After compression, read the piece aloud. Does it still sound like a person talking, or does it sound like a telegram?
- Substep: Compression should remove fat, not muscle. If a sentence contributes to rhythm, pacing, or voice—even if it does not directly serve the spine—consider keeping it.
- Substep: Vary sentence length after compression. All short sentences create a staccato monotony. Mix short punches with longer flowing sentences.
- Substep: Preserve one or two "darlings"—lines that are beautiful or surprising even if not strictly necessary. Total efficiency kills personality.

### Step 8: Final Read-Aloud Test
- Substep: Read the compressed version from beginning to end without stopping.
- Substep: Mark any moment where the flow breaks, the logic gaps, or the energy drops.
- Substep: These moments indicate where compression went too far—restore a connecting sentence or transition.
- Substep: The compressed version should feel faster, cleaner, and more energetic than the original.

## Examples

### Example 1: Email Opening Compression

**First Draft (87 words):**
"I want to share something with you today that I think is really important. Over the past few months, I have been doing a lot of thinking about the way that most people approach the challenge of building an email list. And what I have come to realize is that the conventional wisdom about this topic is not just slightly wrong—it is fundamentally backwards. In this email, I am going to explain why and show you a better approach."

**Compressed (31 words):**
"Most advice about building an email list is not just wrong—it is backwards. Here is what nobody talks about, and why it changes everything about how you should approach list growth."

### Example 2: Case Study Paragraph Compression

**First Draft (94 words):**
"When the team at Meridian first started looking for a solution to their customer onboarding problem, they were not sure exactly what they needed. They had tried several different approaches over the previous year and a half, including building their own internal tool, hiring additional support staff, and creating a comprehensive knowledge base. None of these approaches had delivered the results they were hoping for. The customer onboarding process still took an average of 21 days, which was causing significant churn in their customer base."

**Compressed (42 words):**
"Meridian had tried everything to fix onboarding: an internal tool, more support staff, a knowledge base. Nothing worked. Customers still took 21 days to get started, and the churn rate proved it—they were losing people before they ever experienced the product."

## When to Use

- Every piece of content benefits from at least one compression pass. No exceptions.
- After the structural framework and beats are in place but before final polish.
- When content exceeds the target length for its format.
- When engagement metrics (bounce rate, watch time, read-through rate) suggest attention loss.
- During content repurposing—compressing a blog post into an email, a webinar into a video clip.

## When NOT to Use

- During first-draft writing. Compressing while drafting causes paralysis and thin content.
- When the content is already at or below minimum viable length for its format.
- When the audience expects and rewards depth (academic content, detailed technical guides, book-length content).
- When compression would remove essential proof or narrative beats—cut words, not evidence.

## Integration

- **But-Therefore Causality**: Run the But-Therefore test before compression. "And then" connections often indicate cuttable material.
- **Story Beats and Scenes**: Beat mapping reveals which beats are redundant or non-functional—compression targets.
- **Proof in Narrative Framework**: Protect proof density during compression. Cut narrative filler before cutting evidence.
- **Three-Act Structure**: Audit compression by act. Act I and Act III are most vulnerable to bloat. Act II needs room to breathe.
- **Show Don't Tell Framework**: During compression, "telling" passages are prime cut targets. "Showing" passages earn their length.

## Agent Routing

- **Primary Owner**: Editor Agent — executes all compression passes and maintains quality standards.
- **Assisting Agents**:
  - Story Architect Agent — identifies structural cuts and protects essential narrative beats.
  - Copywriter Agent — preserves voice and rhythm during sentence-level compression.
  - Quality Assurance Agent — verifies that compression has not introduced logic gaps or factual errors.
  - Performance Analyst Agent — uses engagement data to calibrate compression targets by format and channel.
