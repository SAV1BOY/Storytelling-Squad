# Hiring / Employer Brand Story Framework
> Attract talent through narratives of company origin, culture, growth opportunity, impact, and invitation.

## Overview
The best candidates do not choose a company for the salary or the perks. They choose a story they want to be part of. Employer brand storytelling transforms job postings and career pages from feature lists (benefits, tech stack, office amenities) into narratives that answer the candidate's real question: "Will this place help me become who I want to be?" This framework provides a five-beat narrative arc for employer brand content: the company's origin (why we exist), culture (how we work), growth (how people develop here), impact (what your work means), and invitation (your role in the story). When done well, employer brand stories attract candidates who are already aligned with the mission before they even apply.

## Core Methodology
The Employer Brand Story Framework operates on five narrative beats:

1. **Origin** — Why the company exists. Not the corporate history but the human story of founding. Candidates who connect with the origin story self-select for mission alignment.

2. **Culture** — How people actually work here, told through specific stories rather than value statements. "We value innovation" means nothing. "Last month, a junior engineer challenged our CTO's architecture decision in front of the whole team — and the CTO changed the plan" means everything.

3. **Growth** — How people develop, advance, and become better at their craft. Real stories of employee trajectories: where they started, what they learned, where they are now.

4. **Impact** — What the work actually means. Stories that connect the candidate's potential role to a tangible outcome in the world. Engineers need to know their code matters to real humans.

5. **Invitation** — A direct, specific call to join. Not "we are hiring" but "here is the role, here is why it matters right now, and here is what your first 90 days would look like."

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Build the Origin Narrative for Recruiting
- Adapt the founder story (from the Founder Story Framework) for a recruiting audience
- Focus on the element of the origin that speaks to the type of talent you want to attract
- For engineering candidates: emphasize the technical challenge that sparked the company
- For creative candidates: emphasize the vision and the design philosophy
- For sales candidates: emphasize the market opportunity and the customer problem
- Keep the origin to 2-3 paragraphs — enough to create emotional resonance, not so much that it overwhelms

### Step 2: Collect and Curate Culture Stories
- Interview 8-12 employees across roles, levels, and tenure about specific cultural moments
- Ask: "Tell me about a time something happened here that would not have happened at your previous job."
- Ask: "What surprised you most about working here — good or bad?"
- Ask: "What is something we do that you brag about to your friends?"
- Select stories that demonstrate actual cultural practices, not aspirational values
- Include stories that show imperfection — candidates trust companies that acknowledge they are still evolving
- Organize stories by the value they illustrate: psychological safety, ownership, learning, collaboration, candor

### Step 3: Map Growth Trajectories
- Identify 3-5 employees whose growth trajectory exemplifies what is possible at the company
- For each person, tell the story: "When [name] joined, they were [starting point]. In [timeframe], they [growth milestones]. Today, they [current role/impact]."
- Include the mechanisms that enabled growth: mentorship, project opportunities, learning budgets, internal mobility
- Be specific about skills gained and challenges overcome — vague growth stories are not believable
- Include at least one non-traditional trajectory: someone who pivoted roles, someone who joined without the "typical" background, someone who grew faster than expected

### Step 4: Connect Roles to Impact
- For every open role, write an impact narrative: "In this role, you will [specific work]. That work directly affects [specific outcome]. For example, the person in this role last quarter [specific story of impact]."
- Use customer stories to illustrate impact: "The feature your team builds is used by 50,000 teachers every morning. One of them told us it changed how she starts every school day."
- Quantify impact where possible: "Our sales team does not just hit quotas — they bring a product to families who need it. Last year, that meant 12,000 new families with access to affordable health monitoring."
- Impact stories are especially powerful for candidates leaving larger companies where they felt disconnected from outcomes

### Step 5: Craft the Invitation
- The invitation is not a job posting — it is a narrative bridge between the company's story and the candidate's story
- Structure: "We are at [current chapter of the company story]. The challenge ahead is [specific challenge]. We need [type of person] who [specific capability]. Here is what your first 90 days would look like."
- Make the invitation specific: "In week one, you will meet the team and audit our current funnel. By month two, you will own the experimentation roadmap. By month three, you will have run your first set of tests."
- Include the honest challenge: "This role is hard because [real challenge]. We are not looking for someone who avoids hard problems — we are looking for someone who runs toward them."
- End with a personal touch: "If this sounds like your kind of challenge, I want to hear from you. — [Name, Title]"

### Step 6: Deploy Across Recruiting Touchpoints
- **Career page**: Full five-beat narrative as the anchor content. Origin, culture stories, growth profiles, impact examples, open role invitations.
- **Job postings**: Each posting includes a 2-paragraph impact narrative specific to the role, plus a link to the full career page story.
- **LinkedIn**: Weekly employer brand stories — alternating between culture moments, employee spotlights, and impact stories.
- **Recruiting outreach**: Cold messages that reference the company story, not just the role. "I am reaching out because we are building [mission] and your experience in [area] is exactly what this next chapter needs."
- **Interview process**: Interviewers share the company story during the first conversation. Candidates who connect with the narrative self-select forward.

## Examples

### Example 1: Series B Startup — Developer Tools
- **Origin**: "We started this company because every developer on our previous team wasted 2 hours a day fighting their tools instead of building. We decided the tools should fight for the developer, not against them."
- **Culture**: "Last sprint, a new hire in their third week proposed scrapping a feature that had been in development for two months. They presented data showing users did not want it. The team debated for an hour. The feature was killed. That new hire just got promoted."
- **Growth**: "Priya joined as our 11th engineer, fresh from a bootcamp with no production experience. In 18 months, she led the team that built our most-used feature. She told me, 'I learned more in my first 6 months here than in my entire previous career.'"
- **Impact**: "Every time a developer pushes code through our pipeline, they save an average of 45 minutes. Across our user base, that is 2.3 million hours saved per month. Hours that went back to building, not waiting."
- **Invitation**: "We are hiring a senior backend engineer to own our API layer. You will be responsible for the surface that 400,000 developers interact with daily. In your first month, you will ship to production. By month three, you will own a roadmap."

### Example 2: Growth-Stage Company — Healthcare
- **Origin**: "Our founder spent two years watching her mother navigate a broken healthcare system. She built the first version of our platform on a laptop in her mother's hospital room. That laptop is still in the office. It reminds us who we build for."
- **Culture**: "We start every Monday with a 'patient story' — a real message from a user about how the platform affected their health journey. It is not a feel-good exercise. It is a design constraint. Every decision we make has to survive the question: 'Would this help or hurt the patient we heard about on Monday?'"
- **Growth**: "Marcus joined our customer success team 3 years ago. He noticed patterns in support tickets that no one else was tracking. He built a simple dashboard. That dashboard became our product analytics strategy. Marcus now leads a team of 6 product analysts."
- **Impact**: "The notification you build might be the one that reminds a 72-year-old man to take his blood pressure medication. His daughter told us our app is the reason she sleeps at night."
- **Invitation**: "We are looking for a product designer who cares about accessibility and clarity. Your users will include people who have never used a smartphone app before. If simplifying the complex is what excites you, let us talk."

## When to Use
- Career page development and redesign
- Job posting creation
- Recruiting outreach campaigns
- Employer brand content for social media
- Interview process design
- Onboarding materials that reinforce the employer brand promise

### Employer Brand Pitfalls
- **The Culture Lie**: Presenting a culture that does not match reality. Candidates who join based on a false employer brand story leave within 6 months and leave bitter Glassdoor reviews that make future recruiting harder.
- **The Perk Parade**: Leading with ping pong tables, free snacks, and unlimited PTO instead of meaningful stories about work, growth, and impact. Perks attract tourists. Stories attract missionaries.
- **The Generic Career Page**: "We are a fast-growing company with a great culture" describes every company's career page. Without specific stories, names, and details, the employer brand is invisible.
- **Ignoring the Hard Parts**: Pretending the job has no challenges. The best candidates want to know what is hard about the role so they can decide if it is their kind of hard. Honesty about difficulty attracts resilient people.
- **One-Size-Fits-All**: Using the same employer brand narrative for every role and every audience. An engineering candidate needs a different story than a sales candidate. Customize by role and seniority.
- **No Employee Voice**: Telling the company story exclusively through the founder or marketing team. Candidates trust employee voices more than corporate voices. Let employees tell their own stories.

## When NOT to Use
- When the internal culture does not match the external narrative — fabricated employer brand stories backfire spectacularly on Glassdoor
- When the company is in active turmoil (layoffs, leadership changes) and a polished story would feel tone-deaf
- When the role is purely transactional and the candidate cares only about compensation
- When the company is too early to have meaningful culture or growth stories
- When leadership has not committed to the culture the employer brand promises

## Integration
- Origin draws from the **Founder Story Framework**
- Culture stories use the **Internal Communications Story Framework** as a source
- Impact stories connect to the **Case Study Story Framework** and customer proof
- Invitation structure adapts the **Pitch Story Framework** for recruiting
- Connects to the **Discovery Layer** for employee interviews and story mining

## Agent Routing
- **Discovery Agent**: Conducts employee interviews, collects culture stories, and mines growth trajectories
- **Strategy Agent**: Defines employer brand positioning and target candidate profiles
- **Drafting Agent**: Writes career page content, job postings, and recruiting outreach
- **Editing Agent**: Ensures authenticity, removes corporate jargon, strengthens emotional impact
- **Distribution Agent**: Plans employer brand content across LinkedIn, career page, and recruiting channels
- Route to this framework when the task involves attracting talent through narrative — career pages, job posts, recruiting content, or employer brand strategy

### Employer Brand Effectiveness Metrics
- **Application quality**: Are candidates referencing the company story in their cover letters or interviews? This indicates narrative resonance.
- **Offer acceptance rate**: Do candidates who engage with employer brand content accept offers at a higher rate?
- **Time to fill**: Are story-rich job postings filling faster than standard postings?
