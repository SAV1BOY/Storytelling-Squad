# The StoryBrand SB7 Framework
> A seven-part storytelling framework that positions your customer as the hero and your brand as the guide, producing clear messaging that drives action.

## Overview

The SB7 Framework is Donald Miller's foundational contribution to brand messaging. Rooted in classical narrative structure, it maps the seven universal story elements onto the brand-customer relationship. The core insight is counterintuitive for most businesses: **your brand is not the hero of the story — your customer is.** When companies position themselves as the hero, they compete with the customer for the role, creating confusion and disengagement. When companies position themselves as the guide, they tap into the most powerful narrative force in human psychology: the mentor archetype.

Every compelling story follows the same pattern. A CHARACTER who wants something encounters a PROBLEM before they can get it. At the peak of their despair, a GUIDE steps into their lives, gives them a PLAN, and CALLS THEM TO ACTION. That action either ends in SUCCESS or FAILURE. Businesses that communicate using this framework cut through noise because the human brain is wired to receive information structured this way.

The SB7 Framework is the most widely adopted brand messaging framework in modern marketing. It has been used by over 10,000 businesses ranging from solopreneurs to Fortune 500 companies.

## Core Methodology

The framework operates on three psychological principles:

**1. Story as Survival Mechanism**
The human brain processes story faster and more deeply than any other form of communication. Stories are how we simulate threats and opportunities without real-world risk. When a brand tells a story with the customer at the center, the brain engages survival-processing pathways — making the message feel urgent and relevant.

**2. The Noise Problem**
The average person encounters 3,000+ marketing messages per day. Most fail because they are confusing. Confused people do not buy. The SB7 Framework forces radical clarity: every sentence must serve one of seven defined story functions, and anything that does not serve the story gets cut.

**3. The Guide Archetype**
In almost every story, the hero cannot solve the problem alone. They need a guide — a character who has been where they are and has the competence to help them win. Yoda for Luke Skywalker. Haymitch for Katniss. This is the role your brand must play: empathetic (you understand their pain) and authoritative (you have the solution).

### The Seven Elements

| Element | Story Role | Brand Application |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Character | The hero with a desire | Your customer, defined by a specific want |
| 2. Problem | The conflict blocking the desire | The pain point your product addresses |
| 3. Guide | The mentor who appears | Your brand, showing empathy + authority |
| 4. Plan | The path forward | Your simple process or agreement |
| 5. Call to Action | The moment of commitment | Your direct and transitional CTAs |
| 6. Failure | The stakes | What happens if they do NOT act |
| 7. Success | The resolution | The transformed life after your product |

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: A Character (The Customer Has a Desire)

The story starts when the hero wants something. In brand messaging, the character is always the customer, and the desire must be clearly defined and survival-related. Miller identifies four categories of survival-related desire:

- **Conserving financial resources** — saving money, making money, building wealth
- **Conserving time** — efficiency, speed, convenience
- **Building social networks** — belonging, status, community
- **Gaining status** — recognition, power, self-actualization
- **Accumulating resources** — tools, knowledge, assets
- **The innate desire to be generous** — legacy, contribution, meaning
- **The desire for meaning** — purpose, significance, identity

**Key principle:** Define ONE dominant desire. Do not list six desires hoping one sticks. Specificity is clarity, and clarity converts.

**Example (Financial SaaS):** "Business owners who want to stop losing money to manual invoicing errors."

**Example (Fitness brand):** "Busy parents who want to feel strong and energized without spending two hours in the gym."

### Step 2: Has a Problem (External, Internal, Philosophical)

A story only gets interesting when a problem enters. Miller breaks problems into three levels, and most businesses only address the first:

**External Problem:** The tangible, surface-level issue. This is what the customer would describe to a friend. "My invoicing software is slow." "I don't have time to cook healthy meals."

**Internal Problem:** The emotional frustration the external problem causes. This is what the customer actually wants solved. "I feel incompetent when my invoices have errors." "I feel guilty feeding my kids fast food."

**Internal problems drive purchase decisions.** People buy solutions to internal problems and justify the purchase with the external solution. Apple does not sell computers (external). Apple sells the feeling of being innovative and creative (internal).

**Philosophical Problem:** The "ought" and "should" statement. Why this problem is fundamentally wrong in the world. "Nobody should have to waste 10 hours a week on paperwork that software could handle." "Every parent deserves access to quick, healthy meals."

**The philosophical problem elevates the brand from a vendor to a cause.** It gives the customer a sense of moral rightness in choosing you.

### Step 3: And Meets a Guide (Brand With Empathy + Authority)

The guide enters the story with two critical qualities:

**Empathy:** The guide must demonstrate that they understand the hero's pain. This is not sympathy ("I feel sorry for you") but empathy ("I know what you're going through"). Empathic statements sound like: "We know how frustrating it is when..." or "You shouldn't have to deal with..."

**Authority:** The guide must demonstrate competence. Four tools for expressing authority:
1. **Testimonials** — Other heroes who have succeeded with this guide
2. **Statistics** — Data proving the guide's competence
3. **Awards and recognition** — Third-party validation
4. **Logos and partnerships** — Association with known entities

**The balance matters.** Too much empathy without authority makes the brand seem weak. Too much authority without empathy makes the brand seem cold. The sweet spot is leading with empathy and supporting with authority.

### Step 4: Who Gives Them a Plan (3-Step Process or Agreement)

The hero is now interested but hesitant. The guide must reduce confusion and perceived risk by offering a plan. Miller defines two types:

**Process Plan:** A simple 3-4 step roadmap showing the customer how to engage. Steps should be short and clear. Example: "1. Schedule a call. 2. Get a custom plan. 3. See results in 30 days."

**Agreement Plan:** A list of commitments the brand makes to alleviate fears. Example: "We guarantee on-time delivery. We offer a full refund if you're not satisfied. We'll never share your data."

**Critical rule:** The plan should have no more than 3-4 steps. More than that reintroduces complexity and confusion. The purpose of the plan is to reduce cognitive load, not to be comprehensive.

### Step 5: And Calls Them to Action (Direct CTA + Transitional CTA)

Most businesses are passive about asking for the sale. Miller insists on two types of CTAs:

**Direct CTA:** The explicit ask. "Buy Now." "Schedule a Call." "Start Your Free Trial." This should be visually prominent (button on the website, bold in the email) and repeated throughout messaging. Miller's rule: if you feel like you're being too aggressive, you're probably just being clear enough for the first time.

**Transitional CTA:** A softer ask for those not ready to buy. "Download our free guide." "Watch the demo." "Take our quiz." Transitional CTAs build the relationship and move the customer closer to the direct CTA. They also serve as lead generators — the customer exchanges their email for value.

### Step 6: That Helps Them Avoid Failure (The Stakes)

Stories without stakes are boring. Messaging without stakes lacks urgency. The brand must clearly articulate what happens if the customer does NOT act.

**Failure must be specific, not vague.** Not "you'll miss out" but "you'll continue losing $2,000/month to invoicing errors" or "you'll keep feeling exhausted by 3 PM because your nutrition isn't fueling you."

**Use failure sparingly.** Miller recommends a "salt on a recipe" approach. Too little and the message is bland. Too much and it becomes fear-mongering, which erodes trust. A single clear failure statement is usually sufficient.

### Step 7: And Ends in Success (Specific Transformation)

The story must end with a specific, vivid picture of the customer's life after using the product. Miller warns against vague success statements ("achieve your dreams") and pushes for concrete outcomes.

**Three types of success to communicate:**
1. **Status transformation** — How will others see them differently? "Your team will see you as the organized, in-control leader."
2. **Emotional resolution** — How will they feel? "You'll finally stop worrying about payroll at 2 AM."
3. **External achievement** — What measurable outcome will they have? "Save 10 hours per week on administrative tasks."

**The before-and-after grid:** Create a simple two-column comparison. Before: stressed, confused, losing money. After: confident, clear, profitable. This grid becomes a messaging goldmine.

## Examples

### Example 1: B2B Project Management Software

| SB7 Element | Application |
|-------------|-------------|
| Character | Marketing team leads overwhelmed by scattered project communication |
| External Problem | Projects are tracked across email, Slack, spreadsheets, and sticky notes |
| Internal Problem | They feel anxious that something will fall through the cracks and they'll look incompetent |
| Philosophical Problem | No team should lose a client because their tools failed them |
| Guide (Empathy) | "We've seen what happens when talented teams are let down by chaotic workflows" |
| Guide (Authority) | "Trusted by 5,000+ marketing teams. G2 Leader for 3 consecutive years." |
| Plan | 1. Import your projects (takes 10 minutes). 2. Invite your team. 3. Hit your next deadline without a single missed task. |
| Direct CTA | "Start your free trial" |
| Transitional CTA | "Download: The Project Management Checklist for Marketing Teams" |
| Failure | "Without a single source of truth, you'll keep losing hours to status update meetings and risk missing the deadlines that matter most." |
| Success | "Your team executes flawlessly. You leave work on time. Your clients rave about your reliability." |

### Example 2: Direct-to-Consumer Meal Kit Brand

| SB7 Element | Application |
|-------------|-------------|
| Character | Working parents who want to provide healthy dinners without the stress |
| External Problem | No time to plan meals, shop for groceries, and cook from scratch every night |
| Internal Problem | Guilt about relying on takeout and frozen meals for the family |
| Philosophical Problem | Families deserve to eat well together without one parent sacrificing their entire evening |
| Guide (Empathy) | "We're parents too. We know the 5 PM panic of 'what's for dinner?'" |
| Guide (Authority) | "Recipes developed by registered dietitians. 500,000 families served. 4.8-star average rating." |
| Plan | 1. Pick your meals for the week. 2. We deliver pre-portioned ingredients. 3. Cook a healthy dinner in 20 minutes. |
| Direct CTA | "Get 50% off your first box" |
| Transitional CTA | "Download 5 free 15-minute family recipes" |
| Failure | "Another week of drive-throughs and 'I'll start eating better on Monday' promises that never happen." |
| Success | "Your family gathers around the table. The kids try new foods. You feel proud of what you're providing — without the exhaustion." |

## When to Use

- **Website messaging and wireframing** — the SB7 maps directly to homepage sections
- **Sales pages and landing pages** — the narrative arc drives conversion
- **Brand messaging guides** — the BrandScript (see `miller-brand-script.md`) is built on SB7
- **Pitch decks** — investors and stakeholders respond to narrative structure
- **Email sequences** — each email can correspond to an SB7 element
- **Taglines and one-liners** — SB7 condenses into short-form messaging
- **Video scripts** — the story arc maps to video narrative structure
- **Any context where you need to clarify what your brand offers and why it matters**

## When NOT to Use

- **Technical documentation** — SB7 is persuasion, not instruction
- **Internal operational communication** — it is customer-facing by design
- **When the audience already understands the problem deeply** — SB7 excels at clarity for uninformed or mildly aware audiences; expert audiences may find it reductive
- **Crisis communication** — the hero/guide dynamic can feel tone-deaf in crisis contexts
- **Regulatory or compliance content** — narrative framing may conflict with required disclosures
- **When you need analytical depth** — SB7 simplifies by design; for nuanced strategic arguments, consider Duarte's Sparkline (see `duarte-resonate-sparkline.md`)

## Integration

The SB7 Framework is the anchor point for all Miller frameworks:

- **One-Liner** (`miller-one-liner.md`): A compression of SB7 into a single sentence — Problem + Solution + Result
- **BrandScript** (`miller-brand-script.md`): The full expansion of SB7 into a living messaging document
- **Marketing Roadmap** (`miller-marketing-roadmap.md`): The implementation sequence that deploys SB7 across all channels
- **Grunt Test** (`miller-grunt-test.md`): The diagnostic that validates whether SB7 has been applied clearly enough

SB7 also pairs powerfully with Duarte frameworks. Use SB7 for the messaging strategy and Duarte's Sparkline for presentation delivery. Use SB7 for the content and Duarte's S.T.A.R. Moment for the peak emotional beat.
