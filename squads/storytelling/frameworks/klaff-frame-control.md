# Klaff Frame Control
> The 4 frames that govern every persuasive interaction and the mechanics for winning frame collisions.

## Overview

Oren Klaff defines a "frame" as the instrument of power in every interpersonal interaction. A frame is the unspoken set of assumptions, expectations, and power dynamics that define "what is really happening here." When two people meet, their frames collide. The stronger frame absorbs the weaker frame. The person whose frame dominates controls the interaction — they set the rules, define what matters, and determine the outcome.

Frame control is not manipulation. It is awareness. Frames exist whether you manage them or not. In every meeting, negotiation, pitch, and conversation, frames are operating. The only question is whether you are consciously managing your frame or unconsciously surrendering to someone else's.

Klaff identifies four primary frames that operate in business and persuasion contexts. Each has a counter-frame. Mastery of frame control means recognizing which frame the other party is running, deploying the appropriate counter-frame, and maintaining your frame under pressure.

This framework is the single most important concept in Klaff's system. The STRONG method is the pitch architecture, but frame control is the operating system that makes every phase of STRONG work.

## Core Methodology

### Frame 1: The Power Frame

**What it is**: The Power Frame is deployed by someone who holds (or claims) positional authority. It says: "I am more important than you. My time is more valuable. You should be grateful for this meeting."

**How it manifests**: The person with the power frame makes you wait, cuts you short, takes calls during your presentation, delegates to a subordinate mid-meeting, or opens with "You've got five minutes — go."

**The counter-frame — Disruptive Frame**: You cannot fight power with power (that creates an unproductive collision). Instead, you disrupt the frame with a small, unexpected act that breaks the expected pattern:
- Use mild, controlled humor that reframes the power dynamic
- Deny the premise of their power display ("Actually, I'm the one with limited time today")
- Use a prop, a story, or a physical action that pulls attention away from their frame
- Refuse to perform the expected submission behaviors (don't rush, don't apologize, don't shrink)

The key principle: a power frame is only powerful if you accept it. The moment you behave as though the power dynamic is anything other than what they've asserted, their frame weakens.

### Frame 2: The Time Frame

**What it is**: The Time Frame creates artificial urgency or, conversely, uses delay as a power tool. "We need to decide by Friday" is an offensive time frame. "We'll get back to you in a few weeks" is a defensive time frame. Both are about controlling the pace of the interaction.

**How it manifests**: The audience imposes deadlines, shortens meeting times, delays responses, or creates hurdles that consume your time (additional meetings, more documentation, committee reviews).

**The counter-frame — Your Own Time Constraint**: Deploy your own time frame first. Before they can limit your time, limit your own: "I've got about 20 minutes, so let me get right to the core of this." This accomplishes three things:
1. It prevents them from shortening your time (you already did it)
2. It creates scarcity for your attention (you're the busy one)
3. It forces you to be concise, which the primitive brain rewards

Against delay tactics, the counter is withdrawing: "I understand you need time. Let me check whether we can still accommodate your timeline given our other commitments." This reframes delay from their power move to a potential loss for them.

### Frame 3: The Intrigue Frame

**What it is**: The Intrigue Frame captures attention through curiosity and unresolved tension. It says: "There is something here you don't know but want to know." It is the only frame that works purely through attraction rather than power dynamics.

**How it manifests**: In your pitch, the intrigue frame is the moment where you introduce something unexpected — a twist, a counterintuitive insight, a behind-the-scenes story that hasn't been told. The audience's primitive brain, designed to track unresolved patterns, locks onto the intrigue and demands resolution.

**How to deploy it**: The intrigue frame is not a counter-frame — it is a universal engagement tool. Deploy it when:
- Attention is fading (re-engage the primitive brain with novelty)
- The conversation has become too analytical (shift from neocortex back to emotional engagement)
- You need to buy time (an intriguing story pauses the other frames while the audience listens)
- You want to demonstrate depth (the quality of your intrigue signals the quality of your insight)

**Structure of effective intrigue**:
1. Set up a situation with clear stakes
2. Introduce a turning point or complication
3. Build tension — do NOT resolve immediately
4. Let the audience sit in the tension for a moment
5. Resolve with an insight that connects back to your main pitch

### Frame 4: The Prize Frame

**What it is**: The Prize Frame is Klaff's most powerful and distinctive concept. In any transaction, someone is the prize and someone is the commodity. The person who is the prize sets the terms. The commodity person adapts to those terms.

In most pitch situations, the default frame positions the pitcher as the commodity (one of many seeking investment/partnership/approval) and the audience as the prize (the one being courted). The Prize Frame inverts this dynamic entirely.

**How to deploy it**:
- Evaluate the audience openly: "I'm trying to determine if this is the right partnership for us"
- Reference alternative demand: "We've had significant interest from several firms in your space"
- Set your own criteria: "We only work with partners who can contribute X, Y, and Z"
- Withdraw attention strategically: after making a strong point, pull back rather than pressing forward
- Never chase: if the audience isn't leaning in, don't lean further toward them

**How it manifests behaviorally**: The prize frame is maintained through high-status behaviors (Johnstone's framework) combined with genuine selectivity. The audience must believe that you have real alternatives and real standards — because you should. If you don't, build them before you pitch.

### Frame Collisions

When two frames meet, three things can happen:

1. **One frame absorbs the other**: The stronger frame dominates. The weaker frame holder unconsciously adopts the assumptions of the stronger frame. This is the typical outcome when frames are mismatched in strength.

2. **Frames bounce off each other**: Neither frame gains traction. The conversation feels awkward, disconnected, or adversarial. Both parties leave feeling unsatisfied. This happens when both frames are strong but incompatible.

3. **Frames merge**: Both parties find a shared frame. This is the ideal outcome for collaborative contexts — but in competitive contexts (pitching, negotiating), frame merger usually means the more assertive frame has won.

**Rules for frame collisions**:
- The frame that stays calm wins. Emotional reactivity signals a weak frame.
- The frame that controls the physical space wins. Whoever sets the seating arrangement, the room temperature, and the agenda has a frame advantage.
- The frame that names what is happening wins. "I notice we're getting into a negotiation here — let me step back and make sure we agree on the fundamentals first." Naming the dynamic is a meta-frame that supersedes the operating frame.
- The frame that disengages first wins. Being willing to walk away is the ultimate frame strength.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Pre-Meeting Frame Analysis
Before any important interaction, map the likely frames:
- What frame will the other party run? (Power, time, analytical, etc.)
- What is my default frame? (Am I entering as supplicant, peer, authority, or prize?)
- What frame do I want to control the interaction? (This should align with your strategic objective)
- What is my counter-frame if they run a power frame? A time frame?

### Step 2: Set Your Frame Early
The first 60-120 seconds establish the frame. Deploy your frame-setting behaviors immediately:
- Physical: Take space, move deliberately, choose your seat intentionally
- Temporal: State your time constraint before they state theirs
- Status: Establish credentials briefly but confidently
- Agenda: Define what you'll cover and why it matters to THEM

### Step 3: Recognize Frame Challenges
During the interaction, watch for frame challenges:
- Interruptions (power frame assertion)
- Phone checking (power frame / disinterest)
- "We'll need to run this by..." (time frame / power dilution)
- Excessive technical questions (analytical frame attempting to pull you out of your narrative frame)
- Price or terms discussion before value is established (commodity frame)

### Step 4: Deploy Counter-Frames
When a frame challenge occurs:
- Do NOT react emotionally (this signals weak frame)
- Do NOT accommodate automatically (this concedes the frame)
- Pause briefly (composure signals frame strength)
- Deploy the appropriate counter-frame from your preparation
- Continue as if the challenge was minor (frame strength is conveyed through what you treat as unimportant)

### Step 5: Maintain Frame Under Pressure
The most common failure point is frame maintenance during Q&A or objection handling. When challenged:
- Reframe questions as validation: "Great question — it shows you're seeing the depth of the opportunity"
- Answer from within your frame, not theirs: if they're running an analytical frame, acknowledge the data point but redirect to your narrative
- Use the intrigue frame as a bridge: "That connects to something interesting we've been seeing..."

### Step 6: Close Within Your Frame
The close must happen while your frame is still dominant. If the frame has shifted against you, re-establish before attempting to close. Closing from a weak frame produces weak commitments.

## Examples

### Example 1: Frame Control in a Partnership Negotiation
A marketing agency was pitching a large enterprise client. The client opened with a power frame: the meeting was moved to a small conference room, the decision-maker sent a subordinate for the first 15 minutes, and the brief mentioned they were "evaluating several agencies."

The agency principal deployed a prize frame counter: "We evaluated the brief carefully and almost passed — your category isn't our core focus. But the data challenge you described in section three is genuinely interesting and it's a problem we've solved in adjacent categories. Let me show you how." This reframed the dynamic from "agency competes for client" to "agency evaluates whether client is interesting enough."

When the decision-maker arrived 15 minutes late, the principal said: "Perfect timing — I just finished the context. Let me jump to the part that will matter most to you." She didn't restart the presentation (which would have conceded the power frame) and didn't acknowledge the lateness (which would have accepted the disrespect). She treated it as a non-event while controlling the agenda.

The agency won the account against two larger competitors. The client later said, "They were the only ones who seemed like they didn't need us — which made us want them more."

### Example 2: Frame Control in Content Marketing Strategy
A SaaS company applied frame control principles to their content strategy. Their previous approach was commodity-frame content: "Here are 10 tips for..." articles that positioned the brand as a helpful servant to the reader.

The reframed strategy used prize-frame content:
- Articles titled "Why We Won't Work With Companies That Do X" (setting selection criteria)
- A waitlist for their premium newsletter rather than open subscription (scarcity)
- Case studies that highlighted the client's effort to qualify for the program, not just the result
- A "Not For Everyone" positioning statement on their homepage

The result: email subscription rate dropped 20% (fewer unqualified leads), but conversion-to-demo rate increased 3x. The total pipeline impact was positive because the frame attracted self-qualifying prospects.

## When to Use

- Any pitch meeting where decision-making power is asymmetric
- Negotiations where the default frame positions you as the weaker party
- Sales conversations where the buyer attempts to commoditize your offering
- Partnership discussions where mutual evaluation should be the operating dynamic
- Content and brand strategy where positioning and status matter
- Internal meetings where a proposal needs executive buy-in against competing priorities
- Media interactions where the journalist's frame may not serve your narrative

## When NOT to Use

- Genuine collaborative contexts where frame competition is counterproductive
- Relationships built on trust where frame games would erode credibility
- Customer service interactions where the customer should feel empowered
- Mentorship or coaching contexts where the other party's growth is the goal
- Cultural contexts where assertive frame control violates social norms
- Situations where you genuinely are the less knowledgeable or less capable party — attempting to control the frame without substance backing it up destroys credibility

## Integration

- **With Klaff's STRONG Method**: Frame control IS the "S" in STRONG, and it underlies every subsequent phase. STRONG without frame control is a script without power.
- **With Klaff's Crocodile Brain**: Frame control works because it targets the primitive brain's status-detection system. The croc brain asks "who is in charge here?" and the answer determines whether your message gets through.
- **With Klaff's Push/Pull**: Push/Pull is a frame maintenance technique — alternating between building desire (pull) and creating distance (push) keeps the prize frame fresh.
- **With Johnstone's Status Transactions**: Frame control is the strategic layer; status transactions are the behavioral implementation. You control the frame through high-status behaviors.
- **With Ganz's Public Narrative**: In movement-building contexts, frame control applies to how the movement narrative frames the opposition, the urgency, and the community's identity.

## Agent Routing

Route to the Klaff Frame Control framework when:
- The user describes a persuasion challenge where power dynamics are at play
- The request involves negotiation preparation or strategy
- Content or brand positioning feels "weak," "commodity," or "undifferentiated"
- The user asks about authority, confidence, or "commanding the room"
- A pitch or meeting "went off the rails" and the user wants to understand why
- The user mentions Oren Klaff, frame control, or power dynamics

Do NOT route here when:
- The need is for story structure or narrative arc (use structural frameworks)
- The context is genuinely collaborative with no persuasion element
- The request is about creative ideation (use Johnstone frameworks)
- The focus is on community or movement building (use Ganz frameworks)
- The user needs help with written content that has no live delivery component
