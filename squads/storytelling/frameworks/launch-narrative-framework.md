# Launch Narrative Framework
> Structure product/service launches through anticipation, revelation, proof, and urgency phases across multiple channels.

## Overview
A launch is not a moment — it is a narrative. Most companies treat launches as a single event: push the button, send the email, publish the post. This framework treats a launch as a multi-phase story that unfolds over days or weeks, building anticipation, creating revelation, demonstrating proof, and generating urgency. Each phase serves a distinct psychological function and uses different storytelling techniques. The result is a launch that does not just announce — it enrolls the audience in a story they want to see completed. The framework applies to product launches, service launches, feature releases, program announcements, and any situation where something new enters the market.

## Core Methodology
The Launch Narrative Framework operates in four sequential phases:

1. **Anticipation Phase (Pre-Launch)** — Tease the problem, not the solution. Build awareness of the pain or gap that the launch will address. Create curiosity and desire before revealing what is coming. The audience should be hungry for the solution before they know it exists.

2. **Revelation Phase (Launch Day/Week)** — Unveil the solution with maximum narrative impact. The revelation should feel like the answer to a question the audience has been asking. This is the moment of "finally" — the payoff for the anticipation built in phase one.

3. **Proof Phase (Post-Launch Week 1-2)** — Demonstrate that the promise is real. Early adopter stories, initial results, demonstrations, and testimonials. Proof converts curiosity into conviction and observers into customers.

4. **Urgency Phase (Post-Launch Week 2-4)** — Create a legitimate reason to act now. Deadlines, limited availability, rising prices, or closing enrollment windows. Urgency converts intention into action.

## Detailed Steps

### Step 1: Design the Anticipation Phase (2-4 Weeks Pre-Launch)
- **Week 1 — Problem Awareness**: Publish content that articulates the problem your launch will solve. Blog posts, social media, emails that say "Here is the problem" without mentioning the solution.
- **Week 2 — Tension Building**: Deepen the problem narrative. Share data, customer stories, and personal experiences related to the pain. The audience should feel the gap intensifying.
- **Week 3 — Tease**: Begin hinting that something is coming. "We have been working on something. I am not ready to share it yet, but here is why it matters." Give the audience a reason to pay attention to the launch date.
- **Week 4 — Countdown**: Direct anticipation. "In 3 days, we are announcing X." Share behind-the-scenes glimpses, early reactions from beta users, or a preview of the reveal.
- Create a waitlist or early-access signup to capture demand and provide a quantitative signal
- Do not reveal the solution during the anticipation phase. Resist the urge. The tension is the point.

### Step 2: Design the Revelation Phase (Launch Day + 3 Days)
- **Launch Day — The Reveal**: Full announcement across all channels simultaneously. The reveal should answer the anticipation phase's implicit question: "Here is what we built and why."
- **Launch Day Content**: Launch video or post (the hero asset), detailed product/service page, FAQ, pricing
- **Launch Day + 1 — The Story Behind**: "Here is why we built this." Share the origin story, the development journey, the moments of doubt and breakthrough. Humanize the launch.
- **Launch Day + 2 — The Walkthrough**: "Here is how it works." Demo, tutorial, guided experience. Reduce friction between interest and understanding.
- **Launch Day + 3 — The Response**: Share the audience's reaction. Screenshots of comments, emails from excited early users, social media reactions. Social proof from the launch moment itself.
- The revelation should feel like a gift, not a sales pitch. The tone is "we made this for you" not "buy this from us."

### Step 3: Design the Proof Phase (Week 1-2 Post-Launch)
- **Day 4-7 — Early Adopter Stories**: Share stories from first users. Even 48-hour results are valuable when framed correctly: "Sarah signed up on Tuesday. By Thursday, she had already [specific result]."
- **Day 7-10 — Deep Dive Content**: Detailed case studies, tutorials, use cases. Content that helps prospects see themselves using the product/service.
- **Day 10-14 — Third-Party Validation**: Press coverage, influencer reviews, community discussions. External voices carry more weight than your own.
- Proof content should address the three main objections identified in pre-launch research
- Each proof asset should tell a complete mini-story using the Case Study Story Framework

### Step 4: Design the Urgency Phase (Week 2-4 Post-Launch)
- **Legitimate urgency mechanisms**: Launch pricing that expires, limited spots, bonuses that expire, beta access closing
- **Cost-of-delay narrative**: "Every week without this solution costs you [quantified impact]." Back this with data.
- **Social momentum narrative**: "500 people signed up in the first week. Here is what they are already experiencing." The urgency comes from not wanting to be left behind.
- **Final call**: The last email/post of the launch sequence. Summarize the story: problem, solution, proof, and deadline. Make it clear this is the last time you will ask.
- The urgency must be truthful. If the deadline passes and the offer remains, you have destroyed trust for every future launch.

### Step 5: Map the Multi-Channel Narrative
- **Email**: The primary narrative vehicle. Each email advances the launch story by one beat.
- **Social media**: Real-time commentary, behind-the-scenes content, audience reactions, countdowns
- **Website**: Landing page evolves through phases — teaser page in anticipation, full reveal on launch day, proof additions post-launch
- **Content marketing**: Blog posts, podcasts, and videos that provide depth at each phase
- **Paid advertising**: Retargeting during proof and urgency phases for people who visited but did not convert
- **PR/Media**: Timed to the revelation phase for maximum amplification
- All channels tell the same story, adapted to the format. No contradictions, no premature reveals.

### Step 6: Plan the Post-Launch Transition
- After urgency phase ends, transition from launch narrative to ongoing product narrative
- Capture launch metrics: waitlist signups, launch day traffic, conversion rate, revenue, feedback themes
- Conduct a launch retrospective: which phase performed best? Where did the narrative lose the audience?
- Archive launch assets for repurposing: testimonials, case studies, and proof become evergreen marketing material
- Set the foundation for the next launch: what story threads remain open for future continuation?

## Examples

### Example 1: SaaS Feature Launch — Analytics Dashboard
- **Anticipation (2 weeks)**: Blog post: "Why 80% of marketing teams do not trust their own data." Social posts sharing data horror stories. Email: "We have been quietly building something for the past 6 months. Details coming next week."
- **Revelation**: Launch email: "Introducing the Unified Dashboard — every metric, every channel, one view." Launch video showing the dashboard in action with a real customer's data. Product page with full details and a free trial CTA.
- **Proof (Week 1-2)**: Day 5: "Sarah from NextWave connected her accounts in 10 minutes and found $3,000 in wasted ad spend within the first hour." Day 10: Third-party review from a respected industry blog. Day 12: Video walkthrough with a customer showing their actual workflow.
- **Urgency (Week 2-3)**: "Launch pricing ends Friday. After that, the dashboard is included only in the Pro plan at $99/month. Lock in $49/month for life by signing up this week."

### Example 2: Course/Program Launch
- **Anticipation (3 weeks)**: Free workshop teaching 1 of 5 modules. Email series on the problem the course solves. Instagram carousel: "5 mistakes I made before I figured this out." Waitlist: "Be the first to know when enrollment opens."
- **Revelation**: Email to waitlist: "Enrollment is open. 50 spots. Here is everything included." Sales page with full curriculum, instructor background, and student outcomes from beta.
- **Proof (Week 1)**: Day 2: "43 of 50 spots are filled." Day 4: Video testimonial from beta student with specific results. Day 6: Live Q&A addressing common questions and objections.
- **Urgency (Week 2)**: "7 spots remaining. Enrollment closes Thursday at midnight. Here is what last cohort's students are doing now." Final email: "Doors close in 12 hours. Here is what you will miss."

## When to Use
- Product or feature launches
- Service or program announcements
- Course or membership enrollment periods
- Rebranding or repositioning announcements
- Major company milestones or pivots
- Any scenario where something new enters the market and needs narrative momentum

## When NOT to Use
- Minor updates or bug fixes that do not warrant a multi-phase narrative
- Internal-only changes that do not affect the customer
- When the product is not ready — launching a narrative for a product that underdelivers destroys more trust than a quiet release
- When the audience is too small for multi-channel activation (focus on direct outreach instead)
