# Campbell's Monomyth Condensed
> A streamlined five-phase compression of the 17-stage Hero's Journey optimized for fast-paced marketing narratives and short-form content.

## Overview

Campbell's original 17-stage monomyth is comprehensive but unwieldy for modern marketing formats. This condensed version distills the monomyth into five essential phases that preserve the psychological power of the journey while fitting into formats ranging from 60-second ads to single-page landing pages. The condensed monomyth answers a practical question: what is the minimum viable hero's journey?

The five phases are: **Disruption, Commitment, Trial, Breakthrough, and New Normal.** Each phase maps to a specific psychological shift in the audience and a specific marketing function. Unlike the full journey, the condensed version is designed for content that must land in minutes rather than hours.

## Core Methodology

The condensed monomyth is built on the principle that every effective transformation narrative requires exactly five psychological beats:

1. **Recognition** — The audience must see themselves in the starting state (empathy)
2. **Tension** — The audience must feel the cost of inaction (urgency)
3. **Investment** — The audience must witness commitment to change (credibility)
4. **Proof** — The audience must see the solution working under pressure (trust)
5. **Aspiration** — The audience must desire the end state for themselves (motivation)

These five psychological beats map directly to the five narrative phases. Skip any one of them and the story loses its persuasive power.

## Detailed Steps

### Phase 1: Disruption (Maps to Campbell's Call to Adventure)
**What happens:** The hero's ordinary world is interrupted. Something breaks, changes, or becomes visible that cannot be ignored.
**Psychological function:** Creates identification and urgency.
**Marketing function:** Problem awareness. The audience thinks, "That's me."
**Key craft element:** Specificity. The disruption must be concrete and visceral, not abstract. "You got another rejection email" beats "Your job search isn't going well."
**Time allocation:** 15-20% of the narrative.

### Phase 2: Commitment (Maps to Crossing the Threshold)
**What happens:** The hero decides to act despite fear or uncertainty. They choose a path forward.
**Psychological function:** Builds respect for the hero and reduces the audience's own resistance to change.
**Marketing function:** Solution consideration. The audience thinks, "Maybe I should try something different too."
**Key craft element:** Show the cost of commitment. If the decision is easy, it carries no weight. The hero should sacrifice something—time, money, comfort, pride—to demonstrate seriousness.
**Time allocation:** 15-20% of the narrative.

### Phase 3: Trial (Maps to The Ordeal)
**What happens:** The hero encounters the central obstacle. The solution is tested under real pressure. Failure is possible and sometimes occurs before eventual success.
**Psychological function:** Creates suspense and demonstrates authenticity. Audiences distrust narratives where everything works perfectly.
**Marketing function:** Objection handling. The audience thinks, "But what about [concern]?" The trial addresses those concerns through narrative rather than argument.
**Key craft element:** The trial must be relevant to the audience's actual fears about the product or service. If customers worry about implementation difficulty, the trial should show implementation challenges being overcome.
**Time allocation:** 30-40% of the narrative (this is the core).

### Phase 4: Breakthrough (Maps to The Reward)
**What happens:** The hero overcomes the obstacle and achieves the desired outcome. The solution proves its value.
**Psychological function:** Delivers emotional payoff and provides social proof through narrative.
**Marketing function:** Proof of value. The audience thinks, "It actually works."
**Key craft element:** Make the breakthrough proportional to the trial. A massive struggle followed by a small win feels anticlimactic. A small struggle followed by a massive win feels unearned. Match the stakes.
**Time allocation:** 15-20% of the narrative.

### Phase 5: New Normal (Maps to Return with the Elixir)
**What happens:** The hero's world is permanently changed. They share the benefit with others or reflect on the transformation.
**Psychological function:** Projects the audience into a desirable future state.
**Marketing function:** Call to action. The audience thinks, "I want that for myself."
**Key craft element:** Show, don't just tell, the new normal. Specific details of the transformed life are more compelling than general claims of happiness. "She now closes deals in half the time and leaves the office by five" beats "She's more productive and happier."
**Time allocation:** 10-15% of the narrative.

### Transition Techniques Between Phases
- **Disruption to Commitment:** Use a "moment of decision" — a single sentence or scene where the hero explicitly chooses to act. "That night, she opened her laptop and signed up."
- **Commitment to Trial:** Use a time compression — montage, summary, or "fast forward three weeks" to move quickly into the meaty conflict.
- **Trial to Breakthrough:** Use a turning point — a single insight, discovery, or action that shifts the trajectory. This is the "aha moment."
- **Breakthrough to New Normal:** Use a contrast — juxtapose the new state against the opening disruption to create a bookend effect.

## Examples

### Example 1: Customer Testimonial Video (90 seconds)
**Disruption (0:00-0:15):** "Last year, our team was drowning. Three people quit in two months, and every project was behind schedule."
**Commitment (0:15-0:30):** "We'd tried everything—new hires, overtime, even cutting scope. Finally, our CTO said, 'We need to rethink the whole system.' That's when we found [Product]."
**Trial (0:30-1:00):** "Migrating 200 active projects was terrifying. Week one was chaos—people couldn't find things, the old shortcuts didn't work. By week two, something clicked. The automated workflows started saving us hours. But the real test was our Q3 launch—our biggest ever."
**Breakthrough (1:00-1:15):** "We shipped two days early. First time in company history. Our PM actually cried in the standup."
**New Normal (1:15-1:30):** "Now we run 40% more projects with the same team. Nobody's quit since. [Product] didn't just fix our workflow—it fixed our culture."

### Example 2: Landing Page Structure
**Disruption (Hero Section):** Bold headline naming the problem. Supporting stat. Image showing the frustrating status quo.
**Commitment (Problem/Solution Bridge):** "You've tried [common alternatives]. Here's why they fail—and what's different about [Product]."
**Trial (Features Section reframed as Challenges Overcome):** Instead of listing features, describe the challenges they solve: "When [common obstacle] happens, [Feature] automatically [resolution]."
**Breakthrough (Social Proof Section):** Customer results with specific metrics. Before/after comparisons with real data.
**New Normal (CTA Section):** Paint the picture of life after adoption. CTA button text reflects the transformed state: "Start shipping on time" rather than "Sign up now."

## When to Use

- Video testimonials and case studies under three minutes
- Landing pages and sales pages
- Ad scripts (30-second, 60-second, 90-second formats)
- Pitch decks (the narrative spine of the presentation)
- Email campaigns with limited sequence length (3-5 emails)
- Social media story arcs (Instagram/TikTok multi-slide stories)
- Podcast ad reads and sponsorship scripts

## When NOT to Use

- When you have ample time and space for the full Hero's Journey (brand documentaries, book-length content)
- When the transformation requires extensive context-setting that cannot be compressed (complex B2B solutions with long evaluation cycles)
- When the audience needs the mentor/guide relationship to be developed (use full Hero's Journey with Miller StoryBrand)
- When the story involves multiple characters with their own arcs (use full journey or Coyne Story Grid)
- When the emotional register requires slow build rather than efficient impact (use Duarte Sparkline)

## Integration

The Condensed Monomyth integrates with:
- **Campbell's Hero's Journey** — This is a compression of that framework; use the full version when format permits
- **Dicks' Story Beginning-End Rule** — Apply Dicks' technique of knowing your ending first, then crafting the beginning as its opposite, within each condensed phase
- **Miller's Grunt Test** — Ensure the landing page version passes Miller's three-question clarity test
- **Klaff's Crocodile Brain** — The Disruption phase should be crafted to penetrate the primitive brain's novelty filter
- **Hall's Gap-Bridge Framework** — The five phases can be mapped to Hall's Gap (Disruption), Bridge (Commitment through Breakthrough), and New Reality (New Normal)

## Agent Routing

Route to the Condensed Monomyth when:
- The user needs a transformation story but the format is short (under 3 minutes or under 1000 words)
- The user requests a "quick" or "simple" version of the hero's journey
- The content format is a landing page, ad script, testimonial, or pitch deck
- The user has already identified a clear before/after transformation but needs structure
- Time or space constraints make the full 12-17 stage journey impractical

Do NOT route here when:
- The user wants the full mythological depth of Campbell (route to Hero's Journey)
- The narrative is episodic and needs recurring structure (route to Harmon Story Circle)
- The focus is on genre and scene-level craft (route to Coyne or Snyder)
- The user needs a framework for choosing which story to tell rather than how to structure it (route to Hall Story Selection Matrix)
