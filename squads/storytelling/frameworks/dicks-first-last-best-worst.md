# Dicks' First/Last/Best/Worst Prompt System
> A structured exercise for discovering hidden stories by mining personal and organizational experience through four powerful prompts.

## Overview

Matthew Dicks developed the First/Last/Best/Worst exercise as a story-discovery tool for people who say "I don't have any good stories." The exercise works because it bypasses the conscious mind's tendency to filter for "impressive" or "dramatic" events and instead accesses the authentic, specific memories that contain genuine 5-second moments.

The system is deceptively simple: take any topic and ask four questions about it. First time you [X]. Last time you [X]. Best [X]. Worst [X]. Each prompt triggers a different type of memory retrieval and surfaces a different emotional register. Combined, the four prompts reliably produce 2-4 storyworthy moments from any single topic.

Dicks uses this exercise in workshops, coaching sessions, and his own practice. It is the fastest reliable method for generating story candidates when Homework for Life has not yet built a sufficient reservoir.

For marketing and brand applications, the exercise is invaluable for: mining founder stories, discovering customer experience narratives, running client workshops, team storytelling training, and building content reservoirs.

## Core Methodology

### The Four Prompts

**FIRST:** The first time you experienced [topic]. First experiences carry outsized emotional weight because they are colored by novelty, uncertainty, and heightened awareness. The brain records first experiences in high resolution. First-time stories naturally contain transformation because the protagonist moves from inexperience to experience.

**LAST:** The most recent time you experienced [topic]. Last experiences are valuable because they are fresh and often reveal how a person has changed over time. When paired with FIRST, the contrast between the first and last experience reveals growth, regression, or stagnation — all storyworthy.

**BEST:** The best experience of [topic]. Best experiences surface moments of peak emotion, achievement, or connection. They reveal what matters most to the person. However, "best" stories carry a risk: they can become brags. The storyworthy moment in a "best" story is rarely the achievement itself — it is the unexpected emotion, the price paid, or the person who made it possible.

**WORST:** The worst experience of [topic]. Worst experiences are often the richest story material because they contain the most transformation. The worst moment is frequently the setup for the most profound 5-second moment. People resist telling "worst" stories because they feel vulnerable, but vulnerability is precisely what makes stories resonate.

### Why These Four Work Together

The four prompts create a matrix that prevents the most common story-discovery failure: only looking for stories in the obvious places.

| Prompt | Memory Type | Emotional Register | Story Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Novelty-based | Wonder, fear, awkwardness | May be too surface-level |
| Last | Recency-based | Reflection, comparison | May lack emotional peak |
| Best | Peak-positive | Joy, pride, gratitude | May become self-congratulatory |
| Worst | Peak-negative | Vulnerability, regret, anger | May be too raw to tell |

The combination ensures balance. If someone only tells "best" stories, they seem inauthentic. If they only tell "worst" stories, they seem damaged. The four-prompt system produces a range of stories that together create a complete, human portrait.

## Detailed Steps

### Running the Exercise Solo

**Step 1: Choose a Topic**
Select a topic from the list below or generate your own. The topic should be broad enough to trigger multiple memories but specific enough to anchor the recall.

Starter topics:
- Car, job, boss, teacher, meal, trip, phone call, lie, gift, failure, win, conversation, mistake, surprise, goodbye, hello, purchase, loss, fear, risk, silence, apology, compliment, rejection, handshake, argument, secret, lesson, bet, promise

**Step 2: Set a Timer (2 Minutes Per Prompt)**
For each of the four prompts, write continuously for 2 minutes. Do not filter. Do not evaluate quality. Write whatever comes to mind, even if it seems trivial.

**Step 3: Circle the Heat**
After all four prompts are complete, read through your notes and circle any moment that produces a physical reaction: a stomach clench, a laugh, a wince, a catch in the throat. These physical responses indicate emotional resonance — the raw material of storyworthy moments.

**Step 4: Identify the 5-Second Moment**
For each circled moment, ask: "What is the instant of realization or change?" Apply the Storyworthy methodology to determine if the moment contains a genuine 5-second moment.

**Step 5: Test with the Beginning/End Rule**
For each candidate story, draft the opening and closing sentences. Are they opposite enough? If the distance between beginning and end is small, the story may be an anecdote rather than a narrative.

### Running the Exercise with Clients or Teams

The First/Last/Best/Worst exercise is one of the most effective workshop activities for surfacing authentic organizational stories. Here is the facilitation guide:

**Setup (5 minutes):**
- Explain that the goal is to find stories, not tell them. No one will be asked to share anything they do not want to share.
- Emphasize that "storyworthy" does not mean "dramatic." The best stories are often small and personal.
- Distribute blank templates with four quadrants (First, Last, Best, Worst) and the chosen topic at the top.

**Round 1: Individual Writing (10 minutes)**
- Choose a topic relevant to the organizational context. For a startup: "customer interaction." For a brand workshop: "moment when you felt proud of this company." For a sales team: "sales conversation."
- Participants write in silence, 2-3 minutes per prompt.
- Remind them: no filtering, no judgment, no editing.

**Round 2: Partner Share (10 minutes)**
- Pair participants. Each person shares one moment from any quadrant (their choice).
- The partner's job is to listen and then ask one question: "What was that moment like for you?" This question deepens the memory and surfaces emotion.

**Round 3: Group Harvest (15 minutes)**
- Ask for volunteers to share a moment with the full group. Frame it as "a moment that surprised you when it came up" — this lowers the bar and encourages sharing.
- The facilitator's job is to listen for 5-second moments and reflect them back: "It sounds like the moment everything changed for you was when [X]. Is that right?"

**Round 4: Story Selection (10 minutes)**
- Each participant selects one moment they want to develop into a full story.
- Apply the beginning/end opposites rule to test each selection.
- Assign "story development" as homework using the Storyworthy methodology.

### Running the Exercise for Founder Stories

Founder stories are among the most valuable brand assets, and they are almost always undertold or poorly told. The First/Last/Best/Worst exercise with founders surfaces the authentic moments that branding agencies cannot fabricate.

**Topics for founders:**
- First customer, last time you almost quit, best piece of advice you received, worst decision you made
- First hire, last time you were truly surprised by the business, best day at work, worst day at work
- First rejection from an investor, last time you changed your mind about something fundamental, best customer feedback, worst quarter
- First time you knew this would work, last time you doubted it, best partnership, worst partnership

**Key facilitation note for founders:** Founders often want to tell the "impressive" version of their story. The exercise's power is in bypassing that instinct. The WORST prompts are almost always where the best founder stories live, because they reveal the vulnerability and resilience that audiences connect with.

## Examples

### Example 1: Finding a Customer Story for a Financial Planning App

**Topic:** Money conversation

**First:** "The first money conversation I remember is my parents fighting about rent when I was 8. I was hiding behind the couch. I could hear my mom crying."

**Last:** "Last Tuesday I showed my wife our net worth dashboard and she said 'I can't believe this is us.' We were both quiet for a long time."

**Best:** "The best money conversation was when my financial advisor said 'You don't need me anymore.' I felt a kind of pride I had never felt about money before."

**Worst:** "The worst was calling my dad to ask for $2,000 to cover payroll when I was 29 and supposedly a successful entrepreneur. The silence on the phone was the longest of my life."

**Storyworthy analysis:** The WORST entry contains the strongest 5-second moment. The silence on the phone is the moment of realization — the protagonist understands the gap between his identity (successful entrepreneur) and his reality (calling his father for money). The LAST entry contains a beautiful closing moment. Combined: a story that begins with a call to dad for money and ends with a couple quietly staring at a screen showing financial security they built together. The opposites are powerful.

### Example 2: Finding Brand Stories for a Hospitality Company

**Workshop with 12 hotel managers. Topic: "guest interaction"**

**Harvested moments (from group share):**
- A manager's FIRST: checking in a guest who turned out to be the manager's childhood teacher. "She didn't recognize me, but I recognized her. I upgraded her without saying why."
- A manager's WORST: a guest's child had a seizure in the pool. "I held the child while we waited for the ambulance. The mother kept saying 'thank you' and I kept thinking I should be doing more."
- A manager's BEST: a couple returned every year for 11 years to the same room for their anniversary. On the 11th year, they brought their daughter. "She was exactly 11 months old. They had conceived her in that room."
- A manager's LAST: a guest left a note that said "This was the first trip I've taken since my husband died. You made it bearable. I am going to be okay."

**Brand application:** These four moments, developed into full stories using the Storyworthy methodology, became the core of the hospitality company's "Every Stay Has a Story" campaign. Each story was told from the manager's perspective, ensuring authenticity and emotional depth that manufactured testimonials cannot achieve.

## When to Use

- Client workshops to surface authentic brand and customer stories
- Founder interviews to find the real origin story beneath the polished version
- Team storytelling training to build narrative capacity across an organization
- Content reservoir building when you need a stockpile of stories for ongoing content
- Sales team training to equip reps with authentic customer stories
- Brand archaeology — discovering the stories that already exist within an organization
- Personal branding when someone says "I don't have any interesting stories"
- Keynote or presentation prep to find the right opening or closing story

## When NOT to Use

- When you already have a specific story and need to craft it (use Storyworthy methodology directly)
- When you need a strategic narrative framework rather than individual stories (use Howell's Story Cycle)
- When you need to decide which of several known stories to use (use Hall's Story Selection Matrix)
- When the stories needed are not personal/experiential but structural/strategic (use Hall's 4 Story Types)
- When time does not permit a discovery exercise and you need to generate narrative from existing materials

## Integration

- **Dicks' Storyworthy:** First/Last/Best/Worst is the discovery engine; Storyworthy is the craft engine. Moments found through this exercise are developed into full stories using the Storyworthy methodology.
- **Dicks' Homework for Life:** This exercise is the sprint version of Homework for Life. Homework is daily, incremental discovery; First/Last/Best/Worst is concentrated, workshop-based discovery. Both feed the same story reservoir.
- **Dicks' Beginning/End Rule:** Use the opposites rule to test story candidates from this exercise. The best stories will have strong opposition between their opening state and their closing state.
- **Howell's Story Strategy Canvas:** Use this exercise to populate the Canvas's Proof element with specific, authentic stories rather than generic claims.
- **Hall's 4 Strategic Story Types:** Stories discovered through this exercise can be categorized using Hall's types (Value, Founder, Customer, Purpose) to ensure strategic coverage.
- **Hall's Story Selection Matrix:** Once a reservoir of stories is built through this exercise, use the matrix to deploy them in the right contexts.
