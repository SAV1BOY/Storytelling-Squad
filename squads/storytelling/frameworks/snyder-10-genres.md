# Snyder's 10 Story Genres
> A classification system of ten universal story patterns that reveals the deep structure beneath surface-level content, enabling marketers to choose the right narrative DNA for any campaign.

## Overview

Blake Snyder argued that all stories fall into exactly ten genres—not based on setting or topic (sci-fi, romance, thriller) but on the underlying structural pattern. A story about a startup founder and a story about a medieval knight can be the same genre if they follow the same deep pattern. Snyder called these genres the "DNA" of storytelling.

For marketers, genre selection is a strategic decision. Before asking "how do we tell this story?" you must ask "what *kind* of story is this?" The genre determines character dynamics, escalation patterns, emotional arcs, and audience expectations. Choosing the wrong genre creates a narrative that feels "off" even when individual elements are well-crafted.

## Core Methodology

Genre selection follows three steps:

1. **Identify the core dynamic** — What is the fundamental tension driving the narrative? Is it man vs. institution? Man vs. self? A puzzle to solve? A group uniting?
2. **Match to genre** — Select the genre whose structural DNA matches that dynamic. Each genre has required components that must be present for the story to satisfy.
3. **Honor the genre's rules** — Each genre has specific beats, character requirements, and escalation patterns. Violating these creates audience dissatisfaction even if the audience can't articulate why.

## Detailed Steps

### Genre 1: Monster in the House
**Core dynamic:** A contained group faces an unstoppable threat.
**Required components:** A monster (the threat), a house (the bounded environment), a sin (the reason the monster was unleashed).
**Marketing application:** Crisis narratives, cybersecurity campaigns, insurance marketing. "Here's the threat lurking in your system—and you invited it in."
**Key rule:** The sin must be relatable. The audience should think "I could make that mistake."

### Genre 2: Golden Fleece
**Core dynamic:** A hero goes on a journey to find something, but the real prize is what they learn along the way.
**Required components:** A road (the journey), a team (companions who challenge the hero), a prize (the stated goal that evolves).
**Marketing application:** Customer journey narratives, product roadmap stories, founder origin stories. The destination is less important than the transformation.
**Key rule:** The road must change the hero. If they arrive the same person they left as, the genre fails.

### Genre 3: Out of the Bottle
**Core dynamic:** A wish is granted (or a curse imposed), and the hero must learn the lesson or be destroyed.
**Required components:** A wish (the magic), a spell (the rules of the new reality), a lesson (the moral truth the hero must accept).
**Marketing application:** "What if" campaigns, product transformation stories, cautionary tales. "What if you could [impossible thing]? Here's what actually happens."
**Key rule:** The lesson must cost something. Free wisdom is unconvincing.

### Genre 4: Dude with a Problem
**Core dynamic:** An ordinary person faces an extraordinary situation.
**Required components:** An innocent hero (relatable, not special), a sudden event (the disruption is external and unchosen), a life-or-death struggle (stakes must be real).
**Marketing application:** Customer testimonials, crisis response stories, "everyday hero" campaigns. The power comes from the hero's ordinariness.
**Key rule:** The hero must NOT be a specialist. Their relatability is the genre's engine.

### Genre 5: Rites of Passage
**Core dynamic:** A universal life transition forces painful growth.
**Required components:** A life problem (puberty, death, divorce, career change), a wrong way to handle it (the hero's initial coping strategy fails), and acceptance (the hero surrenders to the new reality).
**Marketing application:** Life-stage marketing, wellness brands, education companies, career platforms. "This transition is hard. Here's the path through it."
**Key rule:** The hero must initially resist the change. Immediate acceptance removes all dramatic tension.

### Genre 6: Buddy Love
**Core dynamic:** Two mismatched characters complete each other.
**Required components:** An incomplete hero, a counterpart who has what the hero lacks, and a complication that tests the bond.
**Marketing application:** Partnership announcements, co-branding campaigns, product integration stories, team-building narratives. "Alone we were good. Together we're unstoppable."
**Key rule:** Both parties must be transformed. One-sided benefit is not a buddy story.

### Genre 7: Whydunit
**Core dynamic:** A dark secret is uncovered, revealing uncomfortable truths about human nature.
**Required components:** A detective (the investigator), a secret (the hidden truth), and a dark turn (the truth is worse than expected).
**Marketing application:** Investigative content marketing, industry expose pieces, data-driven storytelling that uncovers hidden patterns. "We looked into [common assumption]. What we found will change how you think about [topic]."
**Key rule:** The revelation must implicate the audience. They should feel complicit, not merely informed.

### Genre 8: The Fool Triumphant
**Core dynamic:** An underestimated underdog defeats the establishment through apparent foolishness that turns out to be wisdom.
**Required components:** A fool (dismissed by the powerful), an establishment (the rigid institution), and a transmutation (the fool's weakness becomes strength).
**Marketing application:** Disruptor brand positioning, startup narratives, challenger brand campaigns. "They said we were crazy. They were right—and that's why it worked."
**Key rule:** The establishment must be genuinely powerful. Defeating a weak opponent carries no meaning.

### Genre 9: Institutionalized
**Core dynamic:** An individual navigates a powerful group, facing the choice of joining, reforming, or destroying it.
**Required components:** A group (the institution), a choice (join or resist), a sacrifice (the cost of either path), and a brand (the institution's ideology).
**Marketing application:** Workplace culture campaigns, industry transformation narratives, community-building content. "Here's how we're changing [industry] from the inside."
**Key rule:** The institution must offer genuine benefits. Pure villainy removes the dilemma.

### Genre 10: Superhero
**Core dynamic:** A uniquely gifted individual must bear the burden of their exceptionality.
**Required components:** A special power (the differentiation), a nemesis (an equal-and-opposite force), and a curse (the cost of the gift).
**Marketing application:** Premium brand positioning, thought leadership, expert personal branding. "Being the best at this means sacrificing [something real]."
**Key rule:** The curse must be proportional to the power. Without cost, the hero is insufferable.

## Examples

### Example 1: B2B Software Campaign Using "Monster in the House"
**The Monster:** A data breach that exploits a common vulnerability.
**The House:** The company's network—bounded and inescapable.
**The Sin:** The security shortcut everyone takes ("We'll update the protocol next quarter").
**Campaign execution:** A video series following a fictional but realistic company through a breach. Each episode maps to Save the Cat beats. The product is the weapon that finally defeats the monster—but only after the team confronts the sin that created the vulnerability.

### Example 2: Challenger Brand Launch Using "The Fool Triumphant"
**The Fool:** A new entrant in the legal tech space built by non-lawyers.
**The Establishment:** The traditional legal industry that insists "only lawyers understand law."
**The Transmutation:** The outsider perspective reveals what lawyers missed—clients don't need legal expertise, they need clarity.
**Campaign execution:** A manifesto-style launch that embraces the "foolishness" of being outsiders, supported by customer testimonials from people who tried traditional options first and failed.

## When to Use

- Campaign strategy phase, before any content is created
- When a narrative "feels wrong" but you can't identify why (likely a genre mismatch)
- Brand positioning to choose a story archetype for the entire brand
- Content series planning where each piece needs a consistent underlying pattern
- Competitive analysis—identify competitors' genres and choose a different one

## When NOT to Use

- When you need scene-level structure (use Save the Cat beat sheet instead)
- When the content is too short for genre dynamics to manifest (under 500 words)
- When the user already knows the genre and needs execution guidance
- For purely informational or transactional content

## Integration

Snyder's 10 Genres integrates with:
- **Snyder's Save the Cat** — Genre selection precedes beat sheet application; each genre inflects the beats differently
- **Coyne's Genre Clover** — Coyne's system provides a complementary genre classification focused on content/reality spectrum
- **Campbell's Archetypes** — Genre determines which archetypes populate the narrative
- **Klaff's Frame Control** — Genre informs which frame to establish (e.g., Fool Triumphant uses the "prize frame")
- **Hall's Story Selection Matrix** — Genre helps filter which real stories to collect and deploy

## Agent Routing

Route to Snyder's 10 Genres when:
- The user is in the early strategy phase and hasn't chosen a narrative pattern yet
- A campaign narrative feels wrong and needs diagnosis
- The user asks "what kind of story should we tell?"
- Brand positioning requires selecting a foundational story type
- The user mentions Snyder genres, story types, or narrative DNA

Do NOT route here when:
- The user already knows their story type and needs structural execution (route to Save the Cat)
- The focus is on character rather than plot pattern (route to Campbell Archetypes)
- The user needs a quick framework for a single piece of content (route to Condensed Monomyth)
