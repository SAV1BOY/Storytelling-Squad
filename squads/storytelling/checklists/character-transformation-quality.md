# Character Transformation Quality Checklist
> Quality gate for validating that the narrative contains a meaningful, visible character arc

## Purpose
Gates the transformation arc within a narrative. Characters who do not change are forgettable.
Audiences connect with characters who struggle, learn, and emerge different. Use this checklist
for any narrative featuring a protagonist: founder stories, customer case studies, brand origin
stories, keynote narratives, campaign heroes, or audience-surrogate characters. Ensures the
transformation is specific, earned, emotionally resonant, and shown rather than merely claimed.

## When to Use
- For any narrative featuring a person who changes: founder stories, customer case studies, keynotes
- When the narrative needs emotional depth beyond informational content
- When feedback says the story is "interesting but I don't care about the person"
- Before finalizing testimonials, origin stories, or hero narratives
- When building audience identification with a protagonist

## Prerequisites
- A complete narrative draft exists with an identifiable protagonist
- The protagonist's role is defined (founder, customer, user, audience surrogate, brand persona)
- The conflict and stakes have been established
- The desired audience takeaway is documented
- The protagonist's real or fictional background is accessible for reference

## Checklist

### State A and State B
- [ ] **State A is concrete** — The protagonist's starting condition is described with specific, observable details (behavior, belief, situation, habits), not abstractions like "struggling" or "unhappy"
- [ ] **State B is concrete** — The protagonist's ending condition is equally specific and observable; the change is visible and demonstrable, not just claimed
- [ ] **Gap between A and B is significant** — The distance between starting and ending states is large enough to be meaningful and worthy of the audience's time, but also believable
- [ ] **State A is relatable** — The audience can see themselves in the protagonist's starting condition or immediately recognize someone they know in that situation
- [ ] **State B is aspirational** — The ending state represents something the audience wants for themselves, believes is worth achieving, or recognizes as genuinely better
- [ ] **Contrast is dramatized** — At least one moment in the narrative directly juxtaposes the before and after states so the audience can see the distance traveled

### The Lie and the Truth
- [ ] **False belief identified** — The protagonist holds a specific, stated or demonstrated belief at the start that is limiting, incorrect, or incomplete
- [ ] **Truth discovered** — By the end, the protagonist has replaced the false belief with a new understanding, and this new belief is clearly articulated or demonstrated
- [ ] **Belief change drives behavior change** — The internal shift in belief causes an observable external change in the protagonist's actions, decisions, or relationships
- [ ] **Moment of realization exists** — There is a specific scene or beat where the protagonist confronts the gap between their old belief and reality, creating a turning point
- [ ] **Lie is sympathetic** — The audience understands why the protagonist held the false belief; it was not stupid but rather a reasonable response to their circumstances

### Transformation Mechanics
- [ ] **Transformation is earned** — The change results from struggle, effort, or meaningful experience, not from luck, coincidence, or sudden epiphany without groundwork
- [ ] **Catalyst is identifiable** — A specific event, person, piece of information, or experience triggers the beginning of the transformation process
- [ ] **Resistance is shown** — The protagonist resists change at some point, demonstrating that the transformation is difficult and therefore meaningful
- [ ] **Cost of change acknowledged** — The narrative recognizes what the protagonist gave up, risked, endured, or sacrificed to transform; change is not free
- [ ] **Transformation is gradual, not instant** — The change unfolds over time or through stages, not as a single switch-flip moment (unless the format demands extreme compression)

### Showing vs. Telling
- [ ] **Transformation shown through action** — At least one scene demonstrates the new behavior rather than just narrating it ("she pushed back on the VP's timeline" vs. "she became more assertive")
- [ ] **Before/after contrast is dramatized** — The narrative includes at least one parallel moment: the protagonist handling a similar situation differently before and after the change
- [ ] **Supporting characters or evidence reflect the change** — Other characters, metrics, environmental details, or outcomes confirm the transformation is real and externally recognized
- [ ] **Emotional resonance present** — The transformation moment produces a felt emotional response in the audience: relief, pride, hope, recognition, or catharsis

### Audience Connection
- [ ] **Transformation is relevant to the audience** — The change the protagonist undergoes maps to a change the audience desires, fears, or needs to make in their own situation
- [ ] **Audience can identify the trigger in their own life** — The catalyst for change is described in terms universal enough that the audience can recognize a similar catalyst in their experience
- [ ] **Transformation serves the narrative's strategic goal** — The character arc directly supports the story's CTA or desired belief change; the audience thinks "I want that change too"
- [ ] **Scale of transformation matches format** — Short-form content shows a single specific shift; long-form content can show a multi-stage journey; the depth matches the available space

## Scoring
Must pass **17/24 minimum**. The following items are CRITICAL and must all pass:
- State A is concrete
- State B is concrete
- False belief identified
- Transformation shown through action
- Transformation is earned

Any CRITICAL failure requires revision of the character arc before the narrative advances.

## Next Steps
- **On Pass:** Advance to emotional arc review (emotional-arc-quality.md) and proof-in-story
  review (proof-in-story-quality.md). The character arc is strong enough to evaluate its
  emotional impact and evidence base.
- **On Fail:** Return to the narrative architect. Reference Campbell's Hero's Journey
  (squads/storytelling/checklists/campbell/) for transformation structure, Dicks' Homework
  for Life technique (squads/storytelling/checklists/dicks/) for finding authentic transformation
  moments, Harmon's Story Circle (squads/storytelling/checklists/harmon/) for the "changed"
  return, or Snyder's Beat Sheet (squads/storytelling/checklists/snyder/) for the "dark night
  of the soul" before transformation. If this is a true story, re-interview the real person to
  surface the genuine internal shift and the specific moment it began.
