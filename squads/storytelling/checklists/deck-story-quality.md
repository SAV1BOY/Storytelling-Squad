# Deck Story Quality Checklist
> Quality gate for validating that a presentation deck tells a story, not just displays information

## Purpose
Gates the narrative quality of slide decks and visual presentations. Use after a deck is drafted
and before design finalization or delivery rehearsal. Applies to pitch decks, keynote presentations,
sales decks, board updates, training decks, and any slide-based communication. Ensures the deck follows a narrative arc with contrast and rising tension, creates at least one
unforgettable moment, and builds toward a decisive call to action rather than fading out with
a "Questions?" slide. Slides are not documents; they are a visual storytelling medium that
requires narrative architecture.

## When to Use
- After a slide deck is drafted and before design finalization or delivery rehearsal
- When a deck feels like "a document on slides" rather than a visual story
- Before any keynote, pitch, board meeting, or client presentation using slides
- When converting a written narrative into a slide-based presentation
- During deck revision after a presentation receives lukewarm audience response

## Prerequisites
- A complete deck draft exists (slides with content, even if not yet designed)
- The presentation context is defined (audience, setting, time limit, stakes)
- The core message and CTA are established and documented
- The pitch or presentation narrative has been reviewed for content quality
- The presenter is identified and available for rehearsal

## Checklist

### Sparkline and Narrative Arc
- [ ] **Sparkline structure present** — The deck alternates between "what is" (current reality) and "what could be" (vision/possibility), creating a rhythmic contrast that pulls the audience forward
- [ ] **Opening establishes the world** — The first 2-3 slides set context: who is the audience, what is the current situation, and why have we gathered; no logo slides or agenda slides first
- [ ] **Tension introduced early** — By slide 5 or the first 15% of the deck, a problem, gap, or opportunity that demands attention has been introduced
- [ ] **Rising action through the middle** — The middle section builds progressively: deepening the problem, stacking evidence, escalating the opportunity, or revealing layers of the solution
- [ ] **Climactic moment exists** — There is a single slide or sequence that represents the peak of the presentation: the big reveal, the key insight, the transformative offer, or the decisive proof
- [ ] **Resolution and CTA close the deck** — The final slides resolve the central tension and present a clear, specific, time-bound call to action; the deck does not end with "Thank you" or "Questions?"
- [ ] **No premature resolution** — The solution or answer is not fully revealed before the audience feels the full weight of the problem; tension before resolution

### Contrast and Persuasion
- [ ] **Before/after contrast used** — At least one slide pair or sequence shows the world without the solution vs. the world with it, making the value gap visceral
- [ ] **Enemy slide present** — The deck names or visualizes the antagonist (broken status quo, competitor weakness, industry failure, outdated approach) that the audience should oppose
- [ ] **"New bliss" slide present** — The deck paints a vivid, specific picture of the desired future state that the audience can aspire to, desire, and feel motivated to reach
- [ ] **Data tells a story** — Charts and numbers are presented with narrative context (what changed, why it matters, what it means for the audience) not just displayed raw

### Audience Journey
- [ ] **Audience journey mapped** — Each slide answers "what does the audience think/feel at this point?" and that answer progresses and changes across the deck
- [ ] **Cognitive load managed** — No single slide contains more than one core idea; complex ideas are broken across multiple progressive-reveal slides
- [ ] **Transitions are logical** — Each slide flows naturally from the previous one; a viewer can anticipate the general direction of the next slide based on the narrative logic
- [ ] **Questions anticipated** — Slides that are likely to prompt audience questions are followed by slides that address those questions before they become objections

### S.T.A.R. Moment and Memorability
- [ ] **S.T.A.R. moment planned** — At least one "Something They'll Always Remember" moment is designed into the deck: a dramatic visual, a live demonstration, a shocking statistic, a physical prop, or an emotional story
- [ ] **S.T.A.R. moment is near the climax** — The most memorable moment occurs in the second half of the deck, near the peak of the narrative arc, where it has maximum impact
- [ ] **Deck has a repeatable headline** — There is one sentence or phrase from the deck that the audience could use to describe the entire presentation to someone who was not there

### Technical and Pacing
- [ ] **Slide count appropriate** — The deck has roughly 1-2 slides per minute of presentation time; it is neither so dense it cannot be delivered nor so sparse it feels padded
- [ ] **No text-wall slides** — No slide contains more than 30 words of body text; data-heavy information uses visuals, charts, progressive reveals, or is moved to a handout
- [ ] **Consistent visual language** — Typography, color palette, imagery style, and layout grid are consistent throughout, reinforcing rather than competing with the narrative
- [ ] **Deck works without presenter** — Key narrative beats are understandable from the slides alone (for leave-behind or email-forward scenarios), or a separate leave-behind version exists
- [ ] **Presenter notes integrated** — Each slide has notes or a script that ensure the presenter delivers the right narrative beat, not just describes what is on screen

## Scoring
Must pass **17/22 minimum**. The following items are CRITICAL and must all pass:
- Sparkline structure present
- Climactic moment exists
- Resolution and CTA close the deck
- S.T.A.R. moment planned
- Cognitive load managed

Any CRITICAL failure requires deck restructuring before design finalization.

## Next Steps
- **On Pass:** Move to visual design polish and presenter rehearsal. Time the full presentation
  at least twice. Iterate on pacing based on rehearsal feedback.
- **On Fail:** Restructure the deck narrative before touching design. Reference Duarte's
  Sparkline framework (squads/storytelling/checklists/duarte/) for contrast structure, Snyder's
  Beat Sheet (squads/storytelling/checklists/snyder/) for dramatic beat pacing, the presentation
  framework (squads/storytelling/checklists/presentation/) for overall deck architecture, or
  Klaff's frame control (squads/storytelling/checklists/klaff/) for pitch-specific deck strategy.
  Storyboard the deck on paper or sticky notes before rebuilding slides; if the story does not
  work on paper, it will not work in slides.
