# Metaphor and Analogy Quality Checklist
> Quality gate for validating that metaphors and analogies clarify, resonate, and do not mislead

## Purpose
Gates the quality and effectiveness of figurative language in a narrative. Metaphors and
analogies are the most powerful tools for making the unfamiliar familiar and the abstract
concrete, but bad metaphors confuse, mislead, or bore. Use this checklist whenever a narrative
relies on metaphors, analogies, or extended comparisons to explain concepts, especially for
technical products, abstract transformations, complex processes, or audiences encountering
unfamiliar ideas. A great metaphor is worth a thousand words of explanation; a bad one costs
a thousand words of clarification.

## When to Use
- When a narrative uses metaphors, analogies, or extended comparisons to explain concepts
- For technical product narratives that need to make complex ideas accessible
- When feedback says "I don't get the comparison" or "that metaphor confused me"
- During revision of content for cross-cultural or international audiences
- When simplifying expert-level concepts for general audiences

## Prerequisites
- A narrative draft exists containing at least one metaphor, analogy, or extended comparison
- The concepts being explained through figurative language are clearly identified
- The target audience's frame of reference, industry, and cultural context are understood
- The core message is established and the role of each metaphor in supporting it is defined

## Checklist

### Clarity and Accuracy
- [ ] **Metaphor clarifies, not decorates** — Each metaphor makes a concept easier to understand for the target audience, not just more literary; removing the metaphor would mean losing clarity
- [ ] **Mapping is accurate** — The comparison holds up under scrutiny: the structural relationship between the compared elements is logically sound, not just superficially similar on one dimension
- [ ] **No false implications** — The metaphor does not suggest properties that are untrue or misleading about the concept it illustrates; unintended connotations have been considered
- [ ] **Metaphor is self-explanatory** — The audience does not need specialized knowledge to understand the comparison; the vehicle (what you compare to) is more familiar than the tenor (what you explain)
- [ ] **Complexity reduced** — The metaphor makes the concept genuinely simpler, not more complicated; it does not require its own multi-sentence explanation to work
- [ ] **Metaphor is precise** — The figurative comparison illuminates the specific aspect of the concept that matters, not a tangential or irrelevant quality

### Originality and Freshness
- [ ] **No dead metaphors** — Overused metaphors are identified and replaced: "think outside the box," "move the needle," "low-hanging fruit," "deep dive," "at the end of the day," "tip of the iceberg," "game changer," "level up"
- [ ] **No mixed metaphors** — No single passage combines two or more unrelated metaphors that clash or create absurd mental images ("We need to hit the ground running and keep our eye on the ball while navigating uncharted waters")
- [ ] **Freshness tested** — Each metaphor has been evaluated with the question: would the audience feel they have heard this exact comparison before? If yes, it is revised or replaced with something original
- [ ] **Specificity over generality** — Metaphors use specific, concrete images rather than broad categories ("like a surgeon removing a splinter with watchmaker's forceps" rather than "like a skilled professional")
- [ ] **Cliche audit complete** — Every figurative expression has been checked against a list of common cliches; those that remain are either genuinely the best choice or have been given a fresh twist

### Audience Fit
- [ ] **Metaphor draws from the audience's world** — The comparison references experiences, objects, processes, or situations the specific target audience encounters in their daily life, work, or culture
- [ ] **Cultural resonance verified** — Metaphors work across the audience's cultural contexts; they do not rely on region-specific sports, age-specific media references, or subculture-specific knowledge unless the audience is known to share that context
- [ ] **Technical level appropriate** — The metaphor neither oversimplifies for expert audiences (making them feel patronized) nor assumes expertise for general audiences (creating new confusion)
- [ ] **Emotional register matches** — The metaphor's emotional tone (playful, grave, urgent, warm, industrial, organic) aligns with the narrative's overall tone and the specific moment where it appears

### Consistency and Extension
- [ ] **Extended metaphors are sustained** — If a metaphor is used as a throughline or organizing principle, it is maintained consistently across the narrative without breaking, mixing, or shifting to a different metaphor mid-stream
- [ ] **No metaphor overload** — The narrative does not introduce more than 3 distinct, unrelated metaphors; excessive metaphors compete for attention and muddy the mental model
- [ ] **Metaphor supports the central message** — Each figurative comparison reinforces the narrative's core point and desired takeaway rather than introducing tangential associations or distracting imagery
- [ ] **Metaphor is memorable** — At least one metaphor in the narrative is vivid, original, and distinctive enough that the audience could recall it, repeat it, and use it in their own conversations a day later

### Testing and Validation
- [ ] **Metaphor tested with target audience** — At least one person from the target audience demographic has confirmed the metaphor makes the concept clearer, not more confusing
- [ ] **Metaphor survives the "actually" test** — No reader's first reaction to the metaphor is "well, actually that's not how X works..." which would undermine rather than support credibility
- [ ] **Metaphor does not trivialize** — Serious topics are not explained through trivializing comparisons; the metaphor respects the weight of the subject matter
- [ ] **Visual potential considered** — For content that may be designed or illustrated, metaphors have been evaluated for visual potential: can a designer or videographer bring this metaphor to life?

## Scoring
Must pass **17/24 minimum**. The following items are CRITICAL and must all pass:
- Metaphor clarifies, not decorates
- No mixed metaphors
- Metaphor draws from the audience's world
- Extended metaphors are sustained
- No dead metaphors

Any CRITICAL failure requires metaphor revision before the narrative advances.

## Next Steps
- **On Pass:** Proceed to final editing review (editing-and-trimming-quality.md) and the
  appropriate format-specific checklist. The figurative language layer is sound.
- **On Fail:** Strip all metaphors and rewrite the concepts in plain, literal language first.
  Verify the concepts are clear without any figurative support. Then introduce one metaphor at
  a time, testing each with the question: "Does this make the concept genuinely clearer for
  THIS audience, or am I adding it because it sounds good?" Reference the narrative framework
  (squads/storytelling/checklists/narrative/) for message-first storytelling. Workshop metaphors
  with someone from the target audience to verify resonance and catch unintended associations
  before finalizing. Remember: no metaphor is always better than a bad metaphor.
