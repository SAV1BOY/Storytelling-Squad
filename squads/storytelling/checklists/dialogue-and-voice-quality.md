# Dialogue and Voice Quality Checklist
> Quality gate for validating that dialogue sounds natural, reveals character, and carries subtext

## Purpose
Gates the quality of dialogue and distinct voice in narratives that include spoken words, quotes,
or character speech. Use for video scripts, case studies with customer quotes, founder stories,
presentation narratives, podcast scripts, or any content where characters speak. Ensures
dialogue does double duty: advancing the plot or argument while simultaneously revealing
character, status, and emotion. Bad dialogue is the fastest way to lose audience trust.

## When to Use
- For any narrative containing spoken dialogue, direct quotes, or character speech
- When drafting video scripts, podcast scripts, or presentation narratives with multiple voices
- For case studies incorporating customer or stakeholder quotes
- When feedback indicates dialogue sounds "wooden," "fake," or "like marketing copy"
- Before recording, filming, or publishing any content with attributed speech

## Prerequisites
- A narrative draft containing dialogue, quotes, or character speech exists
- Characters or speakers are identified with their roles, backgrounds, and motivations
- The scene context for each dialogue exchange is established
- Tone and brand voice guidelines are available
- If using real quotes, source recordings or transcripts are accessible

## Checklist

### Natural Speech Patterns
- [ ] **Read-aloud test passed** — Every line of dialogue has been read aloud by at least one person; lines that feel awkward, stilted, or written-sounding have been revised until they sound like human speech
- [ ] **Contractions used naturally** — Characters use contractions in casual contexts ("don't" not "do not," "we're" not "we are") unless formality is intentional and character-appropriate
- [ ] **Sentence length varies** — Dialogue includes a mix of short, punchy lines and longer thoughts; no character speaks in uniformly long sentences or uniformly short fragments
- [ ] **Filler and false starts used sparingly** — Dialogue includes occasional natural speech artifacts (hesitation, self-correction, trailing off) without overdoing it to the point of distraction
- [ ] **No exposition dumps** — Characters do not explain things they would both already know purely for the audience's benefit; no "As you know, Bob..." constructions
- [ ] **Dialogue is compressed** — Real speech is messy; written dialogue is edited speech that sounds natural but is more efficient than real conversation

### Character Distinction
- [ ] **Characters sound different** — Covering speaker names, a reader can identify which character is speaking based on word choice, sentence rhythm, vocabulary level, and speaking style
- [ ] **Vocabulary matches character** — Each character's word choices reflect their background, expertise level, age, personality, and emotional state
- [ ] **Status dynamics present** — Dialogue reveals power relationships through speech patterns: who defers, who interrupts, who asks questions vs. makes statements, who uses hedging language
- [ ] **Emotional state is audible** — The reader can infer how the character feels from how they speak (word choice, sentence length, directness), not just from narration describing their feelings
- [ ] **Characters have verbal signatures** — At least one character has a distinctive speech habit, phrase, or rhythm that makes them recognizable without attribution

### Subtext and Depth
- [ ] **Subtext present in key exchanges** — At least one important dialogue exchange has characters saying one thing while meaning another; not everything is on-the-nose literal
- [ ] **Dialogue serves dual purpose** — Each significant exchange both advances the plot/argument and reveals something about the character speaking: their fears, values, knowledge, or position
- [ ] **Conflict in conversation** — Key dialogue scenes contain opposing wants or perspectives; characters are not simply agreeing, narrating at each other, or taking turns monologuing
- [ ] **What is unsaid matters** — At least one moment relies on what a character does not say, avoids, or deflects, creating tension or meaning through omission or silence

### Voice Consistency and Appropriateness
- [ ] **Brand voice maintained** — If the narrative represents a brand, the overall dialogue tone aligns with brand guidelines without making characters sound like marketing copy or press releases
- [ ] **Tone matches context** — Serious scenes have appropriately weighted dialogue; light scenes have natural ease; the tonal register fits the dramatic moment without jarring shifts
- [ ] **Cultural sensitivity verified** — Dialogue does not rely on stereotypical speech patterns; any dialect, colloquialism, or cultural reference serves character authenticity rather than caricature
- [ ] **Quotation accuracy confirmed** — If using real quotes from interviews or testimonials, the original speaker has been accurately represented, not edited into someone they are not
- [ ] **Dialogue tags are invisible** — Attribution uses "said" or "asked" primarily; creative tags ("he exclaimed," "she opined," "they retorted") are rare and justified
- [ ] **Action beats replace tags where possible** — Character actions are used to attribute dialogue and convey emotion instead of relying solely on "he said" tags ("She set down her coffee. 'That's not what I agreed to.'")

### Pacing and Economy
- [ ] **Dialogue scenes have shape** — Each dialogue exchange has its own mini-arc: an opening dynamic, a shift or escalation, and a changed state by the end
- [ ] **No circular conversations** — Characters do not repeat the same point or argument without progressing the exchange; each line moves the conversation forward
- [ ] **Entrances and exits are clean** — Dialogue scenes begin after small talk and end before the conversation trails off; the reader enters late and leaves early
- [ ] **Proportion is appropriate** — Dialogue occupies a proportion of the narrative that matches the format: heavy in scripts and case studies, lighter in essays and brand narratives

## Scoring
Must pass **18/24 minimum**. The following items are CRITICAL and must all pass:
- Read-aloud test passed
- Characters sound different
- No exposition dumps
- Subtext present in key exchanges
- Quotation accuracy confirmed

Any CRITICAL failure requires dialogue revision before the narrative advances.

## Next Steps
- **On Pass:** Advance to opening/close review (opening-and-close-quality.md) and the
  appropriate format-specific checklist. The dialogue layer is strong.
- **On Fail:** Revise dialogue using the read-aloud method: speak every line and rewrite until
  it sounds like a real person in that situation. Reference Johnstone's improv principles
  (squads/storytelling/checklists/johnstone/) for status dynamics and natural offers, Dicks'
  storytelling techniques (squads/storytelling/checklists/dicks/) for authentic voice, or
  Howell's delivery techniques (squads/storytelling/checklists/howell/) for spoken rhythm.
  For case studies and testimonials, re-interview real people and capture their actual speech
  patterns, idioms, and rhythms rather than cleaning their quotes into corporate language.
