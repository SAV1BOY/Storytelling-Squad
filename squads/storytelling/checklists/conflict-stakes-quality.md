# Conflict and Stakes Quality Checklist
> Quality gate for validating that the narrative contains meaningful conflict, defined stakes, and genuine urgency

## Purpose
Gates the tension engine of the narrative. Without conflict and stakes, stories become flat
recitations of events that audiences politely ignore. Use this checklist after a draft is
structurally sound to ensure the audience has a genuine reason to care about the outcome.
Essential for any narrative format: pitch, case study, brand story, video, email sequence,
presentation, or campaign. Conflict is not optional; it is the fuel of attention. Even
informational or educational content benefits from framing the knowledge gap as a conflict
between what the audience knows and what they need to know.

## When to Use
- After structural review confirms the narrative has a clear architecture
- When feedback indicates the narrative is "flat," "boring," or "doesn't grab me"
- For any persuasive narrative where the audience must be moved to action
- When a case study reads like a product spec sheet instead of a story
- Before finalizing pitch narratives, brand stories, or campaign content

## Prerequisites
- A complete narrative draft exists
- The central character or protagonist (person, brand, customer, audience) is identified
- The desired outcome or transformation is defined
- Structure and pacing checklist has been run or is in progress
- The audience's own conflicts and pain points are understood

## Checklist

### Antagonist and Obstacle
- [ ] **Antagonist or obstacle is named** — A specific force opposing the protagonist is identified: a competitor, a false belief, a broken system, a circumstance, a fear, or an internal flaw
- [ ] **Obstacle is credible** — The antagonist or obstacle is powerful enough to make the outcome genuinely uncertain; it is not a straw man that collapses at first contact
- [ ] **Obstacle is relatable** — The audience recognizes the antagonist from their own experience or can immediately understand why it is genuinely threatening
- [ ] **Multiple obstacle layers present** — At least two levels of conflict exist (e.g., external market challenge + internal team doubt, or customer pain + industry skepticism)
- [ ] **Obstacle is specific, not abstract** — The conflict is concrete and visual ("we lost our biggest client on a Tuesday morning phone call") not vague ("times were tough")
- [ ] **Obstacle is introduced early** — The antagonist or primary obstacle appears within the first 25% of the narrative, not revealed late as an afterthought

### Stakes Definition
- [ ] **Stakes are explicit** — The narrative clearly states or shows what will be lost if the protagonist fails; the audience does not have to guess
- [ ] **Stakes are personal** — The consequences affect someone the audience cares about (a named person, a team, a community), not just an abstract metric or corporate KPI
- [ ] **Stakes escalate** — The cost of failure increases as the narrative progresses; early stakes are lower than later stakes, building mounting pressure
- [ ] **Stakes are proportional to the ask** — The magnitude of what is at risk matches the action the audience is being asked to take; small CTAs do not require life-or-death stakes
- [ ] **Positive stakes complement negative stakes** — The narrative shows both what can be gained (aspiration, reward, transformation) and what can be lost (pain, regression, missed opportunity)
- [ ] **Stakes are tangible** — At least one stake is described in concrete, measurable terms (dollars, time, relationships, health) rather than purely emotional abstractions

### Escalation and Urgency
- [ ] **Tension increases across the narrative** — Each major beat raises the intensity; the story does not plateau after an early peak or flatline through the middle
- [ ] **At least one moment of doubt** — There is a point where success seems unlikely or impossible, creating genuine suspense and engagement
- [ ] **Urgency is real, not manufactured** — The "why now" pressure comes from genuine circumstances (deadline, market window, health risk, opportunity cost, competitive move) not artificial countdown tactics
- [ ] **Setbacks occur** — The protagonist encounters at least one failure, reversal, or complication before succeeding; the path is not a straight line
- [ ] **Tension is sustained, not just spiked** — Between major tension points, a baseline level of unresolved conflict keeps the audience engaged

### Consequences and Resolution
- [ ] **Consequences are shown, not just claimed** — The narrative demonstrates what happened (or could happen) as a result of the conflict, using specific details, evidence, and vivid description
- [ ] **Resolution earns the stakes** — The way the conflict is resolved is proportional to the difficulty established; easy wins after hard setups feel hollow and undermine audience trust
- [ ] **Audience can map conflict to their own situation** — The conflict and stakes are framed so the audience thinks "this is my problem too" or "this could happen to me"
- [ ] **Residual tension directed toward CTA** — Any unresolved tension at the narrative's end is channeled toward the call to action, not left floating without purpose
- [ ] **Victory has a cost** — The resolution acknowledges that overcoming the conflict required sacrifice, effort, or trade-offs; nothing was free
- [ ] **Lessons are embedded, not lectured** — The conflict teaches the audience something through the story's events rather than through explicit moralizing or instructional tone

## Scoring
Must pass **18/23 minimum**. The following items are CRITICAL and must all pass:
- Antagonist or obstacle is named
- Stakes are explicit
- Tension increases across the narrative
- Consequences are shown, not just claimed
- Obstacle is introduced early

Any CRITICAL failure requires a conflict revision pass.

## Next Steps
- **On Pass:** Advance to character transformation review (character-transformation-quality.md)
  and proof-in-story review (proof-in-story-quality.md).
- **On Fail:** Return to the narrative architect. Map the conflict arc explicitly on one page:
  what is the obstacle, when does it appear, how does it escalate, where are the setbacks,
  and how is it resolved? Reference Campbell's Hero's Journey
  (squads/storytelling/checklists/campbell/) for archetypal conflict structures, Klaff's frame
  control (squads/storytelling/checklists/klaff/) for pitch-specific tension, Coyne's Story
  Grid (squads/storytelling/checklists/coyne/) for genre-specific conflict conventions, or
  Snyder's Beat Sheet (squads/storytelling/checklists/snyder/) for beat-level tension pacing.
  The most common failure is not a lack of conflict but a lack of specificity in describing it.
