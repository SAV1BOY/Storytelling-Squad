# Matthew Dicks: Moth Performances Index
> Matthew Dicks, Multiple Moth StorySLAM and GrandSLAM Performances

## Summary
Matthew Dicks is a 59-time Moth StorySLAM champion and 9-time GrandSLAM champion — arguably
the most successful competitive storyteller in The Moth's history. He is also the author of
"Storyworthy," a practical guide to finding, crafting, and telling stories. His body of work
at The Moth represents a living laboratory of what makes personal narrative land with an
audience under extreme constraints: five minutes, no notes, true stories, live judging.

Dicks's performances consistently demonstrate a core structural principle he calls the
"five-second moment" — every great story is ultimately about a single moment of transformation
where the storyteller's understanding of themselves or the world fundamentally shifts. The
five minutes of story exist to set up, contextualize, and deliver that moment. Everything
in the story must earn its place by serving the five-second moment. If a detail does not
contribute to the transformation, it is cut.

His most celebrated Moth stories share common structural DNA: they begin with a scene (not
exposition), they establish stakes early, they use "but" and "therefore" rather than "and
then" to create causal momentum, and they end on a moment of change — not a punchline, not
a lesson, but a felt shift. The audience does not need to be told the meaning; the meaning
emerges from the contrast between who the storyteller was at the beginning and who they are
at the five-second moment.

## Central Thesis
Every compelling personal story is built around a five-second moment of genuine transformation.
The craft of storytelling is the craft of earning that moment — creating the conditions in
which a small shift in understanding lands with seismic emotional force.

## Key Concepts
- **The Five-Second Moment**: The single instant where the storyteller changes. Not the most
  dramatic event, but the most transformative realization. Every element of the story exists
  to deliver this moment.
- **Begin with a Scene**: Never open with exposition, context, or backstory. Drop the audience
  into a specific moment in time and space. "I was standing in the parking lot of a Walmart
  at 2 AM" — not "Let me tell you about a time when I learned something."
- **But and Therefore**: Stories gain momentum from causation and contradiction. "I wanted X,
  but Y happened, therefore I did Z." The chain of "and then" (this happened, and then this
  happened) is the death of narrative energy.
- **Stakes**: The audience must understand what the storyteller stands to lose. Without stakes,
  there is no tension. Without tension, there is no engagement.
- **The Elephant**: Plant a question or concern in the audience's mind early and do not resolve
  it. This is the "elephant in the room" that keeps the audience leaning forward.
- **The Backpack**: Front-load the audience with information they will need later. When the
  payoff arrives, the audience already has the context to feel its full weight.
- **Location Transitions**: Change locations to signal story progression. Each new location
  signals a new scene. Stories that stay in one place feel static.
- **Homework for Life**: Dicks's daily practice of identifying the most storyworthy moment
  of each day. This builds the storyteller's inventory and trains the eye for transformation.

## Application to Marketing
- **Brand storytelling**: Every customer testimonial should be structured around a five-second
  moment — the instant the customer's understanding shifted. Not "they bought our product and
  liked it" but the moment they realized their problem was solved.
- **Video content**: Open videos with scenes, not logos or context. Drop the viewer into a
  moment. The Moth's constraint (begin in the middle) applies to video ads and social content.
- **Email marketing**: Each email should have one five-second moment — one insight, one shift
  in understanding. Emails that try to deliver multiple messages dilute all of them.
- **Social media stories**: Instagram and TikTok stories that follow "but/therefore" logic
  rather than "and then" sequencing create more engaging narrative arcs.
- **Sales conversations**: Train salespeople to tell customer stories using Dicks's structure
  — scene, stakes, transformation. Not feature lists, but transformation narratives.
- **Podcast interviews**: Guide guests toward their five-second moments. The best podcast
  episodes are built around moments of genuine realization, not summaries of achievement.
