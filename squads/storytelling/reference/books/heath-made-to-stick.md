# Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die
> Chip Heath & Dan Heath, 2007

## Summary
*Made to Stick* investigates why some ideas are memorable and others are instantly forgettable. The Heath brothers — Chip, a Stanford business school professor, and Dan, a former researcher at Duke — analyzed hundreds of naturally "sticky" ideas, from urban legends to proverbs to successful ad campaigns, and distilled their findings into the SUCCESs framework: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, and Stories.

The book opens with a powerful comparison: the urban legend about kidney theft (a man wakes in a bathtub of ice with his kidneys removed) spreads effortlessly across cultures, while carefully crafted corporate messages are forgotten within minutes. The difference is not budget or distribution — it is structural. The kidney story is simple (one core message), unexpected (a shocking violation of expectations), concrete (vivid sensory details), credible (friend-of-a-friend sourcing), emotional (visceral fear), and a story (narrative arc). Most professional communication fails on every one of these dimensions.

The authors systematically address each element with research evidence and practical techniques. They introduce the concept of the "Curse of Knowledge" — the cognitive bias that makes it nearly impossible for experts to communicate with non-experts because they cannot un-know what they know. This curse is the primary enemy of sticky communication, and the SUCCESs framework is designed to overcome it.

Each chapter provides concrete methods for making ideas stickier. For simplicity, they teach the "Commander's Intent" — a single clear sentence that conveys the essential mission. For unexpectedness, they teach "gap theory" — opening a knowledge gap that the audience urgently wants to close. For concreteness, they show how abstract strategies fail while tangible specifics succeed.

## Central Thesis
Stickiness is not an innate quality of certain ideas — it is an achievable property that follows predictable principles. The SUCCESs framework (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories) provides a reliable checklist for making any idea more memorable, shareable, and actionable. The primary obstacle to sticky communication is the Curse of Knowledge: the better you understand something, the harder it is to communicate it simply and vividly.

## Key Concepts
- **Simple**: Find the core of the idea and express it as a compact, profound statement. Simplicity is not about dumbing down — it is about prioritizing ruthlessly. "Commander's Intent" is the model.
- **Unexpected**: Break patterns to get attention, then sustain it by opening knowledge gaps. Surprise gets people to pay attention; curiosity keeps them paying attention.
- **Concrete**: Use sensory language and specific examples rather than abstractions. People remember concrete images; they forget abstract principles.
- **Credible**: Ideas gain credibility through external authorities, anti-authorities (unexpected sources), concrete details, statistics made meaningful, and testable credentials ("see for yourself").
- **Emotional**: People care about people, not numbers. "The Mother Teresa Effect" — we feel more for an individual than for a statistic. Associate ideas with things people already care about, or appeal to self-interest and identity.
- **Stories**: Stories provide simulation (knowledge of how to act) and inspiration (motivation to act). They are the most powerful delivery vehicle for the other five elements.
- **The Curse of Knowledge**: Once you know something, it is extremely difficult to imagine not knowing it. This is the root cause of most communication failures.
- **Commander's Intent**: The military practice of distilling a complex mission into a single, clear priority statement that guides decision-making when plans inevitably change.
- **Gap Theory (Loewenstein)**: Curiosity arises when we perceive a gap between what we know and what we want to know. Effective communication opens these gaps before filling them.
- **The Velcro Theory of Memory**: Ideas stick better when they have more "hooks" — multiple connections to existing knowledge and sensory experience.
- **Schemas and Breaking Schemas**: The unexpected works because humans constantly predict based on schemas. Violating a schema gets attention; explaining the violation creates understanding.
- **The Sinatra Test**: A single powerful example can establish credibility for an entire category. "If I can make it there, I'll make it anywhere."

## Application to Marketing
- **Use SUCCESs as a content quality checklist.** Before publishing any piece of content, evaluate it against all six criteria. Most content fails on "Unexpected" and "Concrete" — it confirms what the audience already believes and uses abstract language.
- **Lead with the gap, not the answer.** Marketing content that opens with "Here is our solution" fails the curiosity test. Instead, open with a mystery, a counterintuitive finding, or a question the audience did not know they had. Then provide the resolution.
- **One message per piece.** The simplicity principle is the hardest for marketers to follow because stakeholders want to include multiple messages. Insist on a single core idea. If you say three things, you say nothing.
- **Replace data with stories that contain data.** The emotional principle means that statistics should be embedded within human narratives, not presented in isolation. "Our customer reduced costs by 40%" is forgettable. The story of how they did it — the struggle, the doubt, the breakthrough — is sticky.
- **Apply the Sinatra Test to case studies.** Identify your single most impressive, most transferable customer success and use it as proof of concept for your entire value proposition. One undeniable example outweighs a dozen mediocre ones.
