# Save the Cat! Goes to the Movies
> Blake Snyder, 2007

## Summary

*Save the Cat! Goes to the Movies* is Blake Snyder's second book, serving as a companion volume to his original *Save the Cat!* by providing detailed Beat Sheet breakdowns of fifty well-known films. The book is organized around Snyder's ten genre categories, with five films analyzed per genre, demonstrating that the Beat Sheet and genre system are not abstract theories but observable patterns in successful films across decades and styles.

The real value of the book lies in its genre system. Snyder's ten genres do not correspond to the conventional categories used by video stores and streaming services (comedy, drama, thriller). Instead, they classify stories by their deep structural DNA—the core dramatic question, the type of hero required, the nature of the conflict, and the kind of transformation that resolves the story.

Two films that look nothing alike on the surface—say, *Jaws* and *The Exorcist*—belong to the same genre (Monster in the House) because they share the same structural logic: a monster, a confined space, and a sin that brought the monster forth.

For each of the fifty films, Snyder identifies all fifteen beats, shows how the genre conventions are fulfilled, and highlights where the filmmakers made particularly effective or surprising structural choices. This granular analysis makes the book an extraordinarily useful reference for anyone who needs to understand how story structure operates in practice, not just in theory.

The accumulated evidence across fifty films makes a persuasive case that the Beat Sheet is descriptive (it maps what successful stories actually do) rather than merely prescriptive (it dictates what stories should do). The book also deepens the reader's understanding of each genre's specific requirements—the obligatory scenes, the character types, the kinds of stakes that work best, and the pitfalls that derail stories within each category.

## Central Thesis

Snyder's core argument is that stories are not infinitely varied—they cluster into a small number of structural families (genres), each with its own DNA, and within each genre, the Beat Sheet reveals a shared rhythm. This is not a limitation but a liberating insight: once you know which genre your story belongs to, you know its rules, its audience expectations, and its most common failure modes.

You can then innovate within the genre's constraints rather than fumbling in the dark. The fifty breakdowns serve as proof by exhaustive demonstration that these patterns hold across vastly different films, eras, and sensibilities.

## Key Concepts

- **Monster in the House (MITH)**: Requires a monster (any overwhelming threat), a house (a confined space the characters cannot easily escape), and a sin (a moral transgression that summoned the monster). Examples: *Jaws*, *Alien*, *The Exorcist*, *Saw*. The sin element is often overlooked but is structurally essential—it gives the monster a reason to exist.

- **Golden Fleece (GF)**: A road story where the hero pursues a tangible goal across geography, but the real treasure is the internal growth that occurs along the way. The journey changes the hero more than the destination. Examples: *Star Wars*, *The Wizard of Oz*, *Back to the Future*.

- **Out of the Bottle (OOTB)**: A wish-fulfillment or curse story where magic disrupts normal life. The lesson is always that the magic is not the answer—the hero must learn to value what they already had. Examples: *Liar Liar*, *Bruce Almighty*, *Freaky Friday*.

- **Dude with a Problem (DWAP)**: An ordinary person thrust into extraordinary circumstances through no fault of their own. The hero has no special skills—only ordinary human resourcefulness. Examples: *Die Hard*, *Titanic*, *Schindler's List*.

- **Rites of Passage (ROP)**: Stories about life transitions—adolescence, midlife crisis, death, addiction—where the hero must accept a painful truth about growth and change. These are often the hardest genre to make commercially successful because the enemy is internal. Examples: *Napoleon Dynamite*, *The Graduate*, *Ordinary People*.

- **Buddy Love (BL)**: Two characters who need each other to be complete. Not always romantic—can be buddy cops, parent-child, even human-animal. The structural requirement is mutual transformation. Examples: *Rain Man*, *When Harry Met Sally*, *E.T.*.

- **Whydunit (WD)**: A mystery or investigation story where the real question is not "who did it?" but "why?"—what dark truth about human nature does the crime reveal? The investigation transforms the investigator. Examples: *Chinatown*, *Mystic River*, *Citizen Kane*.

- **The Fool Triumphant (FT)**: An underdog or seemingly stupid character who triumphs over a powerful establishment. Requires a fool, an establishment, and a transmutation where the fool's apparent weakness becomes their greatest strength. Examples: *Forrest Gump*, *Being There*, *Legally Blonde*.

- **Institutionalized (INST)**: Stories about groups, organizations, or systems where the individual must decide whether to join, reform, or escape the institution. The genre explores the tension between individual identity and group belonging. Examples: *The Godfather*, *M*A*S*H*, *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*.

- **Superhero (SH)**: A hero with extraordinary abilities who must contend with the burden of being special—including a nemesis, a curse that comes with the gift, and the challenge of being misunderstood by the ordinary world. Examples: *Superman*, *Gladiator*, *A Beautiful Mind*.

- **Genre Conventions vs. Genre Innovations**: Each genre has obligatory elements that the audience expects. Innovation comes not from abandoning these conventions but from fulfilling them in unexpected ways.

- **The Genre as Contract**: Choosing a genre is making a promise to the audience about what kind of emotional experience they will have. Violating genre conventions without replacing them with something equally satisfying breaks the contract.

## Application to Marketing

- **Genre Classification for Brand Narratives**: Every brand story fits one of these ten genres. A startup disruption narrative is often "Fool Triumphant" (scrappy underdog defeats corporate establishment). A cybersecurity brand story is "Monster in the House" (the threat is the monster, the network is the house, negligence is the sin). Identifying your genre gives you a structural playbook.

- **Obligatory Scenes for Content Types**: Each genre has obligatory scenes—moments the audience expects and needs. In marketing, these translate to content that must exist in your funnel. A "Monster in the House" brand must show the monster vividly (the problem content), the sin clearly (why it happened), and the defeat specifically (the solution). Skipping obligatory scenes leaves the audience unsatisfied.

- **50 Structural Templates**: The fifty breakdowns function as templates for marketing narratives. Need to structure a customer case study? Find a film in the matching genre and map your story onto its beat structure. The level of specificity makes this practical.

- **Audience Expectation Management**: Genre is a contract with the audience. If your brand has been telling "Golden Fleece" stories (journey of discovery), suddenly switching to "Monster in the House" (fear-based marketing) will confuse and alienate your audience. Genre consistency builds trust.

- **Innovation Within Convention**: The best marketing storytelling, like the best filmmaking, does not abandon genre conventions but fulfills them in surprising ways. Understanding the conventions allows you to subvert expectations purposefully rather than accidentally.

## Critiques and Limitations

- Some film critics argue that Snyder's beat-by-beat breakdowns are retrofitted—he finds the beats because he is looking for them, not because they were intentionally placed by the filmmakers.
- The fifty films skew heavily toward mainstream American cinema. International films, experimental narratives, and non-Western storytelling traditions are underrepresented.
- Some genre classifications feel like stretches. Not every film fits cleanly into one of the ten categories, and forcing the fit can obscure what makes a particular film unique.

Despite these limitations, the book provides an unmatched library of structural analysis that is immediately useful for anyone building narrative content.
