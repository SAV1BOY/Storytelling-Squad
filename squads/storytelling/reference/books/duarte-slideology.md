# slide:ology
> Nancy Duarte, 2008

## Summary

Nancy Duarte's *slide:ology* is a comprehensive guide to visual storytelling in presentations, focused on the design principles that transform slides from cluttered text walls into powerful visual communication tools. Duarte, whose firm Duarte Design has created presentations for some of the world's most influential speakers and companies, argues that the slide deck is not a teleprompter for the presenter or a document for the audience to read—it is a visual medium that should amplify and reinforce the spoken narrative, not duplicate it.

The book covers the full spectrum of presentation design: ideation (how to plan a presentation before opening PowerPoint), diagramming (how to transform abstract concepts into visual relationships), data visualization (how to make numbers tell stories), composition (how to arrange elements on a slide for maximum clarity and impact), typography (how to use text sparingly and effectively), and the use of imagery, color, and white space. Duarte treats each of these as a craft skill that can be learned and practiced, not an innate talent reserved for designers.

A central theme is the distinction between "sliduments" (dense, text-heavy slides that try to serve as both presentation aids and standalone documents) and true presentation slides (sparse, visual, and designed to complement the speaker's words). Duarte makes the case that sliduments fail at both purposes: they are too cluttered to support a live presentation and too fragmentary to function as readable documents. The solution is to create separate artifacts for each purpose—slides for the presentation, a document for distribution—and to design each for its intended medium.

The book also emphasizes the narrative function of slide design. Slides are not isolated frames; they are scenes in a visual story. The sequence, pacing, and visual rhythm of slides should mirror the narrative structure of the presentation itself—building tension, providing relief, and climaxing with the key insight. Duarte provides frameworks for planning this visual narrative before designing individual slides.

## Central Thesis

Duarte's thesis is that presentation design is a form of visual storytelling, and like all storytelling, it is governed by principles that can be learned, practiced, and mastered. The current state of most business presentations—dense text, bullet-point lists, clip art, gratuitous animations—represents not the inherent limitations of the medium but a failure to apply basic design and narrative principles. A well-designed presentation, where visuals amplify the spoken narrative rather than competing with it, is dramatically more persuasive, memorable, and engaging than a text-heavy one. The gap between most presentations and great presentations is not talent—it is knowledge of principles and willingness to invest in design.

## Key Concepts

- **Slides Are Visual, Not Textual**: A slide is a visual medium. Its primary communication channel is imagery, diagram, and spatial arrangement—not written text. Text on slides should be minimal: a headline, a label, a key number. Long text belongs in a separate document.
- **The Slidument Problem**: Sliduments—decks crammed with text intended to serve as both presentation aid and standalone document—fail at both purposes. They overwhelm audiences during presentations and lack the context needed to stand alone as documents. Separate the two artifacts.
- **The Three-Second Rule**: A slide should communicate its core idea within three seconds. If the audience needs longer to parse the slide, it is too complex. Simplify, remove, or split into multiple slides.
- **Diagrams as Thinking Tools**: Abstract concepts (processes, relationships, hierarchies, cycles) should be expressed as diagrams, not described in text. Duarte provides a taxonomy of diagram types (flow, cluster, radial, matrix, timeline) and guidance on choosing the right one for each concept.
- **Data Visualization Principles**: Data should tell a story. Every chart needs a clear point—a headline that states the insight, not just the topic. Color should highlight the key data, not decorate. Chart types should match the relationship being shown (comparison, composition, distribution, relationship over time).
- **The Glance Test**: Show a completed slide to a colleague for three seconds, then take it away. Can they tell you the main point? If not, redesign.
- **White Space as Communication**: Empty space on a slide is not wasted space—it is active communication. White space directs attention, creates emphasis, and provides visual breathing room. The most common design mistake is filling every pixel.
- **Visual Hierarchy**: Every slide should have a clear visual hierarchy—a primary element that draws the eye first, a secondary element that provides context, and (rarely) a tertiary element that adds detail. Without hierarchy, the audience does not know where to look.
- **Slide Transitions as Narrative Beats**: The transition between slides is a narrative moment—a beat in the visual story. The sequence should have rhythm: buildup slides that create tension, reveal slides that deliver the payoff, breathing slides that provide rest.
- **The Power of One**: One idea per slide. One point per chart. One image per concept. Restraint is the hallmark of professional presentation design. When in doubt, split the slide.
- **Color as Meaning**: Color should carry semantic meaning in presentations—consistent use of a color for a specific concept, data series, or emotional tone. Decorative color creates noise; semantic color creates clarity.
- **Photography Over Clip Art**: Full-bleed, high-quality photography creates emotional impact and professionalism. Clip art and stock illustrations signal amateurism and undercut credibility. When using photography, choose images that create an emotional response aligned with the message.
- **Typography Discipline**: Use no more than two typefaces per presentation. Make text large enough to read from the back of the room. Use bold and size, not underline and italic, for emphasis. Align text consistently.

## Application to Marketing

- **Presentation Templates for Sales Teams**: Sales teams armed with well-designed presentation templates close more effectively than those using improvised sliduments. Applying Duarte's principles to sales deck design—visual clarity, one idea per slide, data that tells a story, minimal text—creates a competitive advantage. The deck becomes a visual narrative that supports the salesperson's spoken story rather than competing with it.
- **Social Media Graphics and Visual Content**: Duarte's principles transfer directly to social media visual design. The three-second rule, visual hierarchy, the power of one idea per image, white space, and color-as-meaning all apply to Instagram posts, LinkedIn carousels, infographics, and ad creatives. Visual content designed with these principles outperforms cluttered alternatives.
- **Data Storytelling in Reports and Dashboards**: Marketing reports and dashboards often fail because they present data without story. Applying Duarte's data visualization principles—a clear insight headline on every chart, color highlighting the key finding, chart type matched to the relationship—transforms reports from data dumps into persuasive narratives.
- **Webinar and Video Slide Design**: Webinars and video presentations are increasingly central to content marketing. Slides designed for these formats need to apply Duarte's principles even more rigorously, because the audience can easily leave. Visual clarity, pacing, S.T.A.R. moments (from *Resonate*), and the three-second rule determine whether viewers stay or click away.
- **Brand Presentation Systems**: Duarte's principles inform the creation of brand-wide presentation systems—standardized templates, approved color palettes, diagram libraries, and image guidelines—that ensure every presentation from every team member reinforces the brand's visual identity and narrative standards. This is particularly valuable for organizations where many people present on the brand's behalf.

## Notable Quotes

- "Slides are not a document. They are a visual medium. The moment you fill them with text, you have broken the medium."
- "If your audience is reading your slides, they are not listening to you. And they cannot do both."
- "White space is not empty. It is the pause between notes that makes music out of noise."
- "Every chart needs a headline that states the insight, not the topic. 'Q3 Revenue' is a topic. 'Q3 Revenue Grew 40% Despite Market Decline' is a story."
- "The three-second rule: if your audience cannot grasp the point of a slide in three seconds, the slide needs to be redesigned."
- "One idea per slide. If you have two ideas, you have two slides. This is not a rule about slides. It is a rule about how human attention works."
- "The best presentations are designed twice: once for the story they tell, and once for the way that story looks."
