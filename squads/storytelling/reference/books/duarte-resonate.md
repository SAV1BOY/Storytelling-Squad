# Resonate
> Nancy Duarte, 2010

## Summary

Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* is a landmark work on the art and science of persuasive presentations, arguing that the most effective presentations are not information dumps but stories—specifically, stories in which the audience is the hero and the presenter is the mentor who shows them a path from "what is" to "what could be." Duarte, head of the presentation design firm that created Al Gore's *An Inconvenient Truth* slides, draws on her analysis of hundreds of great speeches and presentations to identify the structural patterns that make some presentations transformative while most are forgettable.

The book's central analytical tool is the Sparkline—a visual diagram that maps the emotional trajectory of a presentation by charting its oscillation between "what is" (the current reality) and "what could be" (the envisioned future).

Duarte demonstrates that the most effective presentations—from Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech to Steve Jobs's iPhone launch—follow a consistent pattern: they begin by establishing "what is," then introduce "what could be," and then alternate between the two, creating a rhythmic tension that builds toward a climactic "new bliss"—a vivid picture of the transformed future that the audience is called to help create.

Duarte devotes significant attention to the concept of the audience as hero. The presenter is not the center of the story; the audience is. The presenter's job is to be a mentor figure—a Yoda, not a Luke Skywalker—who provides the vision, the plan, and the call to action that empowers the audience to embark on their own journey.

This reframe has profound implications for presentation design: it means every slide, every data point, every anecdote should be evaluated not by whether it makes the presenter look good but by whether it serves the audience's journey from their current state to the desired future.

The book also introduces the concept of S.T.A.R. moments—Something They'll Always Remember. These are the emotional peaks of a presentation: a dramatic statistic, a powerful image, a memorable demonstration, a personal story, an evocative soundbite. Duarte argues that every presentation needs at least one S.T.A.R. moment to ensure that the core message survives in the audience's memory after the details fade.

## Central Thesis

Duarte's thesis is that presentations are a uniquely powerful medium for change, but only when they are structured as stories rather than as information transfers. The presenter who simply shares data, conclusions, and recommendations is asking the audience to do all the emotional work of caring.

The presenter who structures the same content as a story—with a clear gap between "what is" and "what could be," rising tension, and a climactic vision—does the emotional work for the audience, making transformation feel not just rational but inevitable. The Sparkline is the structural tool that makes this story-based approach repeatable and teachable.

## Key Concepts

- **The Sparkline**: A visual mapping tool that charts a presentation's oscillation between "what is" (current state) and "what could be" (future vision). Effective presentations create a rhythmic back-and-forth that builds tension toward a climactic resolution.

- **What Is vs. What Could Be**: The fundamental dramatic tension in any persuasive presentation. "What is" grounds the audience in shared reality; "what could be" lifts them toward possibility. Neither alone is sufficient—the alternation creates movement and momentum.

- **The Audience as Hero**: The audience is the protagonist of the presentation. The presenter is the mentor. Every element of the presentation should serve the audience's journey from their current state to the transformed future.

- **The Presenter as Mentor**: Drawing on Campbell's archetype, Duarte positions the presenter as the wise guide who has already made the journey and returns to share the boon. The mentor provides the map, the tools, and the encouragement—but the audience must take the journey.

- **S.T.A.R. Moments (Something They'll Always Remember)**: Planned emotional peaks that create lasting memories. Types include: dramatic statistics, evocative visuals, memorable demonstrations, personal stories, and "shock and awe" revelations. Every presentation needs at least one.

- **The New Bliss**: The climactic vision at the end of the presentation—a vivid, concrete picture of the transformed future that inspires the audience to act. This is the presentation's equivalent of the hero's journey's "freedom to live."

- **The Call to Action**: Every presentation must end with a clear, specific call to action that tells the audience exactly what to do next. A presentation without a CTA is a story without a climax—it dissipates rather than resolves.

- **Contrast as Engine**: The engine of a great presentation is contrast—between what is and what could be, between the problem and the solution, between the current pain and the future gain. Without contrast, there is no drama and no motivation to change.

- **The Big Idea**: A single sentence that captures the presentation's core message, structured as: "Your point of view + what's at stake." This is the controlling idea, and everything in the presentation should support it.

- **The Three-Act Presentation**: Beginning (establish "what is" and introduce the gap), Middle (alternate between "what is" and "what could be" with escalating intensity), End (deliver the "new bliss" and call to action).

- **Emotional Contour**: Presentations should have a deliberate emotional shape—tension and release, gravity and levity, data and story—designed to maintain engagement and prevent audience fatigue.

- **Repetition and Refrain**: Great speeches use repeated phrases as structural anchors ("I have a dream," "Yes we can"). These refrains create rhythm, reinforce the core message, and build cumulative emotional power.

## Application to Marketing

- **The Sparkline for Content Strategy**: The Sparkline framework applies to any content that seeks to persuade. A long-form blog post, a sales page, a webinar, a video—each can be mapped as an alternation between "what is" and "what could be." Content that stays too long in "what is" becomes depressing; content that stays too long in "what could be" becomes ungrounded. The oscillation creates momentum.

- **S.T.A.R. Moments for Social Media and Content Marketing**: Every piece of content should contain at least one S.T.A.R. moment—a statistic that shocks, a story that moves, an image that sticks, a line that gets shared. In an attention-scarce environment, the S.T.A.R. moment is what earns the content survival in the audience's memory and feeds.

- **Presenter-as-Mentor for Thought Leadership**: Thought leaders and brand spokespersons should adopt the mentor posture, not the hero posture. A keynote speaker who makes the audience the hero ("You are the ones who will change this industry") generates more buy-in than one who showcases their own achievements.

- **The New Bliss for Landing Pages**: Every landing page needs a "new bliss" section—a vivid, concrete description of the customer's transformed life after using the product. Not features, not even benefits, but a picture of the customer's new reality. This is the emotional payoff that justifies the CTA.

- **Contrast-Driven Copy**: Duarte's emphasis on contrast translates directly to copywriting. Before/after comparisons, "without us/with us" sections, and problem/solution pairs all leverage the same "what is" vs. "what could be" dynamic that powers great presentations.

## Notable Quotes

- "The audience does not need to tune into your message. You need to tune your message to the audience."
- "Presentations are a uniquely powerful tool for change because they combine the logic of data with the emotion of story."
- "If your presentation does not have a S.T.A.R. moment, it will not be remembered. And what is not remembered cannot change anything."
- "The gap between what is and what could be is where all the energy of your presentation lives."
- "You are not the hero of your presentation. The audience is. The moment you understand this, everything about your presentation changes."
- "Great presenters don't move audiences with data. They move them with the meaning of the data—the story it tells about what could be."
- "Every presentation should begin with: What does the audience need from this? Not: What do I want to say?"
- "A presentation is a moment of transformation. If the audience leaves unchanged, the presentation failed—no matter how polished the slides."
