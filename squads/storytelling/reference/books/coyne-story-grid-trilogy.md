# Story Grid Genre Editions
> Shawn Coyne: Story Grid editions for Nonfiction, Action, Thriller

## Summary

Following the success of *The Story Grid*, Shawn Coyne and the Story Grid community expanded the methodology into genre-specific volumes that drill deep into the obligatory scenes, conventions, and structural requirements of individual content genres. The most significant of these are the treatments of Nonfiction, Action, and Thriller—three genres with distinct audiences, value stakes, and structural demands.

The Action genre edition examines stories where the core value at stake is Life vs. Death. Action stories demand a hero who must physically survive against an antagonistic force, and the genre's obligatory scenes include the hero being outmatched by the villain, the hero's initial plan failing, and a climactic confrontation where life hangs in the balance. The conventions include a MacGuffin, a ticking clock, and escalating stakes that compress the hero's options.

The Thriller edition addresses stories where the core value shifts along the spectrum of Life to Unconsciousness (or Damnation). Thrillers differ from Action stories in that the hero is typically an ordinary person thrust into extraordinary danger, and the primary emotion the genre evokes is suspense rather than excitement. Obligatory scenes include the hero discovering the villain's secret, a "hero at the mercy of the villain" scene, and a false ending that suggests the danger has passed before a final revelation.

The Nonfiction edition is perhaps the most innovative, applying Story Grid principles to a form most people do not think of as "story." Coyne argues that effective nonfiction follows the same structural principles as fiction: it must have an inciting incident (the problem that justifies the book), progressive complications (why the problem is harder than it appears), a crisis (the central question the book addresses), a climax (the book's core argument), and a resolution (the new understanding the reader gains).

The nonfiction genre further divides into Academic, How-To, Narrative Nonfiction, and Big Idea categories, each with its own conventions and obligatory scenes. These genre editions transform the general Story Grid principles into precise, genre-specific checklists that editors and writers can apply immediately.

## Central Thesis

The genre editions argue that general storytelling principles are necessary but insufficient—to write, edit, or evaluate a story effectively, you must understand the specific structural requirements of its genre. A thriller that hits all fifteen beats of Snyder's Beat Sheet but lacks a "hero at the mercy of the villain" scene will fail as a thriller.

A nonfiction book that presents useful information but lacks progressive complication will bore its reader despite the value of its content. Genre mastery is the bridge between understanding story structure in the abstract and executing it in practice.

The deeper argument is that genres exist because audiences have evolved specific emotional expectations that different story types satisfy. Action satisfies the need to vicariously experience survival. Thriller satisfies the need to experience danger while maintaining the illusion of control. Nonfiction satisfies the need to resolve cognitive dissonance about a specific domain. Each genre is a delivery vehicle for a specific emotional experience, and its obligatory scenes and conventions are the mechanisms of that delivery.

## Key Concepts

- **Action Genre Obligatory Scenes**: Hero is outmatched, initial strategy fails, hero must adapt and learn, hero and villain in direct confrontation, hero's sacrifice or near-death, climactic battle resolves the Life/Death question.

- **Action Genre Conventions**: A MacGuffin driving the pursuit, a ticking clock compressing decisions, escalating force from the antagonist, allies who fall along the way, a training or preparation sequence, the hero gaining a key advantage through wit or sacrifice.

- **Thriller Genre Obligatory Scenes**: Hero discovers the antagonist's object of desire, the speech in praise of the villain (establishing the villain's power), hero at the mercy of the villain, false ending, final revelation that recontextualizes the entire story.

- **Thriller Genre Conventions**: MacGuffin, red herrings, a clock, the villain is stronger/smarter than the hero, the hero is an ordinary person (not a trained warrior), confined or limited resources, double crosses and shifting alliances.

- **Life vs. Death Spectrum (Action)**: The value spectrum runs from Life (positive) through Unconsciousness and Damnation to Death (negative). Each scene should shift along this spectrum, creating a pulse of dramatic movement.

- **Life vs. Fate Worse Than Death Spectrum (Thriller)**: Thrillers push beyond physical death to damnation—the hero risks not just dying but losing their soul, identity, or moral integrity. This raises the stakes beyond physical survival.

- **Nonfiction as Story**: Nonfiction must follow the Five Commandments. The inciting incident is the problem or question that prompted the book. The progressive complications are the layers of difficulty. The crisis is the central dilemma. The climax is the author's argument. The resolution is the reader's new understanding.

- **Nonfiction Sub-Genres**: Academic (proving a thesis to a scholarly audience), How-To (teaching a skill with clear steps), Narrative Nonfiction (true stories told with fictional techniques), Big Idea (presenting a paradigm-shifting concept to a general audience). Each has distinct obligatory elements.

- **The Controlling Idea by Genre**: Each genre has a default controlling idea template. Action: "Life is preserved when the hero overpowers or outwits the antagonist." Thriller: "Life is preserved when the hero discovers and exploits the villain's weakness." Nonfiction: "[Understanding/Skill] is achieved when the reader grasps and applies [the book's core framework]."

- **Genre Conventions as Reader Promises**: Conventions are the narrative devices readers expect when they pick up a genre book. Fulfilling conventions is not formulaic—it is keeping a promise. Breaking conventions without providing superior alternatives leaves readers feeling cheated.

- **The Villain's Toolbox**: In both Action and Thriller, the villain must have a toolkit of resources, strategies, and escalating tactics. A weak villain makes for a weak story regardless of how compelling the hero is.

- **Scene-Level Genre Compliance**: Every scene must serve the global genre. A thriller scene that plays as pure comedy without advancing the thriller's value stakes is a genre violation, even if it is entertaining in isolation.

## Application to Marketing

- **Genre-Specific Content Frameworks**: Marketing content should be classified by its operative genre, and the obligatory elements must be present. A fear-based email sequence operates in the Thriller genre—it needs a villain reveal, escalating stakes, and a hero-at-mercy moment before the resolution (CTA). A how-to content series operates in the Nonfiction How-To genre—it needs progressive complications and measurable outcomes.

- **Nonfiction Five Commandments for White Papers**: White papers, industry reports, and long-form educational content should be structured using the Nonfiction Five Commandments. Open with the inciting incident (the industry problem). Build progressive complications (the data showing it is getting worse). Present the crisis (the choice the industry faces). Deliver the climax (your recommendation). Close with resolution (the future state if your recommendations are followed).

- **Villain Design for Problem-Agitation Content**: In marketing content that agitates a problem, the problem itself is the villain and must be developed with the same rigor as a fictional antagonist. It needs resources (why it is hard to solve), escalating tactics (how it gets worse), and apparent invincibility (why previous solutions have failed). Weak villain development produces weak urgency.

- **False Ending Technique for Sales Pages**: The Thriller convention of the false ending can be used in sales pages. Present the obvious solution first, then reveal why it is insufficient (the false ending), and then present the deeper, complete solution (your product). This creates a revelation moment that drives conversion.

- **Genre Compliance Audits**: Audit your existing content library using genre-specific checklists. Is your educational content hitting all five nonfiction commandments? Is your fear-based marketing fulfilling thriller conventions? Non-compliance explains underperformance and provides specific remedies.

## Critiques and Limitations

- The genre editions can feel overly prescriptive. While obligatory scenes exist, great stories sometimes succeed by violating or reimagining genre conventions in ways the system does not fully account for.
- The Nonfiction edition, while innovative, is still evolving. The application of fictional story structure to nonfiction is not yet as well-tested as the fiction genre analyses.
- The Story Grid community has produced many supplementary materials, but the quality and consistency vary. The core Coyne texts remain the most reliable references.

Despite these limitations, the genre editions provide the most systematic and actionable genre-specific storytelling guidance available, making them essential references for content that must satisfy genre expectations.
