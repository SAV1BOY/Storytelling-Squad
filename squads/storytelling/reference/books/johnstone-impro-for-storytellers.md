# Impro for Storytellers
> Keith Johnstone, 1999

## Summary

Impro for Storytellers is Keith Johnstone's follow-up to Impro, focusing specifically on the craft of narrative improvisation and the practical mechanics of running improvised theatre. Where Impro was philosophical and exploratory, this book is technical and instructional. Johnstone draws on decades of directing Theatresports and other improvised performance formats to codify the principles that make improvised stories work or fail.

The book's central contribution is the Platform-Tilt-Resolution model of story structure. Platform is the stable opening where we meet characters in their ordinary world. Tilt is the event that disrupts the platform and sends the story in an unexpected direction. Resolution is how the disruption is resolved and the world returns to stability (or reaches a new one). Johnstone argues that most improvised stories fail because performers rush past the platform (they tilt too early) or because they block the tilt when it arrives (refusing to let the story change direction).

Johnstone also provides detailed analysis of why audiences engage with or disengage from stories. He identifies specific structural errors that kill audience interest: bridging (jumping between unrelated ideas), wimping (making offers so weak they have no impact), gagging (going for laughs at the expense of story), and canceling (negating what has been established). For each error, he provides a diagnosis and a remedy.

The book is densely practical, filled with exercises, games, and directorial notes from actual Theatresports performances. Johnstone's tone is characteristically blunt: he names what does not work with precision and explains why, often with uncomfortable honesty about the psychological dynamics that lead performers (and communicators of all kinds) to make poor narrative choices.

While written for improvisers, the book's insights into story structure, audience engagement, and the psychology of creative collaboration have broad application to anyone who builds narratives, from marketers and strategists to screenwriters and educators.

## Central Thesis

Johnstone argues that effective storytelling, whether improvised or scripted, depends on a small number of structural principles that most people violate instinctively. Performers default to blocking, bridging, and wimping because these behaviors feel safe, but safety is the enemy of engaging narrative. The audience wants to see characters affected and transformed by events, and the performer's job is to allow that transformation rather than protect against it.

The deeper thesis is that narrative is a collaborative contract between storyteller and audience. The audience provides attention and empathy. In return, they expect the storyteller to introduce change, allow consequences, and reach a satisfying resolution. Breaking this contract, by blocking the story, avoiding consequences, or failing to resolve, destroys the audience's trust and engagement.

## Key Concepts

- **Platform**: The stable opening of a story where characters, relationships, and the ordinary world are established. Audiences need time to invest in characters before anything disrupts them.
- **Tilt**: The event, decision, or revelation that disrupts the platform and sends the story in a new direction. The tilt is where the story truly begins.
- **Resolution**: How the disruption is resolved. This can be a return to the original platform, arrival at a new normal, or a transformation of the characters.
- **Tilting Too Early**: A common error where performers rush to create conflict before the audience has invested in the characters. Without platform, the audience does not care about the tilt.
- **Blocking the Tilt**: When a performer refuses to let the story change direction, usually because the change feels threatening or out of control. This kills the story.
- **Bridging**: Jumping between unrelated ideas without developing any of them. Creates a sense of randomness that destroys narrative coherence.
- **Wimping**: Making offers that are too safe, too small, or too vague to matter. The audience needs bold, clear offers that raise the stakes.
- **Gagging**: Sacrificing story for laughs. The quick joke that breaks the narrative spell and resets the audience to zero investment.
- **Canceling**: Negating or undoing what has been established. "It was all a dream" is the extreme example, but canceling operates at micro-levels throughout weak narratives.
- **Reincorporation**: Bringing back an element from earlier in the story. This is the single most powerful technique for creating a sense of meaning and design in improvised narrative.
- **Being Changed**: Characters must be affected by events. Stories where characters remain unchanged are stories where nothing happens, regardless of the external action.
- **Audience as Co-Creator**: The audience projects intention and meaning onto the story. The performer's job is to provide material that rewards this projection.
- **Raising the Stakes**: Progressively increasing what is at risk for the characters. Stakes that remain flat produce flat stories.
- **The Over-Confessing Problem**: Performers who reveal too much too soon rob the story of its mystery and tension. Information should be released strategically.

## Application to Marketing

1. **Content Structure Using Platform-Tilt-Resolution**: Any piece of content, from a blog post to a video to a social media campaign, can be structured using this model. Establish the familiar world (platform), introduce the disruption or insight (tilt), and deliver the payoff (resolution). This simple structure prevents the two most common content failures: starting with the pitch (no platform) and ending without a point (no resolution).

2. **Avoiding Narrative Errors in Campaigns**: Johnstone's taxonomy of errors (bridging, wimping, gagging, canceling) provides a diagnostic checklist for marketing content. A campaign that jumps between unrelated messages is bridging. A campaign that makes weak claims is wimping. A campaign that prioritizes cleverness over clarity is gagging.

3. **Reincorporation in Brand Campaigns**: Multi-touchpoint campaigns that bring back elements from earlier touchpoints create a sense of coherence and design that builds brand affinity. This is reincorporation applied to marketing: the callback, the running theme, the visual motif that returns in a new context.

4. **Stakes Escalation in Nurture Sequences**: Email nurture sequences and content funnels can apply Johnstone's stakes-raising principle. Each touchpoint should increase what is at stake for the prospect, moving from general awareness to specific urgency.

5. **Platform Before Pitch**: Johnstone's insistence on establishing platform before tilting maps directly to the marketing principle of building context before asking for action. Marketers who rush to the call-to-action without establishing the problem, the world, and the character are tilting before the audience is invested.

## Notable Quotes

- "Audiences can take almost anything if the performers are willing to be changed by it."
- "Tilting too early is like proposing marriage on a first date. The other person has not invested enough to care about the answer."
- "Wimping is the improviser's way of staying safe. It is also the fastest way to lose an audience."
- "Reincorporation is the closest thing to magic in storytelling. When something from the beginning returns at the end, the audience feels that the story was meant to be."
- "Gagging kills stories. The quick laugh is the enemy of the long engagement."
- "Platform is not boring. Platform is where the audience falls in love with the characters. Without that love, no tilt matters."
- "Canceling is the storyteller's cowardice made visible. When you undo what you have established, the audience learns not to invest."
- "The audience does half the work. They project meaning, intention, and destiny onto your characters. Your job is to give them material worthy of that projection."
