# Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre
> Keith Johnstone, 1979

## Summary

Impro is one of the most influential books ever written on creativity, spontaneity, and human interaction. Keith Johnstone, a British-Canadian theatre director and educator, wrote it as an account of his discoveries about what blocks creativity and what liberates it. While the book is grounded in theatre improvisation, its insights extend far beyond the stage into communication, leadership, education, and any domain where human beings interact and create together.

The book is organized around four major themes: Status, Spontaneity, Narrative Skills, and Masks and Trance. The Status chapter is the most widely cited outside of theatre circles. Johnstone demonstrates that every human interaction involves a status transaction, a subtle negotiation of relative position that operates through body language, eye contact, spatial positioning, and speech patterns. This analysis provides a remarkably practical lens for understanding power dynamics in any context, from boardrooms to sales calls to dinner parties.

The Spontaneity section explores why adults lose the creative freedom they had as children and how improvisational training can restore it. Johnstone argues that education systematically trains people to be "uncreative" by punishing mistakes and rewarding caution. Improvisation reverses this conditioning by creating environments where the first impulse is honored, mistakes are treated as gifts, and the need to be "original" is replaced by the discipline of being "obvious."

The Narrative Skills section examines how stories work at a structural level, with particular attention to the concepts of blocking and accepting "offers." When one improviser makes an offer (introduces an idea, character, or situation), the other can accept it (build on it) or block it (deny or redirect it). Blocking kills stories; accepting grows them. This principle has profound implications for collaboration, innovation, and communication far beyond the theatrical context.

The Masks and Trance section is the most experimental, exploring how wearing masks triggers altered psychological states that access deeper creative reserves. While less directly applicable to business contexts, this material illuminates the relationship between identity, performance, and authenticity. It suggests that people carry invisible "masks" in everyday life, professional personas, social roles, and that awareness of these masks can liberate more authentic expression.

Impro has influenced fields ranging from organizational development to psychotherapy to design thinking. Its concepts, particularly status transactions and "Yes, And," have become standard vocabulary in business training, even among people who have never read the book.

## Central Thesis

Johnstone's central thesis is that creativity and spontaneity are natural human capacities that are systematically suppressed by education and social conditioning. The purpose of improvisational training is not to teach people something new but to remove the blocks that prevent them from accessing what they already know. The "talented" improviser is not the one with the most skill but the one with the fewest inhibitions.

He extends this to argue that the dynamics of status, offers, and blocking operate in all human interactions, not just theatrical ones. Understanding these dynamics gives people a practical toolkit for more effective communication, collaboration, and creativity. The book is ultimately about how people can be more alive, responsive, and connected in every interaction. It is a book about removing the armor that institutions and education have built around natural human expressiveness.

## Key Concepts

- **Status Transactions**: Every interaction involves a negotiation of relative status. People play high status (confident, still, taking space, making eye contact) or low status (deferential, fidgety, contracting, avoiding eye contact). Status is performed, not inherent, and can be changed at will.
- **Status as a Seesaw**: When one person raises their status, the other typically lowers theirs. Skilled communicators can raise or lower status deliberately to achieve specific effects in any interaction.
- **The Status Gap**: The audience's interest is proportional to the gap between the statuses of two characters. Wide gaps create tension and comedy; narrow gaps create equality and connection. Managing this gap is a core skill.
- **Blocking vs. Accepting Offers**: An "offer" is any idea, suggestion, or action introduced by another person. "Accepting" means building on the offer. "Blocking" means denying, redirecting, or ignoring it. Blocking is the primary killer of creativity, collaboration, and story.
- **"Yes, And..."**: The foundational principle of improvisation. Accept what is offered and add to it. This principle has become the most widely adopted improv concept in business and is the antidote to blocking.
- **Spontaneity and the First Impulse**: The first idea that comes to mind is usually the most authentic and useful. Training people to trust their first impulse, rather than censoring it, produces better creative work and more genuine communication.
- **Being Obvious**: Johnstone argues that trying to be original is a form of blocking. The attempt to be clever or different disconnects the performer from the audience. Being "obvious," responding naturally and authentically, produces work that feels true and connected.
- **The Fear of Failure**: Most creative blocks stem from the fear of being wrong, looking foolish, or being judged. Removing this fear, primarily through environmental design, unlocks creativity. The leader's job is to make failure safe.
- **Masks and Altered States**: Physical masks can trigger psychological shifts that bypass the censoring mechanisms of the conscious mind, accessing deeper creative impulses and different aspects of personality.
- **Trance and Presence**: Deep creative work requires a state of absorbed attention that Johnstone compares to trance. Modern performers achieve this through training and environmental design rather than ritual.
- **The Teacher's Responsibility**: Johnstone argues that the teacher or director's primary job is to create an environment where people feel safe enough to take risks, not to provide content or instruction. Safety produces creativity; pressure produces caution.
- **Reincorporation**: Bringing back an element from earlier in a story or scene. This creates a sense of structure and meaning that audiences find deeply satisfying. It is one of the most powerful tools in improvised narrative.
- **The Imagination Is Already Running**: People do not need to "activate" their imagination. They need to stop censoring it. The imagination is always generating material; social conditioning teaches us to filter it out.
- **Status Experts Read Rooms**: The ability to read the status dynamics in a room, who is playing high, who is playing low, where the tensions and alliances are, is a fundamental skill for anyone who works with groups.

## Application to Marketing

1. **Status Dynamics in Sales and Persuasion**: Understanding status transactions transforms sales conversations, client presentations, and negotiation. A marketer who can read and adjust status dynamics in real time builds rapport, manages power dynamics, and communicates more persuasively. Playing slightly lower status than the client, for example, creates trust and openness.

2. **"Yes, And" for Creative Brainstorming**: The blocking/accepting framework provides a concrete protocol for creative sessions. Teams that practice "Yes, And" generate more ideas, build on each other's contributions, and produce more innovative work than teams that evaluate and critique during ideation. The rule is simple: no idea can be killed during the generative phase.

3. **Being Obvious as Brand Authenticity**: Johnstone's insight that trying to be original is counterproductive applies directly to brand voice. Brands that try too hard to be clever or different often lose authenticity. Brands that are "obvious," saying clearly and directly what they mean, tend to resonate more deeply with audiences.

4. **Spontaneity in Content Creation**: The principle of trusting the first impulse applies to content creation. Teams that overwork and over-edit content often produce bland, safe material. Processes that capture and honor initial creative impulses (while still applying craft in revision) produce more vital, distinctive content.

5. **Status in Brand Positioning**: Brands communicate status through every touchpoint, from typography to tone of voice to customer service scripts. Understanding status dynamics helps marketers make deliberate choices about whether the brand plays high status (authoritative, premium, expert) or low status (accessible, humble, approachable, human).

## Strengths and Limitations

Impro's greatest strength is the originality and depth of its insights. Written in 1979, the book remains ahead of most contemporary work on creativity, status, and collaboration. The status analysis in particular has no real equivalent in business literature, despite being more practically useful than most leadership books.

The writing is exceptionally clear and specific. Johnstone describes what he observed, what he tried, and what worked, with a teacher's eye for precision and a willingness to share failures alongside successes.

The "Yes, And" principle has become the most widely adopted improv concept in business, and for good reason. It provides a simple, memorable rule that immediately improves the quality of creative collaboration and brainstorming.

A limitation is that the book was written for a theatre audience and requires translation for business contexts. The Masks and Trance chapter, while fascinating, has limited direct applicability to marketing or business communication.

The book's structure is more discursive than systematic. It reads as a series of discoveries and reflections rather than a structured framework. This makes it rich and surprising but harder to use as a reference.

Johnstone's educational philosophy, while liberating, can be taken to an extreme where all structure and critique is avoided. In practice, both spontaneity and discipline are needed, and the book leans heavily toward the former.

The book is also a product of its time in some respects. Some examples and framings may feel dated, though the underlying principles remain as relevant as ever.
