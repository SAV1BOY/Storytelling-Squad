# On Writing: A Memoir of the Craft
> Stephen King, 2000

## Summary
Stephen King's *On Writing* is half memoir and half masterclass, combining the story of King's development as a writer with practical, opinionated advice on the craft of writing. The memoir sections trace his journey from a working-class childhood through early struggles, addiction, and recovery to the near-fatal van accident that almost ended his career. These sections are not mere autobiography — they illustrate King's conviction that a writer's life and a writer's work are inseparable, and that the experiences of living are the raw material of fiction.

The craft sections are dense with practical advice, delivered in King's characteristically direct, unpretentious voice. He insists that writing is a craft that can be learned through practice and discipline, not a mystical gift bestowed on the chosen few. His fundamental tools — vocabulary, grammar, style, and the paragraph — are presented as literal tools in a toolbox, to be maintained, sharpened, and deployed with purpose. His most famous piece of advice — "the road to hell is paved with adverbs" — reflects his broader philosophy that strong writing relies on precise nouns and active verbs, not on modifiers that prop up weak word choices.

King's approach to story creation is notably organic. He begins with a situation rather than a plot, places characters in that situation, and then writes to discover what happens. He distrusts plotting as a conscious activity, arguing that stories are found rather than made — the writer excavates a pre-existing form rather than constructing one from blueprints. This does not mean stories lack structure; it means the structure emerges from the characters and the situation rather than being imposed from outside.

The book also addresses the practical habits of writing: King advocates for a daily word count (at least 1,000 words), a dedicated writing space with a closed door, and a first-draft process that is fast and uninterrupted. The revision process — which King sees as the real work — involves cutting at least 10% and focusing on resonance, truth, and "the ideal reader" (his wife Tabitha, in his case).

## Central Thesis
Writing is a craft built on teachable skills and daily discipline, not on inspiration or innate genius. The writer's primary tools are vocabulary, grammar, and the relentless pursuit of precise, honest language. Stories are best discovered through situation and character rather than plotted in advance, and the revision process is where raw material becomes art. Writing is also a form of telepathy — the writer transmits images, emotions, and experiences directly into the reader's mind across time and space.

## Key Concepts
- **The Writer's Toolbox**: Vocabulary, grammar, style, and the paragraph are literal tools. The writer must master them at the mechanical level before attempting artistic expression. A poor mechanic cannot be a great artist.
- **Adverbs and Weak Writing**: "The road to hell is paved with adverbs." Adverbs signal that the writer has not found the right verb. Strong writing uses precise, active language that does not need modification.
- **Situation Over Plot**: King begins with a "what if" situation rather than a planned sequence of events. He places characters in a situation and writes to discover what they do. This produces organic, surprising narratives.
- **Writing as Excavation**: Stories are like fossils — they already exist in some form, and the writer's job is to excavate them as carefully and completely as possible without breaking them.
- **Writing as Telepathy**: The writer creates images and experiences in their mind, encodes them in language, and the reader decodes them — sometimes centuries later. This makes writing a form of telepathic communication.
- **The Closed Door and the Open Door**: First drafts are written with the door closed — for the writer alone, without outside input. Revisions happen with the door open — considering the reader, the ideal audience, and the world.
- **The Ideal Reader**: Write for one specific person (King writes for his wife). This focuses the prose, creates a conversational tone, and prevents the writer from trying to please everyone.
- **Daily Discipline**: Write every day. Aim for at least 1,000 words. Do not wait for inspiration. Do not take days off. The Muse shows up because you are at the desk, not the other way around.
- **Read a Lot, Write a Lot**: These are the two non-negotiable requirements for developing as a writer. Reading teaches you what works and what does not. Writing teaches you your own voice.
- **Kill Your Darlings**: Be willing to cut your most beloved passages if they do not serve the story. Attachment to individual sentences at the expense of the whole is a form of vanity.
- **2nd Draft = 1st Draft - 10%**: King's formula for revision. Every first draft is too long. Cutting 10% forces the writer to identify and eliminate the unnecessary.
- **Truth Over Technique**: Honest, truthful writing connects even when it is technically imperfect. Technically accomplished writing that lacks emotional truth is dead on the page.

## Application to Marketing
- **Apply the toolbox concept to marketing writing.** Most marketing copy fails at the mechanical level — vague vocabulary, passive voice, cluttered sentences, unnecessary modifiers. Before worrying about strategy or narrative, ensure the basic tools are sharp. Eliminate adverbs, use active verbs, and choose precise nouns.
- **Write for one person.** King's "ideal reader" concept is directly applicable to content marketing. Instead of writing for "our target audience" (an abstraction that produces abstract writing), write for one specific person. This creates the voice, directness, and emotional connection that engage real readers.
- **First draft fast, revise deliberately.** Marketing content often suffers from the opposite of King's approach: slow, committee-driven first drafts and no real revision. Instead, encourage writers to produce fast first drafts (closed door) and then revise ruthlessly (open door), cutting at least 10%.
- **Situation over plot in case studies.** Instead of plotting a case study to hit predetermined messaging points, start with the genuine situation: "What was really happening? What did it feel like?" and let the story emerge. Forced messaging produces mechanical content; genuine situations produce compelling narratives.
- **Kill your darlings in brand messaging.** The clever tagline, the elaborate metaphor, the paragraph the CMO loves — if it does not serve the reader's understanding, cut it. Attachment to clever copy at the expense of clarity is the most common sin in marketing writing.
