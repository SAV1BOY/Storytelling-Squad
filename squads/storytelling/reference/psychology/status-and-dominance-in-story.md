# Status and Dominance in Story
> Improvisation Theory / Performance Psychology / Narrative Dynamics

## Summary
Keith Johnstone, the legendary improvisation teacher and author of *Impro* (1979), developed a theory of status transactions that has become one of the most practically useful frameworks for understanding why scenes, stories, and presentations create or fail to create dramatic interest. Johnstone observed that every human interaction involves a continuous, mostly unconscious negotiation of relative status — and that the moments when status shifts (one person rises while the other falls, or both move simultaneously) are the moments that capture and hold attention.

In Johnstone's framework, status is not social rank or authority — it is a behavioral set. High-status behavior includes stillness, direct eye contact, slow speech, controlled gestures, and taking up space. Low-status behavior includes fidgeting, broken eye contact, rapid speech, self-touching, and physical contraction. Crucially, anyone can play high or low status regardless of their actual social position. A janitor can play high status; a CEO can play low status. The dramatic interest comes from the gap between expected and performed status, and from the transactions where status shifts between characters.

Applied to storytelling and marketing, Johnstone's status theory explains why some brand narratives feel compelling and others feel flat. Every story needs status transactions — moments where the protagonist's status changes. The customer journey is fundamentally a status arc: from low status (struggling, confused, frustrated) to high status (confident, successful, in control). The brand's role is to facilitate this status elevation without claiming high status itself — brands that play high status over their customers create resistance, while brands that elevate customer status create loyalty.

## Central Thesis
Dramatic interest arises from status transactions — shifts in the relative behavioral status between characters. Effective stories continuously negotiate status, and effective brand narratives position the customer's status elevation as the central dramatic arc.

## Key Concepts
- **Status as Behavior, Not Position**: Status in Johnstone's framework is performed through body language, speech patterns, and spatial behavior — it is independent of social rank or authority
- **High-Status Behaviors**: Stillness, sustained eye contact, slow deliberate speech, economy of movement, taking up space, being at ease, not seeking approval
- **Low-Status Behaviors**: Fidgeting, broken eye contact, rapid or apologetic speech, self-touching, physical contraction, excessive smiling, seeking approval
- **Status Transactions**: The moments where relative status shifts between characters — these shifts are the fundamental unit of dramatic interest
- **The Status Gap**: Drama increases as the gap between characters' status widens; comedy often arises from inappropriate status (high-status behavior in low-status situations and vice versa)
- **Status Flexibility**: The most compelling characters (and speakers) can move fluidly between high and low status; rigidity in either direction reduces engagement
- **Seesaw Principle**: When one character raises status, the other typically lowers — status operates as a seesaw between interacting characters
- **Status in Pitch/Presentation**: Presenters who play too high (arrogant, dismissive) alienate; those who play too low (nervous, apologetic) lose credibility; the best presenters modulate status dynamically

## Application to Marketing
- **Customer Status Elevation**: The core promise of any brand story should be customer status elevation — "before our product, you were struggling (low status); after, you are in command (high status)"
- **Brand as High-Status Guide**: The brand should display competence (high status) in service of elevating the customer, not in competition with them — think Yoda, not Luke
- **Vulnerability in Founder Stories**: Founders who show moments of low status (failure, doubt, struggle) before high status (breakthrough, success) create more compelling narratives than those who only perform high status
- **Testimonial Design**: The most effective testimonials show a clear status arc — the customer moving from low status (before) to high status (after) — with specific behavioral details
- **Sales Presentations**: Train sales teams to modulate status — open with approachable low status (curiosity, genuine questions) before demonstrating expertise (calibrated high status)
- **Copywriting Tone**: Copy that plays too high status ("We're the best") repels; copy that plays too low ("We hope you'll consider us") weakens; calibrated copy acknowledges the reader's high status while demonstrating competence
- **Conflict Design in Stories**: Every case study and brand story needs a status conflict — the moment where the protagonist's status is threatened — to create dramatic interest
- **Humor in Content**: Comedy often arises from status violations — brands that can play unexpected low status (self-deprecation, admitting mistakes) create warmth and memorability

## Notable Quotes
- "Status is a confusing term unless it's understood as something one does, not something one is." — Keith Johnstone, *Impro*
- "Every inflection and movement implies a status, and no action is due to chance or really 'meaningless.'" — Keith Johnstone
- "Try to get your status just a little above or below your partner's." — Johnstone's core improvisation instruction
- "The audience doesn't want to see someone stay high or stay low. They want to see the change." — Adapted from Johnstone's teaching
