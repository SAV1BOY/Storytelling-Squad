# The Moth's Best Practices for Storytelling
> The Moth, Established 1997, New York City

## Summary
The Moth is a nonprofit organization dedicated to the art and craft of storytelling. Since
1997, it has hosted thousands of live storytelling events where ordinary people tell true,
personal stories without notes. The Moth's methodology — developed through decades of coaching
storytellers ranging from first-timers to celebrities — represents the most refined practical
framework for personal narrative in existence. Their best practices distill what makes a
five-minute true story land with a live audience.

The Moth's approach begins with a fundamental principle: every story must be true and personal.
The storyteller must have been a participant, not an observer. This requirement creates a
specific kind of vulnerability — the storyteller cannot hide behind third-person distance.
They must own the experience, including the parts that are uncomfortable, embarrassing, or
morally complex. The Moth's coaches have found that the moments storytellers most want to
skip are almost always the moments the audience most needs to hear.

The organization's story development process involves multiple rounds of workshopping where
coaches help storytellers identify the core of their story, strip away unnecessary context,
find the opening scene, and build toward a moment of change. The process consistently reveals
that people's first instinct is to tell too much — too much backstory, too many characters,
too many tangential events. The Moth's craft is the craft of radical reduction: finding the
one thread that matters and following it to its emotional conclusion.

## Central Thesis
The most powerful stories are true, personal, and told with vulnerability. The craft of
storytelling is not addition but subtraction — finding the single thread of transformation
in a complex experience and following it to its emotional core. Stories must be lived, not
observed; owned, not reported.

## Key Concepts
- **True and Personal**: Every Moth story must be true and the storyteller must be a central
  participant. This is non-negotiable. Observed stories, stories about other people, and
  fictional stories do not carry the same weight.
- **The Arc of Change**: Every story must have a beginning, middle, and end — and the
  storyteller must be different at the end than at the beginning. Without transformation,
  there is no story. There is only an anecdote.
- **Start in the Action**: Do not begin with backstory, context, or explanation. Drop the
  audience into a scene. "I was standing in the airport when my phone rang" is a story
  opening. "Let me tell you about a time in my life" is not.
- **Stakes Must Be Clear**: The audience needs to understand what the storyteller stood to
  lose. Without stakes, there is no tension. Without tension, there is no engagement.
- **Vulnerability, Not Therapy**: Moth stories require vulnerability — willingness to share
  failure, shame, fear, and confusion. But the purpose is connection with the audience, not
  catharsis for the speaker. If the storyteller has not processed the experience enough to
  tell it with purpose, it is not ready.
- **The Moment of Change**: Similar to Matthew Dicks's "five-second moment," The Moth
  emphasizes the single point where understanding shifts. Everything in the story builds
  to this moment.
- **Radical Reduction**: First drafts are always too long. The Moth's workshop process
  involves cutting characters, backstory, and tangents until only the essential thread
  remains. If removing an element does not diminish the story, it should not be there.
- **No Notes**: Moth storytellers perform without notes. This forces internalization of the
  story's structure rather than memorization of words, creating more natural delivery.
- **Show, Don't Tell Emotions**: "I was terrified" is telling. "My hands were shaking so
  hard I couldn't hold the phone" is showing. The audience should feel the emotion through
  detail, not be told what to feel.
- **The Dinner Party Test**: Tell it like you would tell a friend at dinner, not like you
  are performing on stage. Conversational authenticity creates intimacy that performative
  delivery destroys.
- **Sensory Specificity**: Concrete, sensory details create immersion. "A hospital" is
  generic. "The fluorescent buzz of the third-floor waiting room at 2 AM" is a world.

## Application to Marketing
- **Customer story extraction**: Use Moth principles when interviewing customers for case
  studies. Ask for specific moments, not general assessments. "Tell me about a specific time
  when..." rather than "How do you feel about...?"
- **Video testimonials**: Coach customers to tell their stories using Moth structure — start
  in the action, build to a moment of change, show vulnerability. Testimonials that follow
  this structure are dramatically more engaging than scripted endorsements.
- **Founder talks at events**: Train founders using Moth methodology before conferences and
  speaking engagements. Start in a scene, establish stakes, build to transformation.
- **Brand storytelling workshops**: Run internal workshops using Moth techniques. Have team
  members tell true personal stories about their work. This builds culture and surfaces
  authentic brand narratives.
- **Podcast content**: Podcast interviews that guide guests toward specific moments of change
  rather than broad career summaries produce more compelling episodes.
- **Social media stories**: Instagram stories and LinkedIn posts that begin in a specific
  moment and build to a realization outperform those that begin with conclusions.
- **Content quality test**: Before publishing any narrative content, apply The Moth's test:
  Is there transformation? Are stakes clear? Does it start in action? Is the vulnerability
  genuine?
- **Story bank building**: Use Moth's curation methodology to build a brand story bank —
  collecting, categorizing, and refining authentic stories from customers, employees, and
  partners for use across marketing channels.
