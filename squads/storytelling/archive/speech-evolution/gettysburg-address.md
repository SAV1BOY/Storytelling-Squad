# Lincoln's Gettysburg Address — Narrative Analysis

## Overview

On November 19, 1863, President Abraham Lincoln delivered a 272-word speech at the
dedication of the Soldiers' National Cemetery in Gettysburg, Pennsylvania. The speech
lasted approximately two minutes. It followed a two-hour oration by Edward Everett, then
considered the nation's greatest speaker. Lincoln's address was initially received with
mixed reviews — some newspapers praised it, others dismissed it as inadequate for the
occasion. Within years, it was recognized as one of the greatest speeches in the English
language.

The Gettysburg Address is a masterclass in narrative compression. In ten sentences, Lincoln
reframed the meaning of the Civil War, redefined the purpose of the United States, and
created a narrative structure that has influenced political and brand communication for
over 160 years.

## Historical Context

### The Battle and Its Aftermath

The Battle of Gettysburg (July 1-3, 1863) was the bloodiest battle of the Civil War, with
approximately 51,000 casualties on both sides. The Union victory was a turning point, but
the war was far from over. By November, the battlefield had been partially converted into
a national cemetery. The dedication ceremony was a political and civic event with thousands
in attendance.

### The Speaking Context

Edward Everett, a former Secretary of State, senator, and university president, was the
featured speaker. His two-hour address was a detailed, classical oration in the style
expected at such events — rhetorical, ornate, and comprehensive. Lincoln was invited
almost as an afterthought, asked to make "a few appropriate remarks."

### Lincoln's Communication Style

Lincoln had developed his communication approach through years of legal practice, political
debate, and self-education. He favored clarity, brevity, and Anglo-Saxon vocabulary over
Latin-derived formality. He was influenced by the King James Bible, Shakespeare, and
Euclid's geometry — texts that valued precision and economy of expression.

## Narrative Analysis

### The Three-Part Temporal Structure

The Gettysburg Address operates on a three-part temporal framework that moves the audience
through time:

**Past — "Four score and seven years ago"**
Lincoln opens by reaching back to 1776, not to 1787 (the Constitution). This is a
deliberate narrative choice. By anchoring the nation's founding in the Declaration of
Independence rather than the Constitution, Lincoln centers the story on "all men are
created equal" rather than on the constitutional compromises that permitted slavery.

**Present — "Now we are engaged in a great civil war"**
The middle section brings the audience to the immediate moment: the war, the battle, the
dead. Lincoln uses this section to diminish his own role and elevate the soldiers': "The
world will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what
they did here." This humility serves the narrative by shifting attention from words to
action.

**Future — "that government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not
perish from the earth"**
The closing section projects forward, transforming the dedication of a cemetery into a
rededication of the nation to its founding principles. The dead are honored not through
memory but through future action.

### Key Narrative Techniques

**Reframing**: Lincoln reframed the Civil War from a political dispute over states' rights
into a test of whether democratic self-governance could survive. This reframing changed
the meaning of every sacrifice: soldiers did not die for territory or political advantage
but for an idea.

**Narrative Compression**: By condensing a complex political and military situation into
272 words, Lincoln forced every word to carry maximum meaning. There is no redundancy,
no decoration, no filler. This compression creates density of meaning that rewards
repeated reading and reflection.

**Anaphora and Rhythm**: The repetition of "we can not dedicate — we can not consecrate —
we can not hallow" creates a rhetorical rhythm that builds emotional momentum. The
parallel structure of "of the people, by the people, for the people" creates a closing
cadence that is both musical and definitive.

**Humility as Authority**: Lincoln's claim that "the world will little note, nor long
remember what we say here" is factually wrong — but narratively brilliant. By diminishing
his own words, Lincoln elevates the soldiers and positions himself as a servant of the
cause rather than its leader. Paradoxically, this humility is what makes the speech
memorable.

**Birth and Death Imagery**: The speech begins with the nation being "brought forth" and
ends with a "new birth of freedom." The cemetery context makes death literal. Lincoln
weaves biological language — conception, birth, death, rebirth — into a political
narrative, giving it the weight of natural law.

### What Lincoln Left Out

Equally remarkable is what Lincoln did not include: no mention of the enemy, no details
of the battle, no list of regiments or commanders, no policy prescriptions, no partisan
language. By excluding specifics, Lincoln created a narrative that transcends its immediate
context and speaks to any audience in any era facing a test of its foundational values.

## Marketing Application

### Principles for Modern Storytellers

1. **Brevity is not the opposite of depth.** The shortest version of your story may be
   the most powerful. Compression forces precision and eliminates everything that does
   not serve the narrative.

2. **Reframe the meaning, not the facts.** Lincoln did not change what happened at
   Gettysburg. He changed what it meant. The most powerful brand stories reframe existing
   realities rather than inventing new ones.

3. **Use temporal structure deliberately.** Past-present-future is a narrative arc that
   works in any context. Anchor your audience in history, ground them in the present
   moment, and project toward a future that requires their participation.

4. **Humility creates authority.** Diminishing your own role in the story elevates your
   audience and, paradoxically, increases trust in your message.

5. **Leave out the specifics that limit universality.** Every detail you include narrows
   your audience. Every detail you exclude widens it. Choose carefully.

6. **End with a call to future action.** The most memorable narratives do not conclude
   with reflection — they conclude with obligation. Give your audience something to do
   with the emotion you have created.
