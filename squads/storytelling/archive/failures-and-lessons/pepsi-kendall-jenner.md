# Pepsi's Kendall Jenner Ad — Failure Analysis

## Overview

In April 2017, Pepsi released "Live for Now Moments Anthem," a television and digital
advertisement featuring model and reality television star Kendall Jenner. In the ad,
Jenner leaves a photo shoot to join a diverse group of young people in what appears to be
a protest march. The ad culminates with Jenner handing a can of Pepsi to a police officer,
who smiles and drinks it, while the crowd cheers. Pepsi pulled the ad within 24 hours of
its release following immediate and intense public backlash.

The ad was widely condemned for trivializing the Black Lives Matter movement and social
justice protests. Bernice King, daughter of Martin Luther King Jr., tweeted a photo of her
father being confronted by police with the caption: "If only Daddy would have known about
the power of #Pepsi." The incident became one of the most studied advertising failures
of the decade and offers critical lessons about the risks of appropriating social
narratives for commercial purposes.

## Historical Context

### The Cultural Context

By 2017, social justice movements — particularly Black Lives Matter — had become central
to American cultural conversation. Images of protests, confrontations with police, and
demands for systemic change were prominent in media. The emotional and political stakes of
these movements were high, with real consequences including injuries, arrests, and deaths.

### The Brand Context

Pepsi had long positioned itself as the youthful, culturally relevant alternative to
Coca-Cola. Previous campaigns like "The Choice of a New Generation" and collaborations
with Michael Jackson had successfully aligned Pepsi with youth culture. The brand's
instinct to connect with current cultural moments was consistent with its historical
strategy.

### The Production Context

Unlike most major brand campaigns, the ad was produced in-house by Pepsi's internal
creative team, Creators League Studio, rather than by an external agency. This is
significant because external agencies typically provide critical distance and cultural
sensitivity review that internal teams may lack.

## Narrative Analysis

### What Went Wrong — The Narrative Failures

**Appropriation Without Understanding**: The ad borrowed the visual language of real
social justice protests — crowds marching, signs with peace symbols, raised fists — but
emptied them of their actual content. No specific cause was identified. No real grievance
was expressed. The protest was aestheticized into a generic celebration of youth
togetherness. This is the narrative equivalent of using someone else's pain as your
set decoration.

**The Savior Narrative**: Kendall Jenner — a wealthy, white, famous celebrity — leaves
her position of privilege (a glamorous photo shoot) to "join" the protest and then
"resolves" the tension by offering a Pepsi to a police officer. This narrative places
Jenner as the hero who solves a complex social conflict with a consumer product. The
implication that a soft drink could bridge the gap between protesters and police was
received as offensive minimization of real suffering and structural inequality.

**Emotional Tone Deafness**: The ad's tone was celebratory and upbeat throughout. Real
protests involve fear, anger, grief, and courage. By presenting a protest as a party, the
ad demonstrated a fundamental misunderstanding of the emotional reality it was referencing.
The narrative emotional register was wrong at every moment.

**Product as Resolution**: The climactic moment — Jenner handing a Pepsi to the officer —
positions the product as the solution to social conflict. This is the most extreme version
of the brand-as-hero narrative: not just the brand solving a customer problem but the
brand solving a societal crisis. The absurdity of this claim is what generated the most
ridicule and anger.

**Lack of Specificity**: The ad was deliberately vague about what was being protested.
Signs read "Join the Conversation" and showed peace symbols. No actual issue was named.
This vagueness was intended to be universally appealing but instead communicated that
Pepsi saw social justice as an aesthetic rather than a reality.

### The Production Failure

The absence of an external agency or diverse creative team meant the ad was created and
approved by people who apparently did not recognize the problems that were immediately
obvious to the public. This represents a systemic failure: when your storytelling team
does not include people with lived experience of the narratives you are referencing, you
will get those narratives wrong.

### The Response Failure

Pepsi's initial statement read: "Pepsi was trying to project a global message of unity,
peace and understanding. Clearly, we missed the mark, and we apologize." This response
failed because it framed the problem as a missed execution ("missed the mark") rather
than a fundamentally flawed concept. The apology addressed the outcome (public backlash)
rather than the cause (cultural appropriation and trivialization).

## Key Lessons

### For Storytellers and Brands

1. **Real movements are not your backdrop.** Social justice movements involve real people
   facing real consequences. Using their visual language without engaging with their
   substance is appropriation. If you cannot name the specific cause, you should not
   reference the movement.

2. **Diverse creative teams are not optional.** Had Pepsi's creative process included
   people with direct experience of protest movements, the problems would likely have been
   identified before production. Diversity in storytelling teams is not a moral nicety —
   it is a quality control mechanism.

3. **Products do not solve social problems.** No consumer product resolves systemic
   inequality. Stories that position products as solutions to complex social issues will
   always ring false. The brand can support a cause, but it cannot claim to be the answer.

4. **Tone must match reality.** When referencing real-world situations, the emotional tone
   of your narrative must respect the emotional reality of those situations. Celebrating
   what is actually painful is the fastest way to alienate an audience.

5. **Vagueness is not universality.** Being deliberately unspecific about social causes
   does not make your message universal — it makes it shallow. Authenticity requires
   specificity. If you are not willing to be specific, you are not ready to engage.

6. **Celebrity does not equal credibility.** Kendall Jenner's fame did not transfer
   credibility to the social message. In fact, her privileged position amplified the
   perception of inauthenticity. The messenger must have a credible connection to the
   message.

7. **Test with the communities you are depicting.** Before publishing stories that
   reference specific communities or movements, show those stories to members of those
   communities. If they find it offensive, your intentions do not matter.

## Marketing Application

The Pepsi ad is now the standard cautionary tale in marketing education. It demonstrates
that the gap between "brand engages with culture" and "brand exploits culture" is narrow
and that crossing it has immediate, severe consequences. The lesson is not that brands
should avoid social topics — the Always #LikeAGirl campaign shows that brand activism
can work brilliantly. The lesson is that authentic engagement requires genuine
understanding, diverse perspectives, and the humility to support rather than co-opt.
