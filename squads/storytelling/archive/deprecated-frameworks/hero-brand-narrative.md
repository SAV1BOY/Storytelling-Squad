# Brand-as-Hero Narrative — Deprecated Framework

**Status: DEPRECATED — Replaced by customer-as-hero approaches**

## Overview

The brand-as-hero narrative is a storytelling framework in which the brand positions itself
as the central protagonist of its own marketing stories. In this model, the brand is the
hero that saves customers from their problems, defeats competitors, and triumphs through
superior products, services, or innovation. The customer is cast as the beneficiary — the
person rescued by the brand's heroism — rather than as the protagonist of their own story.

This framework has been deprecated in favor of customer-as-hero approaches, popularized by
Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework and validated by decades of marketing evidence. The
shift reflects a fundamental insight: audiences do not want to watch a brand be heroic.
They want to see themselves as heroic, with the brand serving as the guide or tool that
enables their success.

## Historical Context

### The Brand-as-Hero Era

For much of advertising history, brands were positioned as heroes. Advertisements showed
the product arriving to solve problems, the company overcoming challenges, and the brand
identity as central to the story. This positioning was natural in an era when brands
controlled the narrative through broadcast media and customers had limited alternatives.

Classic examples include:
- **IBM's dominance narrative**: "Nobody ever got fired for buying IBM" — positioning
  IBM as the safe, heroic choice that protects the buyer
- **Early technology advertising**: Products depicted as revolutionary forces that change
  the world, with the brand as the agent of change
- **Pharmaceutical advertising**: The medicine as hero, arriving to defeat the disease
  and save the patient

### The Shift

The transition from brand-as-hero to customer-as-hero was driven by multiple forces:

**Consumer empowerment**: The internet gave customers choice, voice, and information. They
were no longer passive recipients of brand narratives. They were active evaluators with
alternatives and platforms to share their experiences.

**Content marketing**: As brands became publishers, they discovered that content about
customers outperformed content about the brand. Customer stories generated more engagement,
more shares, and more conversions than brand stories.

**StoryBrand and narrative frameworks**: Donald Miller's "Building a StoryBrand" (2017)
codified the customer-as-hero principle into a practical framework that thousands of
businesses adopted. The book's central argument — that the brand should be the guide, not
the hero — gave marketers a clear alternative to the default approach.

**Authenticity culture**: Audiences in the 2010s and 2020s increasingly valued humility
and authenticity over self-promotion. Brands that talked about themselves were perceived
as less trustworthy than brands that talked about their customers.

## Narrative Analysis

### Why Brand-as-Hero Fails

**It violates audience psychology**: In any story, the audience identifies with the
protagonist. When the brand is the protagonist, the audience is asked to identify with a
corporation — an abstract entity with commercial motives. This identification rarely
occurs. Instead, the audience watches the brand congratulate itself, which generates
indifference or annoyance.

**It creates competitive narratives rather than customer narratives**: Brand-as-hero
stories are often about defeating competitors. This positions the marketing conversation
in a competitive frame that the audience does not care about. Customers care about their
own problems, not about inter-brand rivalry.

**It makes the customer passive**: In the brand-as-hero model, the customer's role is to
have a problem, encounter the brand, and be saved. This passive positioning is
unflattering to the customer and fails to create the aspirational identification that
drives purchase decisions.

**It does not scale narratively**: A brand can only be the hero of so many stories before
the narrative becomes repetitive and self-congratulatory. Customer-as-hero stories are
infinitely varied because every customer has a unique story.

### The Customer-as-Hero Alternative

The customer-as-hero framework recasts the brand as the guide — the Obi-Wan to the
customer's Luke Skywalker, the Gandalf to their Frodo. The guide has wisdom, tools, and
experience. The hero has the challenge, the courage, and the transformation.

**The brand's role changes from protagonist to enabler**:
- Brand-as-hero: "We built the most advanced platform in the industry"
- Customer-as-hero: "Sarah used our platform to grow her business from 5 to 50 employees"

**The brand's voice changes from self-promotion to empathy**:
- Brand-as-hero: "We are committed to innovation and excellence"
- Customer-as-hero: "We understand the challenge of scaling a business because we have
  helped thousands of founders do it"

**The brand's evidence changes from features to transformations**:
- Brand-as-hero: "Our product has 47 features that competitors lack"
- Customer-as-hero: "Our customers have collectively saved 2 million hours this year"

### The Guide Archetype

In the customer-as-hero framework, the brand inhabits the guide archetype. The guide has
two essential qualities:

1. **Empathy**: The guide understands the hero's struggle because the guide has either
   experienced it or has helped many others through it.
2. **Authority**: The guide has the competence, tools, and track record to actually help
   the hero succeed.

Empathy without authority creates sympathy but not trust. Authority without empathy
creates respect but not connection. Both together create the ideal brand positioning:
a trusted advisor who understands your situation and can help you through it.

### Where Brand-as-Hero Still Appears

Despite its deprecation, brand-as-hero narrative persists in several contexts:

- **Founder-led companies** where the founder's personal narrative is the brand narrative.
  This can work when the founder's story is genuinely compelling and is told with
  self-awareness.
- **Innovation announcements** where the company's achievement is genuinely newsworthy.
  A breakthrough product deserves a moment of brand-centered storytelling.
- **Crisis communication** where the brand must demonstrate its own competence and
  commitment to resolution.
- **Employer branding** where the company is the protagonist of its own culture story,
  aimed at attracting employees rather than customers.

In these contexts, brand-as-hero storytelling can be appropriate if used sparingly and
balanced with customer-centered narrative in the broader communication strategy.

## Key Lessons

### Why This Framework Was Deprecated

1. It centers the brand when audiences want to see themselves centered.
2. It creates passive customer positioning that fails to drive aspiration.
3. It generates self-congratulatory content that audiences ignore.
4. It limits narrative variety by making every story about the same protagonist.
5. It has been empirically outperformed by customer-as-hero approaches across engagement,
   conversion, and brand preference metrics.

### The Transition Challenge

Moving from brand-as-hero to customer-as-hero is difficult for organizations because it
requires a form of institutional humility. It asks the brand to step out of the spotlight
and illuminate someone else. For executives who have built careers around their brand's
achievements, this repositioning can feel like diminishment. It is not — it is strategic
maturity.

The most successful transitions happen when organizations recognize that becoming the
guide does not reduce their importance. Gandalf is not less important than Frodo. The
guide is essential — but the guide is essential because of what the hero achieves, not
because of what the guide achieves alone.

## Marketing Application

For the Storytelling Squad, the customer-as-hero principle is foundational. Every story
we tell should answer the question: "Who is the hero of this narrative?" If the answer
is our brand or our company, we should revise. The hero is always the customer, the
community member, the user, or the partner. Our brand is the guide that makes their
heroism possible. This positioning is not only more effective — it is more honest about
the relationship between any brand and its customers.
