# Linear Testimonial Format — Old Q&A Approach

**Status: DEPRECATED — Replaced by narrative testimonial approaches**

## Overview

The linear testimonial format — also known as the Q&A testimonial or structured
testimonial template — is a customer evidence format that presents customer feedback
as a series of questions and answers, typically following a fixed sequence: Who are you?
What was your problem? How did you find our product? What do you like about it? What
results have you seen? Would you recommend it? This format dominated customer evidence
for decades across industries, from B2B case studies to consumer product reviews.

This framework is deprecated not because customer evidence is unimportant — it remains
essential — but because the linear Q&A format strips customer stories of their narrative
power. The format produces content that is structurally predictable, emotionally flat, and
indistinguishable across brands. Narrative testimonial approaches produce content that
engages, differentiates, and persuades.

## Historical Context

### The Origin of Structured Testimonials

The structured testimonial format emerged from the convergence of marketing needs and
operational constraints. Marketing teams needed scalable customer evidence. Customer
success teams needed efficient processes for capturing customer feedback. The Q&A format
solved both problems: it was easy to administer (send a list of questions), easy to
process (organize answers into a template), and easy to approve (customers reviewed
only their own words).

The format was also influenced by journalism practices — the interview as evidence
collection — and by legal caution. Pre-written questions and customer-approved answers
minimized the risk of misrepresentation.

### The Template

A typical linear testimonial follows this structure:

**Section 1 — Company/Customer Background**
"Tell us about your company and your role."

**Section 2 — The Challenge**
"What challenge were you facing before using [Product]?"

**Section 3 — The Search**
"How did you find [Product]? What alternatives did you consider?"

**Section 4 — The Solution**
"How did [Product] address your challenge?"

**Section 5 — The Results**
"What results have you seen since implementing [Product]?"

**Section 6 — The Recommendation**
"Would you recommend [Product] to others?"

### Why It Dominated

The format dominated because it served internal needs efficiently. It was:
- **Scalable**: Any team member could conduct the interview
- **Consistent**: Every testimonial followed the same structure
- **Approvable**: Legal and customer review was straightforward
- **Measurable**: ROI claims could be extracted and standardized

## Narrative Analysis

### Why the Linear Format Fails

**Predictability destroys engagement**: When every testimonial follows the same structure,
audiences learn to skim. They know the challenge comes first, then the solution, then the
results. There is no surprise, no tension, no reason to keep reading. Predictable
structure produces predictable disengagement.

**The interviewer is invisible but omnipresent**: The Q&A format reveals its constructed
nature — someone asked these questions in this order. The customer's "story" is actually
the interviewer's structure filled with the customer's words. The customer is not telling
their story; they are answering someone else's questions about their story.

**Emotional flatness**: The sequential question format produces chronological, rational
accounts. It rarely captures emotion because it does not ask for emotion. "What challenge
were you facing?" elicits a description of a business problem. "Tell me about the moment
you realized something had to change" elicits a story.

**The recommendation section is meaningless**: Every testimonial ends with a positive
recommendation. This is selection bias made visible — no company publishes testimonials
from unhappy customers. The "would you recommend" question adds no information and
signals inauthenticity.

**Interchangeability**: Linear testimonials from competing companies in the same category
are virtually interchangeable. Replace the product name and the testimonial works for any
competitor. This is the same failure as corporate speak — content that is fungible is
content that is useless.

### The Narrative Alternative

Narrative testimonials treat customer evidence as storytelling. The difference is
structural and philosophical:

**Start with the human, not the company**: "When Maria started her bakery in 2019, she
had a vision for what it could become" vs. "Maria is the owner of Portland Bakery, a
small business serving the Portland metro area."

**Find the moment of crisis**: Every customer story has a moment when the status quo
became untenable. Finding and centering that moment creates narrative tension. "The
morning she realized she had double-booked 15 orders and had no way to track which were
fulfilled" is a story. "She was facing inventory management challenges" is a category.

**Show the transformation, not just the outcome**: Numbers matter, but the human
experience of transformation matters more. "Maria now leaves work at 5 PM instead of 9 PM"
is more compelling than "Maria reduced operational inefficiency by 40%."

**Include imperfection**: The most credible testimonials include moments of doubt,
adjustment, or difficulty. "The first month was rough — the team resisted the change"
makes the eventual success more believable than a seamless adoption story.

**Let the customer's voice drive structure**: Instead of imposing a question framework,
let the customer tell their story in their own order, with their own emphasis. The
interviewer guides but does not dictate.

### The Interview Technique Shift

Moving from linear to narrative testimonials requires a different interview approach:

**Old approach**: "What challenge were you facing?"
**New approach**: "Take me back to the day you decided something had to change. What was
happening?"

**Old approach**: "What results have you seen?"
**New approach**: "What is different about your typical Tuesday now compared to a year ago?"

**Old approach**: "Would you recommend our product?"
**New approach**: "If a friend in your industry was struggling with the same thing, what
would you tell them?"

The narrative approach elicits stories by asking for specific moments, sensory details,
and personal reflection rather than categorical summaries.

## Key Lessons

### Why This Format Was Deprecated

1. It produces predictable, emotionally flat content that audiences skip.
2. It serves internal process needs rather than audience engagement needs.
3. It strips customer stories of their narrative power by imposing external structure.
4. It creates interchangeable content that fails to differentiate.
5. It prioritizes data extraction over human storytelling.

### What to Preserve

- The discipline of capturing customer evidence systematically
- The practice of customer approval and review
- The inclusion of specific, measurable outcomes
- The habit of gathering customer stories regularly

These operational practices should be maintained while the narrative framework is
modernized.

## Marketing Application

For the Storytelling Squad, customer evidence is our most powerful narrative asset.
Real customer stories carry more credibility than any brand-produced content. Our role
is to capture these stories with the craft and care they deserve — as narratives with
characters, tension, transformation, and emotional truth, not as completed forms with
predictable answers.
