# John Lewis Christmas Ads — Emotional Storytelling Tradition

## Overview

Since 2007, British department store John Lewis has released an annual Christmas
advertisement that has become a cultural event in the United Kingdom and beyond. These
ads — typically two to three minutes long — tell self-contained emotional stories that
often involve animated characters, unlikely friendships, or poignant family moments. They
rarely feature products directly and instead build brand association through pure emotional
storytelling.

The John Lewis Christmas ad has become one of the most anticipated annual marketing events
in the UK. Each release generates millions of views within hours, dominates social media
conversation, and receives extensive news coverage. The tradition has elevated John Lewis
from a respected but conventional retailer to a brand synonymous with emotional
intelligence and storytelling excellence.

## Historical Context

### The Retail Advertising Landscape

Christmas advertising in the UK retail sector was traditionally promotional — focused on
sales, product ranges, and competitive pricing. Retailers competed on value propositions:
who had the best deals, the widest selection, the most convenient locations. Emotional
storytelling was rare in the category.

### The Strategic Shift

In 2007, John Lewis and its agency Adam & Eve (later Adam & Eve DDB) made a deliberate
strategic decision to differentiate through emotional storytelling rather than promotional
messaging. The rationale was that John Lewis could not compete on price with discount
retailers and needed to build brand preference through emotional connection.

The first significant Christmas ad, "Shadows" (2007), showed children creating shadow
puppets that formed the shapes of products. It was creative but not yet the emotional
powerhouse the series would become. The series found its voice with "The Long Wait" (2011),
which depicted a child impatiently counting down to Christmas — not to receive gifts but
to give one to his parents. The twist ending redefined what a retail Christmas ad could be.

### The Annual Tradition

By 2013, the release of the John Lewis Christmas ad had become a national event. Media
outlets covered the launch. Social media buzzed with predictions and reactions. The ad
became its own cultural product — something people anticipated, discussed, and shared
independently of any purchase intent.

## Narrative Analysis

### The Signature Formula

While each ad tells a unique story, a consistent narrative formula has emerged:

**1. An Unlikely Protagonist**: The central character is often unexpected — a penguin, a
monster under the bed, a snowman, a Venus flytrap, a boy on the moon. These characters
are endearing precisely because they are unconventional. They bypass the viewer's
advertising defenses because they do not feel like marketing.

**2. A Simple Emotional Journey**: Each story follows a clear emotional arc, usually
centered on longing, friendship, or the desire to connect with someone. The plots are
deliberately simple — a child befriends a monster, a penguin wants a companion, a snowman
journeys to find a gift. Simplicity allows the emotion to be universal.

**3. A Musical Backbone**: Each ad features a cover version of a well-known song,
performed in a stripped-down, emotionally resonant arrangement. The music does enormous
narrative work — it sets pace, builds emotion, and creates a sonic signature that becomes
associated with the campaign. Several of these covers have become hit singles.

**4. The Emotional Turn**: Each ad contains a moment — usually near the end — where the
narrative reveals its deeper emotional truth. In "The Long Wait," it is the child giving
rather than receiving. In "Man on the Moon," it is a girl reaching across space to connect
with a lonely old man. This turn is the structural heart of every John Lewis Christmas ad.

**5. Minimal Brand Presence**: John Lewis appears only at the very end, usually as a logo
and tagline. The stories are not about shopping, products, or retail. The implicit message
is: "A brand that tells stories like this understands what matters to you."

### Notable Entries and Their Techniques

**"The Bear and the Hare" (2013)**: Hand-drawn animation style. A bear who always
hibernates through Christmas is woken by an alarm clock gift from his friend the hare.
Technique: the animation style signals warmth and craftsmanship, differentiating the
ad from CGI-heavy competitors.

**"Monty the Penguin" (2014)**: A boy's best friend is a penguin who seems lonely. The
boy gets Monty a companion penguin for Christmas. The reveal: Monty is a stuffed toy
brought to life by the boy's imagination. Technique: the reveal reframes the entire
narrative, inviting the viewer to rewatch with new understanding.

**"Moz the Monster" (2017)**: A boy discovers a friendly monster under his bed. They
become friends but the monster keeps the boy awake. On Christmas morning, the boy gives
Moz a nightlight so they can both sleep. Technique: the gift represents sacrifice and
care — giving up late-night fun so the friend can rest.

### Why the Formula Works

**Emotional Priming**: By establishing an annual tradition of emotional storytelling,
John Lewis has trained its audience to approach each new ad in a receptive emotional
state. Viewers arrive expecting to feel something, which lowers psychological resistance.

**Anticipation as Narrative**: The wait for the annual ad has itself become part of the
story. The months of speculation, the pre-release teasers, and the moment of reveal create
a narrative arc that extends far beyond the ad itself.

**Shareability Through Emotion**: Emotional content is shared more than rational content.
Each ad is designed to generate the response: "You have to see this." The sharing act
extends reach organically and adds social validation to the emotional experience.

## Marketing Application

### Principles for Modern Storytellers

1. **Build annual narrative traditions.** Recurring storytelling moments create anticipation
   and deepen brand connection over time. Each installment adds to a growing mythology.

2. **Separate the story from the sell.** John Lewis proves that you do not need to show
   products to drive purchase intent. Emotional affinity drives commercial results.

3. **Music is a narrative multiplier.** The right musical choice can carry half the
   emotional weight of your story. Invest in sonic storytelling.

4. **Design for the emotional turn.** Every story needs a moment where the audience's
   understanding shifts. This moment is what makes the story memorable and shareable.

5. **Create characters, not mascots.** John Lewis characters are narratively complete —
   they have desires, relationships, and arcs. They are not logos with legs.

6. **Consistency builds expectation.** Audiences who know what to expect emotionally
   arrive ready to feel. This is a compounding advantage over time.
