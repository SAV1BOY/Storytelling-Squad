# Authority Phrases

## Purpose

Authority in marketing storytelling isn't about bragging. It's about demonstrating competence, track record, and depth of understanding in a way that builds confidence. The best authority language is specific, evidence-based, and delivered with enough restraint that the audience trusts it. These phrases help establish credibility for brands, founders, and thought leaders without crossing the line into arrogance.

## Collection

### Track Record Phrases

> Use to reference past experience and results. Track record authority comes from specificity: names, numbers, timeframes, and outcomes.

- "We've been in this space since before it had a name."
- "Over the past [X years], we've worked with [number] companies on exactly this problem."
- "Our team has collectively shipped [number] [products/campaigns/projects]."
- "This is our [Nth] time building in this category. We know where the landmines are."
- "We've seen this pattern play out in [number] organizations."
- "This framework has been tested across [number] industries and [number] companies."
- "We didn't learn this from a textbook. We learned it from [X years] in the field."
- "Our track record speaks for itself: [specific metric] across [specific timeframe]."
- "Three of the last five companies we worked with went on to [achievement]."
- "We've been wrong enough times to know what right looks like."

### Expertise Signaling Phrases

> Use to demonstrate deep knowledge without lecturing. Expertise is most convincing when it's shown through insight, not claimed through titles.

- "Most people focus on [surface issue]. The real leverage point is [deeper issue]."
- "There's a nuance here that's easy to miss, and it changes everything."
- "The conventional wisdom on this is outdated. Here's what the current data shows."
- "Having worked both sides of this problem, I can tell you the real challenge is..."
- "The question isn't [obvious question]. The question is [deeper question]."
- "This is a common mistake, and it's common because it seems intuitive. Here's why it's wrong."
- "We've mapped the entire landscape. Here's what most people don't see."
- "There are three levels to this problem. Most solutions address level one. We start at level three."
- "I've studied [X] for [time period]. The insight that changed my thinking was..."
- "The difference between good and great in this space comes down to [specific detail]."

### Understated Confidence Phrases

> Use to project authority through calm certainty rather than bold claims. Understated confidence is the highest form of authority language.

- "We're comfortable with what we've built."
- "The results have been consistent."
- "This isn't our first time solving this problem."
- "We expected it to work. It did."
- "We're not the cheapest option. We're the one that works."
- "We don't need to oversell this. The work speaks."
- "If you've done your homework, you already know why we're here."
- "We didn't rush this. We got it right."
- "Our clients tend to stay. That tells you something."
- "We're selective about who we work with. That's by design."

### Borrowed Authority Phrases

> Use to leverage the credibility of others: clients, publications, research institutions, or industry figures. Borrowed authority should always be attributed and genuine.

- "As [respected expert] has argued, [insight], and our work confirms it."
- "[Credible publication] identified this as one of the top trends in [year]."
- "When [impressive client] faced this challenge, they chose our approach."
- "[Industry organization] recognized our methodology as a best practice."
- "The research from [institution] aligns with what we've observed in practice."
- "Our approach builds on the work of [recognized thought leader], extended with [our contribution]."
- "[Partner/client] described our impact as [direct quote]."
- "This methodology is endorsed by [number] leading practitioners in [field]."

### Selective Disclosure Phrases

> Use to imply depth without revealing everything. Selective disclosure signals that you know more than you're sharing, which creates intrigue and authority simultaneously.

- "I can't share the full details, but the results were significant."
- "There's a proprietary element to how we do this, but the principle is simple."
- "What I can tell you is this much..."
- "We've developed an approach that we don't publish widely. Here's the headline version."
- "Some of our best insights come from data we can't share publicly, but the pattern is clear."
- "There's more behind this than I can cover here. What matters is the outcome."
- "Our internal research shows [broad finding]. The specifics are what give us our edge."
- "The full methodology is reserved for our clients, but the philosophy is straightforward."

### Credibility Through Honesty Phrases

> Use to build authority by admitting limitations. Paradoxically, saying what you don't know or can't do increases trust in what you claim you can.

- "We're not the right fit for everyone. Here's how to know if we're right for you."
- "We don't do [X]. What we do exceptionally well is [Y]."
- "If you need [X], there are better options. If you need [Y], we're the best."
- "We don't have all the answers. But we have the right questions."
- "Our approach has limitations. Here they are. And here's why we're still the best choice."
- "We'll tell you if we're not the right solution. That's happened, and the honesty built more trust than the sale would have."
- "We're experts in [specific area]. Outside that area, we bring in people who are better than us."
- "There are things we're still figuring out. Here's what we've already solved."

## Usage Guidelines

- **Show, don't claim.** "We're the best in the industry" is a claim. "47 of the Fortune 500 use our platform" is evidence. Always prefer evidence over assertion.
- **Be specific.** Vague authority ("years of experience," "proven track record") is invisible. Specific authority ("11 years, 340 clients, $47M in measurable impact") is concrete.
- **Underclaim and overdeliver.** It's better to set expectations low and exceed them than to set them high and meet them. Understated authority builds lasting trust.
- **Don't compare yourself to competitors directly.** Let the audience make comparisons. Your job is to present your strengths, not attack others' weaknesses.
- **Pair authority with vulnerability.** Pure authority can feel cold. Adding a moment of honesty or uncertainty makes you human and makes the authority more believable.
