# Status Language

## Purpose

Status language, drawn from Keith Johnstone's work in improvisational theater, describes how people signal dominance, authority, deference, or approachability through word choice, syntax, and framing. In marketing and sales, status awareness lets you calibrate your voice to the context: commanding authority in a pitch, creating warmth in a community post, or establishing peer-level credibility with executives. This file catalogues high-status and low-status language patterns for deliberate, strategic use.

## Collection

### High-Status Patterns

> Use in pitches to investors, executive presentations, thought leadership, competitive positioning, and authority-building content. High-status language is direct, certain, and economical.

- "Here's what we know." (Not "Here's what we think.")
- "The data is clear."
- "We've made our decision."
- "This is how it works."
- "Let me be direct."
- "I won't waste your time with context you already have."
- "We anticipated that."
- "That's been addressed."
- "The results speak for themselves."
- "We don't compete on price. We compete on outcomes."
- "I'd rather be right than popular."
- "We chose not to do [X]. Here's why."
- "This is a solved problem."
- "We've been doing this longer than most companies have existed."

**High-Status Structural Patterns:**
- Short, declarative sentences. ("We did it. It worked.")
- Statements, not questions. ("This is the path forward." vs. "Do you think this could work?")
- No hedging language. Eliminate "just," "sort of," "kind of," "maybe," "I think."
- Silence after key points. Let the statement breathe.
- Fewer words, more weight. ("No." is higher status than "No, I don't think that's the right approach for us at this time.")

### Low-Status Patterns (Intentional Warmth)

> Use for community building, customer support content, onboarding, vulnerability-driven storytelling, and grassroots marketing. Low-status language is inclusive, uncertain (by design), and inviting.

- "We're figuring this out together."
- "Honestly, we don't have all the answers yet."
- "We messed this up, and here's what we learned."
- "I'd love your input on this."
- "What do you think?"
- "We're still early in this journey."
- "This is an experiment. Help us shape it."
- "I'm not sure, but here's my best guess."
- "We've got a long way to go."
- "Your feedback literally changed what we built."
- "I was nervous sharing this."
- "I might be wrong about this, and that's okay."

**Low-Status Structural Patterns:**
- Questions instead of statements. ("What if we tried...?" vs. "We're doing...")
- Self-deprecating humor. ("We built the first version in a garage. It looked like it.")
- Hedging language (used intentionally). "Maybe," "I think," "It seems like."
- Longer, more conversational sentences.
- Acknowledging uncertainty openly.

### Status Matching (Peer Level)

> Use when speaking to equals: fellow founders, industry peers, potential partners. Status matching signals "I'm one of you."

- "You've seen this before. So have we."
- "We're solving the same problem from different angles."
- "You know how it goes."
- "Between us, the real challenge is..."
- "We both know the playbook. Let's skip to the interesting part."
- "I respect what you've built. Here's what we've built."
- "No need to posture. Let's talk about what's actually working."
- "We've made the same mistakes. Here's where our paths diverged."

### Status Shifting (Strategic Transitions)

> Use to move between status levels within a single piece of content. This is advanced: it creates dynamic range and keeps the audience engaged.

- **High to Low:** "We've generated $50M in revenue for our clients. [pause] But I still remember the night we almost shut down."
- **Low to High:** "I had no idea what I was doing when I started. Today, we serve 10,000 companies."
- **High to Peer:** "We've built the leading platform in this space. But honestly, you're the reason it works."
- **Low to Peer:** "I was the new kid in the room. Turns out, everyone else was figuring it out too."
- **Peer to High:** "We all see the problem. We happen to have the solution."

### Status Markers to Avoid

> These patterns undermine your intended status position. Watch for them in drafts.

- **Unintentional low status:** "I just wanted to follow up..." / "Sorry to bother you..." / "I'm no expert, but..."
- **Unintentional high status (arrogance):** "Obviously..." / "As everyone knows..." / "It's simple, really."
- **Status confusion (mixed signals):** "We're the industry leader, and we'd love your help figuring out what to do next." (Pick one.)
- **Over-hedging in a pitch:** "We think we might potentially be able to possibly help..." (This kills confidence.)

## Usage Guidelines

- **Context determines status.** A pitch deck demands high status. A community post benefits from low status. A partnership proposal needs peer status. There is no universally "correct" status.
- **Status is relative.** You are always higher or lower status relative to your audience. Match or intentionally contrast.
- **The most powerful communicators shift status fluidly.** A founder who can be commanding in a boardroom and vulnerable in a keynote has range. Range builds trust.
- **Read Johnstone's "Impro."** This file is a starting point. The full framework is in the source material.
- **Status is conveyed through body language too.** In written content, syntax and word choice carry the status signal. In video or speaking, posture, pace, and eye contact matter equally.
