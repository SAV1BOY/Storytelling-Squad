# Status Phrases

## Purpose
High-status and low-status phrases based on Keith Johnstone's status theory from *Impro*.
Status is not about power — it's about the behavioral signals that communicate relative
positioning in any interaction. Both high and low status have storytelling uses.

---

## High-Status Phrases
Signal confidence, control, and calm authority.

### Calm Authority
1. "Let me be direct."
2. "I'll tell you what I know."
3. "Here's what's going to happen."
4. "I've seen this before."
5. "The answer is simple."
6. "Allow me to reframe."
7. "I'll take that question."
8. "This is what I'd recommend."
9. "I'll make this easy."
10. "You have my word."

### Controlled Tempo
11. "There's no rush. Let me walk you through this."
12. "I want to make sure we're clear before we move on."
13. "Let me finish this thought."
14. "Good question. And the answer matters, so I want to get it right."
15. "I'll say this once, because it's the most important point."

### Generosity from a Position of Strength
16. "I want to give you something before we start."
17. "This insight isn't something we normally share. But I think it will help you."
18. "You're right about that. And here's what that means."
19. "That's a fair challenge. Here's how I'd answer it."
20. "I appreciate you pushing back. Let me respond."

### Comfortable Silence
21. [Pause before answering.] "..."
22. "Let me think about that for a moment." [Silence.]
23. "That's worth sitting with."
24. "I'd rather pause than give you a shallow answer."

## Low-Status Phrases
Signal humility, accessibility, openness, and often create likeability and trust.

### Self-Deprecation (Intentional)
25. "I'm probably the least qualified person in this room to say this, but..."
26. "My first attempt was terrible. Let me show you."
27. "I got this wrong three times before I got it right."
28. "If you'd seen me five years ago, you wouldn't have let me in the building."
29. "I'm not going to pretend I have all the answers."
30. "Honestly? I Googled it."

### Inclusive Uncertainty
31. "I might be wrong about this, but here's what I see."
32. "Help me understand."
33. "I'm thinking out loud here."
34. "Can I ask a dumb question?"
35. "I'm curious — what do you think?"
36. "I'm not the expert here. You are."

### Vulnerability Signals
37. "This is the part of the story I usually skip."
38. "I was afraid to share this."
39. "I'll tell you something I haven't told many people."
40. "This might be oversharing, but..."
41. "I don't have a clever way to say this."
42. "Here's the thing I keep getting wrong."

### Deference (Strategic)
43. "You know this space better than I do."
44. "I'd love your take on this before I continue."
45. "Teach me."
46. "I came here to learn, not to lecture."
47. "What am I missing?"
48. "I'm genuinely asking — not rhetorically."

## Status Dynamics in Storytelling

### The Status Drop
A character starts high and drops low. Creates empathy.
> "She walked into the conference room like she owned it. Six months later, she
> was cleaning out her desk at 7 a.m. to avoid seeing anyone."

### The Status Rise
A character starts low and rises. Creates inspiration.
> "He couldn't make eye contact during the interview. Five years later, he was
> interviewing the CEO who'd rejected him — for his podcast."

### The Status Inversion
Two characters swap positions. Creates dramatic irony.
> "The investor who said 'this will never work' ended up licensing the technology.
> The founder who was rejected set the licensing terms."

### The Status Mismatch
A character's external status contradicts their internal state.
> "She had the corner office, the title, and the respect of the board. But every
> Sunday night, she sat in her car in the garage for ten minutes before going
> inside, rehearsing how to sound like someone who had it together."

## Status in Different Contexts

| Context | Effective Status | Why |
|---------|-----------------|-----|
| Investor pitch | High (with moments of low) | Investors fund confidence; vulnerability shows self-awareness |
| Sales meeting | Matched (slightly above) | Too high alienates; too low undermines credibility |
| Keynote speech | High → Low → High | Open with authority, share vulnerability, close with confidence |
| Job interview | Moderate-high | Confident but not arrogant; eager but not desperate |
| Customer support | Low | The customer has the problem; you're here to serve |
| Team leadership | Flexible | High when directing, low when listening |
| Social media | Low-to-moderate | Vulnerability drives engagement; authority drives follows |

## Status Markers Beyond Language

### High-Status Behaviors
- Still posture, minimal fidgeting
- Slow, deliberate speech
- Comfortable silence (not rushing to fill gaps)
- Direct eye contact
- Economy of movement
- Taking up space (physically and temporally)

### Low-Status Behaviors
- Frequent nodding
- Quick to agree
- Filling silences
- Qualifying statements ("I'm no expert, but...")
- Touching face/hair (self-soothing)
- Apologizing before speaking ("Sorry, but...")

---

## Usage Notes
- Status is a tool, not an identity. The best communicators move fluidly between high and low.
- In storytelling, status changes create movement. A character at the same status level throughout is flat.
- The most trusted communicators pair high-status delivery with low-status content (confident presentation of vulnerable material).
