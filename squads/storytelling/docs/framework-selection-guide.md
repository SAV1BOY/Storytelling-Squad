# Framework Selection Guide

## Purpose

Help storytellers choose the right narrative framework for any project. This decision tree maps business objectives, audience types, and content formats to the most effective storytelling framework, eliminating guesswork and ensuring structural consistency across deliverables.

## When to Use This Guide

- Starting a new story project and deciding how to structure the narrative.
- Reviewing a draft that feels structurally weak and considering a framework switch.
- Advising a cross-squad partner on which framework fits their request.
- Onboarding a new squad member who needs to understand framework options.

## The Framework Library

### 1. Hero's Journey (Campbell/Vogler)
**Structure:** Ordinary world, call to adventure, refusal, mentor appears, crossing the threshold, tests and allies, ordeal, reward, return with the elixir.
**Best for:** Long-form brand stories, founder narratives, keynote presentations, content series.
**Audience:** Audiences who have time to invest in a full narrative arc (10+ minutes of reading or viewing).
**Strength:** Deep emotional engagement and transformation.
**Risk:** Too long and complex for short-form content.

### 2. StoryBrand (Donald Miller)
**Structure:** A character has a problem, meets a guide, who gives them a plan, calls them to action, that helps them avoid failure, and ends in success.
**Best for:** Website messaging, brand positioning, sales pages, email sequences.
**Audience:** Prospects in the consideration stage who need clarity about what you offer.
**Strength:** Customer-as-hero positioning; extremely clear and practical.
**Risk:** Can feel formulaic if not customized with specific details.

### 3. Problem-Agitation-Solution (PAS)
**Structure:** State the problem, agitate the emotional impact, present the solution.
**Best for:** Ad copy, landing pages, email subject lines, social media posts, short-form content.
**Audience:** Audiences with short attention spans or in the awareness stage.
**Strength:** Fast, punchy, drives immediate action.
**Risk:** Can feel manipulative if the agitation is overdone.

### 4. Before-After-Bridge (BAB)
**Structure:** Show the before state, paint the after state, reveal the bridge (your solution).
**Best for:** Case studies, testimonials, product demos, comparison content.
**Audience:** Prospects evaluating options; customers considering upgrades.
**Strength:** Concrete contrast makes the value proposition tangible.
**Risk:** Requires strong before/after data or examples; weak without specifics.

### 5. Sparkline / Contrast Pattern (Nancy Duarte)
**Structure:** Alternate between "what is" and "what could be," building tension toward a new vision.
**Best for:** Keynotes, TED-style talks, internal change management, vision presentations.
**Audience:** Audiences who need to be inspired to adopt a new idea or direction.
**Strength:** Creates powerful momentum toward a call to action.
**Risk:** Requires strong delivery; less effective in written-only format.

### 6. Pixar Story Spine
**Structure:** Once upon a time... Every day... But one day... Because of that... Until finally... And ever since then...
**Best for:** Quick story drafting, brainstorming sessions, social micro-stories, internal communication.
**Audience:** Broad; works across experience levels and contexts.
**Strength:** Simple enough to use in improv exercises and workshops; surprisingly versatile.
**Risk:** Can produce simplistic narratives if not developed beyond the spine.

### 7. Five Commandments (Shawn Coyne / Story Grid)
**Structure:** Inciting incident, progressive complication, crisis, climax, resolution.
**Best for:** Building individual scenes within larger narratives, pivotal moments, climactic sections.
**Audience:** Any audience; this is a scene-level framework, not a full-story framework.
**Strength:** Ensures every scene has real dramatic movement.
**Risk:** Not a standalone structure; must be nested within a larger framework.

### 8. STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result)
**Structure:** Describe the situation, define the task, explain the actions taken, share the result.
**Best for:** Case studies, project retrospectives, interview answers, internal reports.
**Audience:** Analytical audiences (executives, investors, technical buyers) who value clarity and outcomes.
**Strength:** Logical, easy to follow, results-oriented.
**Risk:** Can feel dry without emotional layering; add character and conflict to elevate.

### 9. Made to Stick (Heath Brothers — SUCCESs)
**Structure:** Ensure the message is Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, and contains Stories.
**Best for:** Optimizing any message for memorability and retellability.
**Audience:** Any audience; this is a diagnostic overlay, not a structural framework.
**Strength:** Actionable checklist applicable to every content type.
**Risk:** Tells you what to fix, not how to structure a full narrative; use as a complement.

### 10. Frame Control (Oren Klaff / Pitch Anything)
**Structure:** Set the frame, manage frame collisions, maintain prize frame, control status dynamics.
**Best for:** High-stakes pitches, sales conversations, investor meetings.
**Audience:** Audiences where power dynamics are in play (investors, executives, negotiation partners).
**Strength:** Directly addresses power dynamics most frameworks ignore.
**Risk:** Can feel aggressive if misapplied; requires social calibration.

## Decision Tree

Use this flowchart to select a framework:

```
START: What is the primary content length?

Short-form (under 300 words)?
  |-- Yes --> Is the goal to drive immediate action?
  |           |-- Yes --> PAS
  |           +-- No  --> Before-After-Bridge or Pixar Story Spine
  +-- No  --> Is the content a live presentation or speech?
              |-- Yes --> Is the goal to inspire change?
              |           |-- Yes --> Sparkline / Contrast Pattern
              |           +-- No  --> Hero's Journey (condensed) or StoryBrand
              +-- No  --> Is the content focused on one customer or one project?
                          |-- Yes --> Is the audience analytical?
                          |           |-- Yes --> STAR with emotional layering
                          |           +-- No  --> Before-After-Bridge or Hero's Journey
                          +-- No  --> Is the content brand-level messaging?
                                      |-- Yes --> StoryBrand
                                      +-- No  --> Hero's Journey or Sparkline
```

## Framework Pairing Guide

Some projects benefit from combining frameworks:

| Primary Framework | Secondary Framework | Use Case |
|---|---|---|
| StoryBrand | Five Commandments | Website messaging with a powerful hero scene |
| Hero's Journey | Sparkline | Keynote that tells a customer story as a vision piece |
| Before-After-Bridge | PAS | Case study with a punchy opening hook |
| STAR | Five Commandments | Executive report with one dramatic turning point |
| Pixar Story Spine | Before-After-Bridge | Social post expanded into a blog article |
| StoryBrand | Made to Stick | Brand messaging optimized for memorability |
| Sparkline | Frame Control | Investor pitch with vision and power dynamics |
| Hero's Journey | Story Grid | Long-form brand story with scene-level precision |

## Framework Selection by Output Type

| Output Type | Recommended Framework(s) |
|---|---|
| Website homepage | StoryBrand |
| Blog post | Before-After-Bridge, Hero's Journey |
| Case study | Before-After-Bridge, STAR |
| Email sequence | PAS (subject line), StoryBrand (body) |
| Social media post | PAS, Pixar Story Spine |
| Pitch deck | Sparkline, StoryBrand |
| Keynote speech | Sparkline, Hero's Journey |
| Brand manifesto | Hero's Journey |
| Product launch | Before-After-Bridge, Sparkline |
| Internal change communication | Sparkline, StoryBrand |
| Ad copy | PAS |
| Video script | Hero's Journey, Before-After-Bridge |
| Investor pitch | Sparkline, Frame Control |

## How to Validate Your Framework Choice

After selecting a framework, run these checks:

1. **Audience fit:** Does this framework match the audience's attention span and decision stage?
2. **Content fit:** Can the available material fill the framework's required elements?
3. **Channel fit:** Does this framework work in the intended distribution channel?
4. **Emotional fit:** Does the framework support the desired emotional arc?
5. **Proof fit:** Does the framework have natural insertion points for evidence?

If any check fails, reconsider or pair with a complementary framework.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Using Hero's Journey for short-form content.** It is too complex; default to PAS or BAB.
2. **Using PAS without genuine agitation.** If the problem is not painful, PAS falls flat.
3. **Using StoryBrand without a clear guide role.** If the brand is the hero instead of the guide, the framework breaks.
4. **Using STAR without emotional detail.** Pure STAR reads like a report; add character and conflict.
5. **Using Frame Control for written content.** Frame dynamics are interpersonal; use StoryBrand for written channels.
6. **Using Story Grid for a tweet.** Over-engineered; use Made to Stick instead.
7. **Using only one framework for everything.** Each has blind spots; combine to cover gaps.
8. **Not using any framework at all.** Unstructured narratives consistently score lower in quality gates.
