# Dialogue Component with Beat Actions

## Purpose
Dialogue is the most immediate form of narrative — it puts the audience in the room. In business storytelling, dialogue transforms abstract claims into vivid moments. This component provides the structure for writing dialogue that advances the story, reveals character, and creates memorability through beat actions.

---

## What Is a Beat Action?

A beat action is the physical, emotional, or psychological behavior that accompanies a line of dialogue. Dialogue tells the audience what was said; the beat action tells them what it meant. The gap between words and behavior is where subtext lives.

**Example without beat action:**
> "We're fine," the CEO said.

**Example with beat action:**
> "We're fine," the CEO said, closing her laptop before anyone could see the dashboard.

The beat action contradicts the words, creating subtext the audience decodes.

---

## Beat Action Categories

### 1. Confirming Actions
The action reinforces the dialogue. No subtext — what you see is what you get.
- "I believe in this team." She stood up and handed each person a copy of the revised plan.
- Use sparingly. Confirming actions are clear but lack depth.

### 2. Contradicting Actions
The action opposes the dialogue. Maximum subtext.
- "I'm not worried about the competition." He refreshed their pricing page for the third time that hour.
- Creates dramatic irony — the audience knows more than the words convey.

### 3. Amplifying Actions
The action escalates the emotion beyond what words can carry.
- "This changes everything." She stared at the screen, then slowly turned it to face the rest of the room.
- Used at climactic moments when words alone feel insufficient.

### 4. Deflecting Actions
The character avoids the emotional weight of the moment through action.
- "It was a tough quarter." He straightened the papers on his desk and asked if anyone wanted coffee.
- Reveals character through avoidance — what people don't address tells you everything.

### 5. Revealing Actions
The action accidentally exposes something the character intended to hide.
- "We've always been transparent with our investors." She glanced at the CFO, who looked away.
- Powerful for building tension and foreshadowing conflict.

## Dialogue Types for Business Narrative

### The Discovery Dialogue
Two characters uncover an insight through conversation. The audience discovers alongside them.

**Structure:**
- Character A states the problem
- Character B asks a question that reframes it
- Character A resists the reframe
- Character B provides evidence
- Character A's beat action reveals the shift before their words do

**Example:**
> "Our funnel is leaking at stage three," James said, pointing at the waterfall chart.
> "What happens at stage three?" asked Nina.
> "They get the pricing deck."
> "And what story does the pricing deck tell?"
> James opened his mouth, then closed it. He pulled up the deck and read it as if for the first time.
> "It doesn't tell a story," he said quietly. "It tells a spreadsheet."

### The Decision Dialogue
A character commits to a course of action in conversation. The audience witnesses the moment of choice.

**Structure:**
- External pressure is stated
- Character weighs options aloud (or in subtext)
- Another character or event forces the moment
- The character decides — and the beat action confirms the weight of the decision

### The Confrontation Dialogue
Two characters with opposing views collide. The audience sees the conflict externalized.

**Structure:**
- Positions are stated (or implied through beat actions)
- Each exchange escalates the stakes
- One character says something that can't be taken back
- The beat action after the final line tells the audience who won

## Dialogue Rules

### Rule 1: Cut the first exchange
Most dialogue scenes start one exchange too early. The first two lines are usually throat-clearing. Delete them and start where the tension begins.

### Rule 2: Each character must have a distinct voice
- Vary sentence length, vocabulary, and rhythm between characters
- A reader should be able to identify the speaker without attribution
- Base voice on status, expertise, emotional state, and background

### Rule 3: Never use dialogue for exposition
If a character is explaining something both characters already know, it's exposition disguised as dialogue. Use dialogue to reveal what characters feel, not what they know.

### Rule 4: Beat actions replace adverbs
- Never write: "she said angrily"
- Instead write: "she set down her pen with deliberate precision"
- The action shows the emotion; the adverb merely labels it

### Rule 5: Silence is dialogue
A character choosing not to respond is often the most powerful beat. Use it.
> "Are you going to fight for this account or not?"
> Sarah looked out the window for a long time.
> "I'll have the revised proposal by Friday," she finally said — answering a question he hadn't asked.

## Dialogue in Business Formats

| Format | Dialogue Length | Beat Action Density |
|--------|----------------|---------------------|
| Case study | 1-2 quoted exchanges | 1 beat action per exchange |
| Keynote story | 3-5 exchanges | Heavy beat actions for stage performance |
| Brand video | 2-4 exchanges | Actions are visual — shown, not described |
| Blog narrative | 1-3 exchanges | Light — focus on the key revealing moment |
| Pitch anecdote | 1-2 exchanges | Single powerful beat action |

---

## Common Dialogue Failures

1. **On-the-nose dialogue:** Characters say exactly what they mean with no subtext
2. **Identical voices:** All characters sound like the same person (usually the writer)
3. **Orphaned dialogue:** Lines with no beat actions — talking heads floating in space
4. **Overwritten attribution:** "he exclaimed enthusiastically" — trust the dialogue and action
5. **Unrealistic perfection:** Real people stumble, interrupt, and trail off
