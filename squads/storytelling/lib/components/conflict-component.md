# Conflict Component (Stakes + Obstacle)

## Purpose
Conflict is the engine of story. Without conflict, a narrative is merely a sequence of events. This component provides the structure for designing conflict that creates tension, sustains attention, and makes resolution satisfying. Conflict is not optional decoration — it is the mechanism by which audiences become invested.

---

## Conflict Anatomy

### The Want
What the character is actively pursuing. Must be concrete, specific, and visible.
- Weak: "She wanted success."
- Strong: "She wanted to close the Series A before the runway hit zero in 47 days."

### The Obstacle
What stands between the character and the want. Must be formidable and specific.
- Weak: "Things were hard."
- Strong: "Every investor said the same thing: 'Love the product, don't trust the market.'"

### The Stakes
What happens if the character fails. Must be felt, not just understood.
- Weak: "It wouldn't be good."
- Strong: "Twelve employees who left stable jobs to join her would need to update their resumes."

---

## Conflict Intensity Scale

### Level 1: Friction
- Minor obstacle, low stakes, quick resolution
- Use for: Subplots, transitions, light content
- Example: "The deck wasn't ready. She had two hours to rebuild it."

### Level 2: Struggle
- Meaningful obstacle, moderate stakes, requires effort
- Use for: Case study midpoints, blog narratives, social content
- Example: "Three pilots failed. The board wanted to kill the project."

### Level 3: Crisis
- Severe obstacle, high stakes, outcome uncertain
- Use for: Keynote climaxes, founder stories, brand origin moments
- Example: "The biggest client threatened to leave. If they did, payroll would bounce."

### Level 4: Existential Threat
- Obstacle threatens the character's identity, mission, or survival
- Use for: Brand purpose stories, transformation narratives, crisis stories
- Example: "The lawsuit didn't just threaten the company — it questioned whether the founder's entire thesis was a lie."

---

## Conflict Design Framework

### Step 1: Establish the want clearly
- State what the character wants in one sentence
- Make it tangible: a number, a deadline, a specific outcome
- The audience must be able to picture what success looks like

### Step 2: Introduce the obstacle progressively
- First obstacle: external and concrete (market, competitor, technical failure)
- Second obstacle: interpersonal (team disagreement, stakeholder resistance)
- Third obstacle: internal (self-doubt, conflicting values, fear)
- Layered obstacles create deepening engagement

### Step 3: Raise the stakes at each turn
- Each new obstacle should increase what's at risk
- Stakes should expand from personal to communal
- The audience should feel the weight accumulating

### Step 4: Create the dilemma
- The best conflicts force a choice between two valued things
- "She could save the deal or keep her integrity — but not both."
- Dilemmas are more powerful than simple obstacles

## Conflict Types for Business Narrative

### External Conflicts
- **Market vs. Company:** Shifting market rejects the current approach
- **Competitor vs. Company:** Direct competitive threat
- **Customer vs. Company:** Customer demands challenge the company's model
- **Regulation vs. Company:** New rules threaten operations

### Internal Conflicts
- **Founder vs. Self:** Imposter syndrome, burnout, conflicting identities
- **Vision vs. Reality:** The plan meets the messy real world
- **Growth vs. Values:** Scaling threatens the culture or mission
- **Speed vs. Quality:** Pressure to ship vs. commitment to craft

### Interpersonal Conflicts
- **Co-founder disagreements:** Different visions for the future
- **Team resistance:** The team doesn't believe in the new direction
- **Investor pressure:** Board pushes for growth that conflicts with strategy
- **Customer expectations:** What customers want vs. what they need

## Conflict Resolution Patterns

### 1. The Breakthrough
- Character finds a creative solution that resolves the conflict on new terms
- Neither surrender nor brute force — innovation

### 2. The Sacrifice
- Character gives up something valuable to resolve the conflict
- Creates emotional weight and demonstrates commitment

### 3. The Alliance
- Character cannot resolve the conflict alone — finds an ally
- Useful for positioning the brand as the guide

### 4. The Reframe
- Character realizes the conflict was based on a false assumption
- Resolution comes through changed perspective, not changed circumstances

---

## Common Mistakes

1. **Conflict without stakes:** The obstacle exists but nothing important hangs in the balance
2. **Resolved too quickly:** Conflict introduced and solved in the same paragraph — no tension built
3. **Artificial conflict:** The obstacle feels manufactured rather than organic to the situation
4. **Conflict avoidance:** Glossing over the hard parts to keep the story "positive"
5. **Villain without nuance:** The obstacle is a cartoon bad guy instead of a complex force
