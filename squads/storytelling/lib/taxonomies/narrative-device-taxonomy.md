# Narrative Device Taxonomy

## Purpose
Narrative devices are the tools storytellers use to manipulate time, perspective, meaning, and emphasis within a story. This taxonomy catalogs the primary devices available, with definitions, examples, and application guidance for business storytelling.

---

## Temporal Devices

### Flashback (Analepsis)
**Definition:** The narrative jumps backward in time to show a past event that contextualizes the present.
**Business use:** Founder origin moments, "how we got here" context, customer backstory
**Example:** "Three years before the IPO, in a garage that smelled like burnt coffee, the first line of code was written on a borrowed laptop."
**Rule:** Enter and exit flashbacks with clear temporal signals. Never strand the audience in time.

### Flash-forward (Prolepsis)
**Definition:** The narrative jumps ahead to show a future state before returning to the present.
**Business use:** Vision casting, "imagine the outcome" sections, pitch deck future-state slides
**Example:** "Fast forward six months. The dashboard that once showed red across every metric is now green. But getting here cost them more than they expected."
**Rule:** Flash-forwards create anticipation but can reduce tension. Use sparingly.

### In Medias Res
**Definition:** Starting the narrative in the middle of the action, then filling in context later.
**Business use:** Hooks for any format — pitches, case studies, keynotes, emails
**Example:** "The room was silent. Twelve investors. Zero questions. She wasn't sure if that was very good or very bad."
**Rule:** The opening moment must be genuinely dramatic. Mild moments in medias res feel pretentious.

### Foreshadowing
**Definition:** Planting hints about future events that gain meaning in retrospect.
**Business use:** Subtle setup for turning points, building subconscious anticipation
**Example:** "The team celebrated their best month ever. Nobody noticed the churn report sitting unopened in the shared drive."
**Rule:** Foreshadowing must be recognizable on re-read but not obvious on first read.

### Montage
**Definition:** A compressed sequence of events that shows passage of time or accumulated change.
**Business use:** Implementation timelines, growth sequences, transformation progress
**Example:** "Week one: confusion. Week four: small wins. Week eight: the first unsolicited customer referral. Week twelve: a waitlist."

## Perspective Devices

### Unreliable Narrator
**Definition:** The narrator's account is biased, incomplete, or intentionally misleading — and the audience must discern the truth.
**Business use:** Self-aware brand content, lesson-learned stories, contrasting perspectives
**Example:** "We told ourselves the product was perfect. The customer told us something different."
**Rule:** In business narrative, unreliability must be eventually corrected. Leaving the audience misled damages trust.

### Point-of-View Shift
**Definition:** The narrative switches from one character's perspective to another's.
**Business use:** Showing the same event from the customer's view and the company's view
**Example:** First perspective — the sales rep who thought the deal was going well. Second perspective — the buyer who was already talking to competitors.

### Direct Address
**Definition:** The narrator speaks directly to the audience, breaking the fourth wall.
**Business use:** CTAs, audience challenges, empathy-building moments
**Example:** "You've heard this story before. You've lived it. The question is whether you'll do something about it this time."

## Meaning Devices

### Metaphor
**Definition:** Describing one thing in terms of another to transfer meaning and create resonance.
**Business use:** Explaining complex concepts, making abstract ideas tangible
**Example:** "Your brand story is the operating system. Everything else — campaigns, content, pitches — is an application running on it."
**Rule:** One controlling metaphor per narrative. Mixed metaphors create confusion.

### Irony
**Types:**
- **Dramatic irony:** The audience knows something the character doesn't
- **Situational irony:** The outcome is the opposite of what was expected
- **Verbal irony:** Words mean the opposite of their literal meaning

**Business use:** Cautionary tales, twist reveals, self-deprecating brand content
**Example (situational):** "They hired a branding agency to differentiate them from competitors. The agency gave them the same brand framework they'd given to three other clients in the same industry."

### Juxtaposition
**Definition:** Placing two contrasting elements side by side to highlight their differences.
**Business use:** Before/after comparisons, competitive positioning, contrast-driven decks
**Example:** "Monday: 47 spreadsheets, 3 meetings about meetings, zero customer conversations. Tuesday: one narrative framework, one team alignment, four customer calls."

### Symbolism
**Definition:** An object, person, or event that represents something beyond its literal meaning.
**Business use:** Brand identity elements, recurring motifs in content, visual storytelling
**Example:** "The original napkin sketch hangs in every conference room. It's not decoration — it's a reminder that every billion-dollar company starts as a scribble."

---

## Structural Devices

### Repetition (Anaphora)
**Definition:** Repeating a word or phrase for emphasis and rhythm.
**Business use:** Keynote speeches, brand manifestos, social media content
**Example:** "We believe stories change minds. We believe stories change markets. We believe stories change the world."

### Parallel Structure
**Definition:** Using the same grammatical structure for related elements, creating rhythm and coherence.
**Business use:** Value propositions, comparison content, persuasive writing
**Example:** "Old way: guess what customers want. New way: hear what customers say. Our way: understand what customers mean."

### Chiasmus
**Definition:** A rhetorical figure in which the second half reverses the order of the first.
**Business use:** Memorable one-liners, keynote punchlines, brand slogans
**Example:** "Don't tell a story to make a sale. Make a sale by telling a story."

---

## Device Density Guidelines

| Format | Devices per piece | Best choices |
|--------|-------------------|--------------|
| Social post | 1 | Metaphor, irony, or repetition |
| Blog post | 2-3 | In medias res, juxtaposition, metaphor |
| Case study | 2-3 | Flashback, before/after, montage |
| Keynote | 4-6 | Full range, varied across sections |
| Pitch deck | 2-3 | In medias res, foreshadowing, juxtaposition |
