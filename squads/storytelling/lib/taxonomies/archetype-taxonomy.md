# Campbell's 12 Archetypes Catalog

## Purpose
Joseph Campbell's archetypal framework provides universal character patterns that audiences recognize instinctively. In business storytelling, archetypes help brands, founders, and customers embody recognizable roles that create immediate emotional resonance. This catalog defines each archetype and maps it to business narrative applications.

---

## The 12 Archetypes

### 1. The Hero
**Core desire:** Prove worth through courageous action
**Core fear:** Weakness, vulnerability, being a quitter
**Strategy:** Become as strong and competent as possible
**Brand examples:** Nike, FedEx, the U.S. Army
**Business narrative use:** Customer transformation stories where the customer overcomes significant obstacles. The customer is the hero — not the brand.
**Shadow form:** The bully who uses strength to dominate rather than protect

### 2. The Sage
**Core desire:** Find truth, understand the world
**Core fear:** Ignorance, being misled, being wrong
**Strategy:** Seek knowledge and share wisdom
**Brand examples:** Google, Harvard, McKinsey
**Business narrative use:** Thought leadership content, educational series, research-driven stories. The brand as teacher and analyst.
**Shadow form:** The ivory tower — knowledge without practical application

### 3. The Explorer
**Core desire:** Freedom to discover and experience
**Core fear:** Conformity, being trapped, inner emptiness
**Strategy:** Journey, seek new experiences, avoid boredom
**Brand examples:** Patagonia, Jeep, National Geographic
**Business narrative use:** Innovation stories, market expansion narratives, product discovery journeys. Stories about finding new territory.
**Shadow form:** The wanderer who never commits, always seeking the next thing

### 4. The Outlaw (Rebel)
**Core desire:** Revolution, radical change, breaking the rules
**Core fear:** Being powerless, being co-opted by the system
**Strategy:** Disrupt, destroy what isn't working, shock
**Brand examples:** Harley-Davidson, Virgin, Diesel
**Business narrative use:** Challenger brand positioning, industry disruption stories, "we broke the mold" narratives.
**Shadow form:** The criminal — destruction without purpose

### 5. The Magician
**Core desire:** Transform reality, make dreams come true
**Core fear:** Unintended negative consequences
**Strategy:** Develop a vision and live it
**Brand examples:** Apple, Disney, Dyson
**Business narrative use:** Product launch narratives, innovation stories, "we made the impossible possible" content.
**Shadow form:** The manipulator who transforms reality through deception

### 6. The Everyperson
**Core desire:** Connection, belonging, fitting in
**Core fear:** Standing out, being excluded, pretentiousness
**Strategy:** Develop ordinary solid virtues, be the common touch
**Brand examples:** IKEA, Target, Budweiser
**Business narrative use:** Community stories, accessibility narratives, "built for real people" positioning.
**Shadow form:** The victim who uses ordinariness as an excuse for mediocrity

### 7. The Lover
**Core desire:** Intimacy, experience, sensual pleasure
**Core fear:** Being alone, unwanted, unloved
**Strategy:** Become increasingly attractive and emotionally connected
**Brand examples:** Chanel, Godiva, Victoria's Secret
**Business narrative use:** Brand affinity content, premium experience stories, relationship-focused customer narratives.
**Shadow form:** The obsessive who loses identity in the pursuit of connection

### 8. The Jester
**Core desire:** To live in the moment with full enjoyment
**Core fear:** Being boring, boredom itself
**Strategy:** Play, make jokes, be funny
**Brand examples:** Ben & Jerry's, Old Spice, Dollar Shave Club
**Business narrative use:** Viral content, brand personality development, disarming narratives that use humor to deliver serious points.
**Shadow form:** The cynic who uses humor to avoid genuine engagement

### 9. The Caregiver
**Core desire:** Protect and care for others
**Core fear:** Selfishness, ingratitude
**Strategy:** Do things for others, nurture, protect
**Brand examples:** Johnson & Johnson, TOMS, Volvo
**Business narrative use:** Customer care stories, social impact narratives, "we go beyond the product" content.
**Shadow form:** The martyr who sacrifices to the point of self-destruction

### 10. The Creator
**Core desire:** Create something of enduring value
**Core fear:** Mediocrity, having a pedestrian vision
**Strategy:** Develop artistic control and skill
**Brand examples:** Adobe, LEGO, Crayola
**Business narrative use:** Behind-the-scenes creation stories, craft narratives, "how we built it" content.
**Shadow form:** The perfectionist who never ships

### 11. The Ruler
**Core desire:** Control, create order from chaos
**Core fear:** Chaos, being overthrown
**Strategy:** Exercise power, create structure, set standards
**Brand examples:** Mercedes-Benz, Rolex, Microsoft
**Business narrative use:** Industry leadership narratives, standard-setting stories, governance and trust content.
**Shadow form:** The tyrant who controls through fear

### 12. The Innocent
**Core desire:** To be happy, to experience paradise
**Core fear:** Doing something wrong, punishment
**Strategy:** Do things right, be optimistic, have faith
**Brand examples:** Coca-Cola, Dove, Whole Foods
**Business narrative use:** Origin purity stories, brand promise narratives, simplicity-focused content.
**Shadow form:** The naive who denies reality to maintain the illusion

---

## Archetype Selection for Brands

A brand should select one primary and one secondary archetype:
- **Primary:** The archetype that defines the brand's core identity (unchanging)
- **Secondary:** The archetype that adds dimension and prevents the primary from becoming a cliche

Example: Nike = Hero (primary) + Explorer (secondary)

---

## Archetype Combinations to Avoid
- Hero + Ruler: Comes across as authoritarian
- Jester + Sage: Contradictory — undermines credibility
- Rebel + Innocent: Dissonant — audience doesn't know what to expect
- Lover + Everyperson: Dilutes both — premium vs. accessible conflict
