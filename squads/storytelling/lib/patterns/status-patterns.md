# Johnstone Status Dynamics Patterns

## Purpose
Keith Johnstone's status theory (from Impro) reveals that every human interaction involves a continuous negotiation of relative status. Status is not rank or authority — it is a behavioral signal that can be raised or lowered intentionally. In storytelling, status dynamics create tension, reveal character, and make scenes feel alive. This file codifies status patterns for narrative use.

---

## Status Fundamentals

### What Status Is
- Status is **behavior**, not position. A CEO can play low status; an intern can play high.
- Status is **relative** — it exists only in comparison to another character.
- Status is **fluid** — it shifts moment to moment within a scene.
- Status is **communicated physically** — before words, through posture, eye contact, pace, and space.

### High-Status Signals
- Still body, minimal unnecessary movement
- Sustained eye contact during speaking; breaks eye contact at will during listening
- Slow, deliberate speech with pauses
- Takes up physical space
- Comfortable with silence
- Makes definitive statements rather than asking questions

### Low-Status Signals
- Fidgeting, self-touching (hair, face, neck)
- Breaks eye contact first; looks away while speaking
- Fast speech, filling silences, nervous laughter
- Minimizes physical footprint
- Asks permission, hedges, qualifies statements
- Apologizes unnecessarily

## Pattern 1: The Status Shift (Reversal)

### Definition
A character's status flips from high to low (or low to high) within a single scene.

### Structure
1. Establish character at one status level
2. Introduce an event, information, or character that disrupts the status
3. Status shifts — the character who was high is now low, or vice versa

### Example
- High-status investor controls the meeting, interrupting, checking phone
- Founder reveals a metric that the investor didn't know about — a competitor the investor backed is losing market share to this founder's company
- Status shifts: The investor puts down the phone. The founder now controls the room.

### Narrative Function
Status shifts are among the most satisfying moments in any story. The audience feels the power dynamic change viscerally.

---

## Pattern 2: The Status Gap

### Definition
Two characters maintain a persistent status difference throughout a scene, creating tension through the gap itself.

### Structure
1. Establish a clear status differential
2. The low-status character wants something from the high-status character
3. Every interaction reinforces the gap
4. Resolution: the gap either closes (satisfaction) or widens (tragedy)

### Example
- A startup founder (low status) pitches a legendary investor (high status)
- Every question from the investor widens the gap
- The founder's narrative gradually earns respect — the gap closes word by word
- Final moment: The investor says, "Tell me more." Status gap closed.

## Pattern 3: The Status Match

### Definition
Two characters of equal status negotiate for dominance. Neither concedes.

### Structure
1. Both characters signal high status
2. Each exchange is a move to claim slightly higher status
3. Tension escalates as neither yields
4. Resolution requires one of three outcomes: one yields, both find mutual respect, or the conflict destroys both

### Narrative Function
Status matches create some of the most riveting dialogue scenes. Used for co-founder conflicts, negotiation narratives, competitive standoffs.

---

## Pattern 4: Intentional Status Lowering

### Definition
A character deliberately lowers their own status for strategic or emotional effect.

### Common Uses
- **Vulnerability play:** A leader lowers status to create psychological safety
- **Connection building:** Lowering status to match the audience creates rapport
- **Disarming:** Entering a hostile room at low status defuses aggression

### Example
- "I'm going to be honest — I almost didn't come today. I've given this pitch eleven times and failed eleven times. But I think the twelfth time might be different, and here's why."
- The speaker lowers their own status through confession, which paradoxically raises their credibility and likability.

### Rules
- Intentional lowering must feel genuine, not performative
- Lower status temporarily, then earn your way back up through substance
- Never lower status so far that the audience loses confidence in your competence

---

## Pattern 5: The Status Inversion (Underdog)

### Definition
A narrative where the expected status hierarchy is inverted by the end.

### Structure
1. Establish the expected hierarchy (powerful entity vs. underdog)
2. The underdog operates with apparent low status
3. Through skill, insight, or persistence, the underdog rises
4. By the climax, the status has fully inverted

### Why It Works
Underdog stories are universally compelling because every audience member has experienced feeling low-status. The inversion provides vicarious satisfaction.

## Status in Business Storytelling

### Brand as Guide (Medium-High Status)
- The brand should occupy a status position slightly above the customer — expert but accessible
- Too high: the brand seems arrogant ("We know everything")
- Too low: the brand seems uncertain ("We hope this helps")
- Right level: confident competence with warmth ("We've seen this before. Here's the path.")

### Customer as Hero (Rising Status)
- Customer starts at low status (struggling, confused, behind)
- Through the brand's guidance, their status rises
- End state: Customer at high status, brand at supporting guide status

### Founder as Vulnerable Leader (Strategic Low-to-High)
- Open with vulnerability (low status) — creates empathy
- Build through struggle and insight — status rises with competence
- Close with earned authority (high status) — the journey justifies the position

## Status Calibration by Audience

| Audience | Your Opening Status | Why |
|----------|-------------------|-----|
| C-suite executives | Medium-high (peer, not supplicant) | Entering low triggers dismissal |
| Practitioners | Medium (fellow traveler) | Too high triggers resistance |
| Students or early-career | Medium-high (mentor, not lecturer) | Authority balanced with accessibility |
| Skeptical audience | Low, then rising (earn it) | High status entry triggers pushback |
