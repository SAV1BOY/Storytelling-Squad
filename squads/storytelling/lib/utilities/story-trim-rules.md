# Story Trim Rules

## Purpose
Every story has fat. Trim rules provide a systematic process for cutting and compressing narrative without losing meaning. A story that's 30% shorter and 50% sharper will always outperform the longer version. These rules are not about brevity for its own sake — they are about eliminating everything that doesn't serve the audience's experience.

---

## The Prime Directive
**If removing a sentence doesn't change the meaning or impact, remove it.**

---

## Rule 1: Kill the Throat-Clear

### What It Is
The first 1-3 sentences of any draft are almost always throat-clearing — the writer warming up before they say something meaningful.

### How to Apply
- Read the piece. Find the first sentence that creates energy, tension, or curiosity.
- Delete everything before it.
- In most cases, your real opening is buried in paragraph two or three.

### Example
- Before: "Storytelling has been an important part of human culture for thousands of years. In the business world, we're just beginning to understand its power. Let me share a story that illustrates this perfectly. Last March, our biggest client called to fire us."
- After: "Last March, our biggest client called to fire us."

---

## Rule 2: Collapse Redundant Beats

### What It Is
Two or more narrative beats that make the same point. The writer included both because they weren't sure which was stronger.

### How to Apply
- Identify beats that serve the same narrative function
- Choose the stronger one (more specific, more emotional, more vivid)
- Delete the other. If in doubt, delete the longer one.

### Example
- Before: "The team was exhausted. Morale was at an all-time low. People were burning out left and right. Nobody wanted to come to work anymore."
- After: "Nobody wanted to come to work anymore."

---

## Rule 3: Replace Adjectives with Evidence

### What It Is
Adjectives tell the audience what to think. Evidence lets them think it themselves. Evidence is always more powerful and usually more concise.

### How to Apply
- Search for adjectives: amazing, incredible, revolutionary, powerful, groundbreaking
- For each, ask: "What happened that made this true?"
- Replace the adjective with the specific event, number, or action

### Example
- Before: "The results were absolutely incredible and truly transformative."
- After: "Churn dropped from 18% to 3% in a single quarter."

---

## Rule 4: Cut Hedge Words

### What It Is
Hedge words signal the writer's lack of confidence. They weaken every sentence they appear in.

### The Hit List
- "Kind of," "sort of," "a little bit," "somewhat"
- "I think," "I believe," "I feel like" (when not genuinely about feelings)
- "Maybe," "perhaps," "possibly," "potentially"
- "Basically," "essentially," "fundamentally" (when used as filler)
- "Very," "really," "quite," "rather"
- "In order to" (replace with "to")
- "The fact that" (delete entirely; rephrase)

### How to Apply
Find and delete. If the sentence breaks without the hedge word, rewrite the sentence from scratch.

---

## Rule 5: One Idea Per Sentence

### What It Is
Compound sentences that carry two or three ideas dilute all of them. Split or choose.

### How to Apply
- If a sentence has "and" connecting two independent ideas, split into two sentences
- If both ideas say the same thing, keep the stronger one
- If the sentence has more than one comma-separated clause, consider restructuring

---

## Rule 6: Cut the Setup Before the Setup

### What It Is
Context that prepares the audience for the context. Double setup is the most common source of narrative bloat.

### Example
- Before: "Before I get into the details, I want to give you some background on how we got here. A few years ago, the market was changing rapidly, and we realized that we needed to adapt. So we started looking at different approaches, and that's when we discovered something interesting."
- After: "Three years ago, we discovered something that changed our approach entirely."

---

## Rule 7: Trim Dialogue to Its Core

### What It Is
Reported dialogue in business narrative is often too long. Real conversations are messy; narrative dialogue should be distilled.

### How to Apply
- Keep only the lines that advance the story or reveal character
- Remove pleasantries, transitions, and anything the audience can infer
- If a character's key point takes 4 lines, compress to 1-2

---

## Rule 8: Delete Transitions Between Strong Beats

### What It Is
If two consecutive beats are both strong, the transition between them is often unnecessary. The reader's brain bridges the gap.

### Example
- Before: "The pitch failed. As a result, the team went back to the drawing board. They spent weeks rethinking their approach. Eventually, they emerged with a completely different strategy."
- After: "The pitch failed. Six weeks later, they emerged with a completely different strategy."

---

## Rule 9: The 10% Rule

### What It Is
After your first full edit, cut an additional 10% of the remaining word count. This forces you past your comfort zone and into genuine concision.

### How to Apply
- Count total words after the first trim pass
- Calculate 10% of that number
- Find and remove that many words through any combination of the above rules
- This pass almost always improves the piece

---

## Trim Targets by Format

| Format | Target Word Reduction | Priority Rules |
|--------|----------------------|----------------|
| Social post | 30-40% from draft | Rules 1, 4, 5 |
| Email | 25-35% from draft | Rules 1, 2, 6 |
| Blog post | 20-30% from draft | Rules 2, 3, 5 |
| Case study | 15-25% from draft | Rules 3, 7, 8 |
| Keynote script | 20-30% from draft | Rules 1, 2, 7 |
| Pitch deck | 40-50% from draft | Rules 1, 3, 4 |

---

## The Preservation List

Never trim:
- **Specific details** that create vivid imagery (names, numbers, dates, places)
- **Emotional beats** that create audience connection
- **The single best proof point** in any section
- **Dialogue that reveals character** (not information)
- **Silence and pauses** in spoken narrative — these are features, not bugs
