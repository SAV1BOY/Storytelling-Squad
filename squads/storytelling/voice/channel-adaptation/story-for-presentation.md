# Story Adaptation: Presentations

## Purpose
Adapt narrative storytelling for live presentations — conference keynotes, sales
meetings, board presentations, and internal all-hands. The spoken word has different
rules than the written word.

## The Fundamental Difference
Written stories are consumed privately, at the reader's pace. Presented stories are
performed publicly, at the speaker's pace. This means:
- You control timing (pauses, acceleration, silence).
- You cannot be re-read. Clarity must be instant.
- Emotion is amplified by physical presence.
- Slides support the story; they do not contain it.

## The Duarte Sparkline Structure
Nancy Duarte's research on great presentations revealed a consistent pattern:
alternation between "what is" (current reality) and "what could be" (desired future).

```
Audience Energy
^
|    /\    /\    /\    /\
|   /  \  /  \  /  \  /  \___ (New Bliss)
|  /    \/    \/    \/
| /
|/ (What Is)
+---------------------------------> Time
```

Each upswing is a "what could be" moment. Each downswing returns to "what is."
The presentation ends on a sustained high — the "new bliss."

**In practice:**
1. Open with "what is" — the current state, the problem, the status quo.
2. Contrast with "what could be" — the vision, the possibility, the better way.
3. Return to "what is" — the obstacles, the cost of inaction.
4. Rise again to "what could be" — the plan, the evidence, the path.
5. Close with "new bliss" — the world after the audience acts.

## Slide Design for Story

### The One-Idea Slide Rule
Each slide should contain exactly one idea. If you need to explain the slide, it has
too much on it.

### Slide Types for Storytelling

| Slide Type | Purpose | Design |
|-----------|---------|--------|
| **Scene-setter** | Ground the audience in time/place | Full-bleed photo, minimal text |
| **Data reveal** | Present a surprising finding | Single number, large font, black background |
| **Quote** | Let a character speak | Quote text + attribution, no other elements |
| **Contrast** | Show before/after | Split screen or sequential reveal |
| **Blank/black** | Create silence, focus attention on speaker | Completely black slide |
| **Transition** | Signal a new act | Single word or phrase, centered |

### The Blank Slide Technique
The most powerful slide in any presentation is a blank screen. It forces the audience
to look at you. Use it:
- Before the most important point in your talk.
- When telling the emotional peak of a story.
- After a provocative question.
- During audience interaction.

## Delivery Techniques for Storytelling

### Pacing
- **Slow down for the emotional beats.** The audience needs time to feel.
- **Speed up for action sequences.** Acceleration creates excitement.
- **Pause after key lines.** Count to three in your head. It will feel like an eternity. It will look like confidence.

### Voice Modulation
- **Lower your pitch for authority.** Deep voice = gravitas.
- **Raise your pitch for wonder.** Slightly higher = discovery, surprise.
- **Drop to near-whisper for intimacy.** The room leans in.
- **Full volume for declarations.** Save it for the single most important line.

### Physical Storytelling
- **Move to a different spot on stage for each story character.** The audience will track characters by position.
- **Stand still for the climax.** Movement during emotional peaks dissipates energy.
- **Make eye contact with one person per sentence.** It feels like you're talking to everyone.
- **Use your hands to show scale, direction, and contrast.** Open palms = honesty. Pointed finger = direction. Hands apart = scale.

## Presentation Story Structure

### For a 20-Minute Keynote
- **0–3 min:** Opening story (personal, specific, grounding).
- **3–7 min:** "What is" — the problem in the world/industry.
- **7–12 min:** "What could be" — the insight, framework, or solution.
- **12–16 min:** Evidence (data + customer stories + demonstrations).
- **16–18 min:** "What if" — the vision of the world after adoption.
- **18–20 min:** Closing story (callback to opening, transformed by the journey).

### For a 45-Minute Sales Presentation
- **0–5 min:** Rapport story (shared experience with the prospect).
- **5–15 min:** Problem deep-dive (using the prospect's data and language).
- **15–25 min:** Solution as narrative (how it works, told through customer journey).
- **25–35 min:** Proof (case studies, data, live demo as story).
- **35–40 min:** Objection handling through stories ("Another client asked the same thing…").
- **40–45 min:** Close with vision + specific next step.

## Timing Guidelines

| Element | Time Allocation |
|---------|----------------|
| A single story | 2–5 minutes max |
| A data point + context | 30–60 seconds |
| A quote from a customer | 15–30 seconds |
| A demonstration | 3–7 minutes |
| Audience interaction | 2–5 minutes |
| Transitions between sections | 15–30 seconds |

## Common Mistakes
1. **Reading slides aloud.** The audience can read. You are there to add what slides cannot: emotion, context, emphasis, and story.
2. **Too many stories.** Three stories in a 20-minute talk is the maximum. One great story beats three good ones.
3. **No rehearsal.** Rehearse the story sections out loud at least five times. Timing, pauses, and transitions need muscle memory.
4. **Ending with Q&A.** End with your closing story, then take Q&A, then close again with one final line. Control the last thing they hear.
