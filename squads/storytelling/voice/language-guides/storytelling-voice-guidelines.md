# Storytelling Voice Guidelines

## Purpose
This document defines the baseline voice principles for all storytelling content
produced by the squad. Individual tone profiles may override specific settings,
but these guidelines are the foundation.

## Tone Foundation

### Default Register
Conversational-professional. Write the way a smart colleague speaks at a whiteboard:
clear, direct, occasionally witty, never stuffy.

### Rhythm
Vary sentence length deliberately. Follow a long sentence with a short one. Three
medium sentences, then a fragment. Then a long one again. Monotonous rhythm is the
fastest way to lose a reader.

**Pattern to follow:**
> Long sentence that sets the scene and provides context for what you're about to say,
> pulling the reader forward with a rolling clause. Short punch. Then a medium sentence
> that explains the point. And a fragment for emphasis.

### Lexicon Rules
1. **Prefer concrete nouns over abstract ones.** "Revenue" over "success." "Tuesday" over "recently." "Three customers" over "some users."
2. **Prefer active verbs over passive constructions.** "The team rebuilt the page" over "the page was rebuilt by the team."
3. **Prefer short words over long synonyms.** "Use" over "utilize." "Help" over "facilitate." "Show" over "demonstrate."
4. **Eliminate filler phrases.** Cut "in order to" (use "to"), "due to the fact that" (use "because"), "at the end of the day" (delete entirely).
5. **Avoid adverb crutches.** If the verb needs an adverb to carry meaning, find a stronger verb. "Sprinted" not "ran quickly." "Whispered" not "said softly."

## Cadence Guidelines

### Opening Cadence
Start with energy. The first sentence should earn the second sentence. Techniques:
- Lead with a surprising fact or counterintuitive claim.
- Open in the middle of a scene (in medias res).
- Ask a question that the reader genuinely wants answered.
- State the stakes plainly: "This mistake costs companies $X/year."

### Middle Cadence
Maintain momentum through variation. Every 3–4 paragraphs, introduce:
- A new perspective or character.
- A data point or concrete example.
- A shift in sentence structure (dialogue, list, question).
- A transitional phrase that creates a micro-hook.

### Closing Cadence
End with resonance, not summary. Techniques:
- Return to the opening image with new meaning.
- End on a single, vivid image.
- Pose a forward-looking question.
- Close with a one-sentence call to action.

## Person and Perspective

| Context | Recommended Perspective |
|---------|----------------------|
| How-to content | Second person ("you") |
| Case studies | Third person (client as protagonist) |
| Thought leadership | First person plural ("we") or first singular ("I") |
| Brand storytelling | Third person or second person |
| Internal comms | First person plural ("we") |

## Punctuation as Voice Tool
- **Em dashes** — for parenthetical energy and mid-thought pivots.
- **Colons:** for setup and payoff within a sentence.
- **Semicolons;** sparingly, for parallel ideas that are equals.
- **Ellipses...** rarely, and only for genuine trailing off, never for dramatic pause.
- **Exclamation marks!** Maximum one per piece. Earn it.
- **Question marks?** Powerful in headlines and transitions. Use often.

## Formatting as Voice
- **Bold** for key terms on first introduction and for pull-quote emphasis.
- *Italic* for internal thought, emphasis on single words, and titles.
- Short paragraphs (2–4 sentences) for digital content.
- Bulleted lists for scannable information, but limit to 5–7 items.
- Headers as promises: each header should tell the reader what they'll gain by reading the section.

## Words We Use / Words We Avoid

### We Use
Clear, show, build, discover, transform, real, specific, you, because, imagine,
here's why, let me show you, the truth is, what if

### We Avoid
Synergy, leverage (as verb), robust, scalable (unless technical), cutting-edge,
world-class, best-in-class, game-changer, disruptive (unless truly so), revolutionary,
utilize, facilitate, paradigm, bandwidth (non-technical), circle back, move the needle

## Reading Level Target
- General audience: Flesch-Kincaid Grade 7–9
- Executive audience: Grade 9–11
- Expert audience: Grade 11–13
- Social media: Grade 5–7

## Self-Editing Checklist
1. Read the piece aloud. If you stumble, rewrite that sentence.
2. Cut 10% of the word count. Then cut 5% more.
3. Check every paragraph: does it have one clear purpose?
4. Search for "very," "really," "actually," "basically," "just." Delete most of them.
5. Verify that the first and last sentences are the strongest in the piece.
6. Confirm you've shown at least one concrete example per abstract claim.
