# Sensory Detail Guide

## Purpose
Sensory details transform abstract ideas into lived experience. This guide covers
when, where, and how to deploy sensory writing in business storytelling without
turning a case study into a novel.

## The Sensory Hierarchy in Business Storytelling

| Sense | Frequency of Use | Best For |
|-------|------------------|----------|
| **Visual** | Most common | Setting scenes, showing scale, creating contrast |
| **Auditory** | Common | Dialogue, atmosphere, tension |
| **Tactile** | Moderate | Emotional states, physical product descriptions |
| **Olfactory** | Rare but powerful | Memory triggers, place-setting, authenticity |
| **Gustatory** | Rare | Food/beverage brands, metaphor, cultural moments |

## The Rule of One
In business storytelling, you rarely need all five senses. Pick the one detail that
does the most work. One precise sensory detail beats five generic ones.

**Five generic details (too much):**
> The office was bright, the coffee machine hummed, the chairs were uncomfortable,
> the air smelled like carpet cleaner, and someone had left stale donuts on the table.

**One precise detail (just right):**
> The only sound in the conference room was the slow tick of a wall clock that was
> seven minutes fast.

That single detail tells you: tension, waiting, a room where people watch the clock,
imperfection that nobody has fixed.

## Sensory Detail by Context

### Setting a Scene (Case Study / Brand Story)
Ground the reader in a specific moment before introducing the business problem.

> **Without sensory detail:** "The team met to discuss the failing product launch."
>
> **With sensory detail:** "The team gathered around a table still cluttered with
> yesterday's takeout containers. Someone had written 'LAUNCH DAY' on the whiteboard
> in red marker three weeks ago. Nobody had erased it."

### Describing a Product
Move beyond features. What does using the product *feel* like?

> **Feature language:** "The dashboard loads in under two seconds and displays
> real-time analytics."
>
> **Sensory language:** "You open the dashboard and the numbers are already there —
> no loading spinner, no waiting. Just the data, clean and current, like checking
> the weather by looking out the window."

### Customer Transformation Stories
Use sensory contrast between the "before" and "after" states.

> **Before:** "Her inbox was a wall of red notification badges, each one a small
> emergency she couldn't get to."
>
> **After:** "Monday morning, she opened her laptop to six emails. Six. She read
> them with her coffee still warm."

### Emotional Moments
When the story hits its emotional peak, anchor it in the body.

> "When the call came through — the one they'd been waiting for — she didn't
> cheer. She set the phone down on the desk, pressed both palms flat against
> the wood, and exhaled for what felt like the first time in three months."

## Sensory Detail Techniques

### 1. The Anchoring Object
Choose one physical object that carries emotional weight throughout the story.
- The cracked phone screen that reminds the founder of the day they almost quit.
- The sticky note with the customer's first complaint, still taped to the monitor.
- The prototype that sits on the shelf — the one that didn't work.

### 2. The Environmental Contrast
Use environment to mirror emotional state.
> **Low point:** "The fluorescent light in the hallway flickered like it couldn't
> decide whether to stay on."
> **High point:** "The windows of the new office faced east. Every morning started
> in sunlight."

### 3. The Telling Absence
Sometimes what's *missing* is the most powerful detail.
> "The office that used to hold forty people now had six desks. The rest were stacked
> against the wall."

### 4. Synesthesia Crossing
Blend senses for unexpected vividness.
> "The silence in the room was heavy, almost tactile."
> "The color of the rebrand was loud — a neon green that practically hummed."

## Sensory Detail Checklist for Editing

1. Does the piece have at least one grounding sensory detail per major scene?
2. Is every sensory detail earning its place (reveals character, setting, or emotion)?
3. Are the details specific? ("A 1994 Honda Civic" not "a car")
4. Have you avoided cliche sensory details? (No "butterflies in stomach," "heart racing")
5. Is there sensory contrast between before/after or low/high points?
6. Have you used restraint? One precise detail per scene is often enough.

## Sensory Details to Avoid

| Cliche | Better Alternative |
|--------|--------------------|
| "Butterflies in her stomach" | "Her hands wouldn't stay still" |
| "His heart was racing" | "He could feel his pulse in his ears" |
| "A cold sweat" | "The back of his shirt stuck to the chair" |
| "Eyes lit up" | "She leaned forward so fast her chair scraped the floor" |
| "Pin-drop silence" | "The only sound was the air conditioning cycling off" |
| "Tears streaming down her face" | "She pressed her thumbnail into her palm and kept talking" |

## When to Skip Sensory Detail
Not every piece needs it. Skip sensory detail when:
- The content is purely instructional (how-to, documentation).
- The audience is scanning for specific information (comparison pages, FAQs).
- Adding detail would slow a time-sensitive communication (alerts, updates).
- The format is too short to support it (tweet, push notification).
