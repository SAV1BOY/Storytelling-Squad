# Dialogue Writing Guide

## Purpose
Dialogue brings characters to life in stories, case studies, testimonials, and
brand narratives. This guide covers how to write natural, purposeful dialogue
for marketing and business storytelling contexts.

## The Three Rules of Story Dialogue

### Rule 1: Dialogue Must Do Work
Every line of dialogue should accomplish at least one of these:
- Reveal character (personality, values, emotional state)
- Advance the plot (new information, decision, action)
- Create tension (disagreement, subtext, stakes)

If a line of dialogue does none of these, cut it.

**Bad:** "Hi, how are you?" "I'm fine, thanks. How are you?"
**Good:** "You're late." "I know. The board said no."

### Rule 2: People Don't Talk in Complete Sentences
Real speech is messy. Use fragments, interruptions, trailing thoughts.

**Stiff:** "I believe that our marketing strategy needs a fundamental revision because the current approach is not generating sufficient leads."
**Natural:** "The leads aren't there. We need to rethink — I mean, not just tweak. Rethink the whole thing."

### Rule 3: Each Character Has a Distinct Voice
If you cover the names, can you tell who is speaking? Differentiate through:
- Sentence length (some people are brief; others ramble)
- Vocabulary level (some use jargon; others keep it plain)
- Verbal tics ("Look," "I mean," "Here's the thing")
- Rhythm (staccato vs. flowing)

## Dialogue in Business Storytelling

### Customer Quotes (Case Studies)
Don't just use the quote they gave you. Help them sound like themselves, but clearer.

**Raw quote:** "So basically what happened was, um, we were using the old system and it was kind of, you know, not really working for us anymore because of the growth and everything."

**Cleaned for readability:** "The old system couldn't keep up with our growth. We were patching it every week, and every patch created a new problem."

**Principle:** Keep the voice. Remove the filler. Sharpen the specifics.

### Founder Stories
Let founders sound human, not polished.

**Over-polished:** "We identified a significant market opportunity in the underserved SMB segment."
**Human:** "We kept hearing the same complaint from small business owners: 'The big tools don't work for us, and the small tools don't work at all.' That's when we knew."

### Testimonials as Mini-Dialogues
Frame testimonials as conversation, not endorsement.

**Static:** "Great product. Highly recommend. 5 stars."
**Narrative:** "My CFO asked me, 'What changed in Q3?' I pulled up the dashboard and said, 'This tool. This is what changed.'"

## Dialogue Formatting

### Attribution Basics
- **"Said" is invisible.** Use it freely. Readers' eyes skip right over it.
- **Fancy tags distract.** "He exclaimed," "she opined," "he interjected" — these pull attention away from the words.
- **Action beats replace tags.** Instead of attribution, show what the character does.

**With tag:** "We can't afford that," she said nervously.
**With beat:** "We can't afford that." She closed the laptop. "Not this quarter."

### Punctuation
- Comma before closing quote when followed by attribution: "Let's go," she said.
- Period when followed by action beat: "Let's go." She grabbed her keys.
- Em dash for interruption: "I was going to tell you about the—"
- Ellipsis for trailing off: "I thought we had more time..."

### Paragraph Breaks
New speaker, new paragraph. Always. Even for one word.

> "Is it ready?"
>
> "No."
>
> "When?"
>
> "Tuesday. Maybe."

## Subtext: What Characters Don't Say

The most powerful dialogue carries meaning beneath the surface.

| What they say | What they mean |
|--------------|----------------|
| "That's an interesting approach." | "I disagree but I'm being polite." |
| "Let me think about it." | "No." |
| "We should get coffee sometime." | "I'm ending this conversation." |
| "I'm fine." | "I'm not fine." |
| "Do whatever you think is best." | "I've given up trying to convince you." |

## Dialogue Patterns for Storytelling

### The Reveal
Use dialogue to deliver the turning-point information.
> The doctor put down the chart. "It's not the medication. It's the water."

### The Challenge
Use dialogue to create the moment of decision.
> "You can take the severance and walk away. Or you can stay and fix this. But you can't do both."

### The Commitment
Use dialogue to mark the character's transformation.
> "I'm not asking for permission anymore. I'm telling you: we're doing this."

### The Reflection
Use dialogue to deliver the lesson, but through a character's mouth, not the narrator's.
> "You know what I learned? The pitch didn't fail because of the slides. It failed because I didn't believe it yet."

## Common Mistakes

1. **Exposition dumping through dialogue.** "As you know, Bob, our company was founded in 2015 and serves over 3,000 customers." Nobody talks like this.
2. **All characters sound the same.** Read each character's lines in isolation. They should be distinguishable.
3. **Too much dialogue.** In business storytelling, dialogue is seasoning, not the main course. Use it for key moments.
4. **Quotation marks around paraphrases.** If you're paraphrasing, don't use quotes. Only use quotes for actual or reconstructed speech.
5. **Perfect recall.** Nobody remembers exact conversations from years ago. Frame it: "The way I remember it, she said something like…"
