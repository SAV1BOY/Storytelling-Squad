# Cultural Adaptation: Brazilian Portuguese and LATAM

## Purpose
This guide covers linguistic, cultural, and tonal adaptations needed when applying
the storytelling squad's frameworks to Brazilian Portuguese content and broader
Latin American audiences.

## Key Cultural Dimensions for Storytelling

### 1. Warmth Is the Default
Brazilian communication is inherently warm. Formality without warmth reads as cold
or hostile. Even corporate content uses diminutives, inclusive language, and
conversational markers.

**English (professional):** "Here are three steps to improve your marketing."
**Brazilian Portuguese (equivalent tone):** "Olha, separei três passos que vão te ajudar a melhorar seu marketing."

Note the "Olha" (Look) — a conversational opener that signals approachability. This
is standard, not informal.

### 2. Collective Over Individual
Brazilian culture leans collectivist. "We" lands better than "I" in most contexts.
Individual achievement stories should frame success as benefiting the group.

**US framing:** "I built a million-dollar company from my garage."
**BR adaptation:** "Junto com minha equipe, construímos algo que mudou a vida de milhares de pessoas."

### 3. Relationship Before Transaction
In Brazil, trust is built through relationship, not credentials. Story structure
should invest more time in the "Story of Us" (shared experience) before
moving to the ask.

### 4. Emotional Expressiveness Is Normal
Brazilian audiences are comfortable with higher emotional intensity. What might
feel like Level 3 intensity in US English is Level 2 in Brazilian Portuguese.
Adjust the emotional-intensity-scale upward by roughly one level.

### 5. Humor Is Expected
Even serious business content in Brazil often includes lightness. Pure gravitas
without any human warmth reads as arrogant or disconnected.

## Linguistic Adaptations

### Formality Registers in Brazilian Portuguese

| Register | Portuguese Marker | Context |
|----------|------------------|---------|
| **Muito informal** | "Tu" / gírias / abreviações | Social media (TikTok, Instagram Stories) |
| **Informal** | "Você" / contrações / tom leve | Blog, email marketing, LinkedIn |
| **Profissional** | "Você" / vocabulário cuidado / sem gírias | Propostas, apresentações de vendas |
| **Formal** | "O senhor/a senhora" / linguagem protocolar | Comunicação institucional, jurídica |

**Important:** Brazilian Portuguese uses "você" as the standard second person in almost
all business contexts. "Tu" varies by region (common in the South and Northeast, rare
in São Paulo). When in doubt, use "você."

### Common Translation Traps

| English Phrase | Bad Translation | Good Adaptation |
|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| "Game-changer" | "Mudador de jogo" | "Isso muda tudo" / "Um divisor de águas" |
| "No-brainer" | "Sem cérebro" | "É óbvio" / "Não tem nem o que pensar" |
| "Pain point" | "Ponto de dor" | "Problema" / "Desafio" / "O que tira seu sono" |
| "Call to action" | "Chamada para ação" | "Próximo passo" / context-specific verb |
| "Storytelling" | "Contação de histórias" | "Storytelling" (the English word is used in BR marketing) |
| "Leverage" | "Alavancar" | "Usar a seu favor" / "Potencializar" |
| "Mindset" | "Mentalidade" | "Mindset" (commonly used in BR) or "Mentalidade" |

### Sentence Structure Differences
- Brazilian Portuguese favors longer sentences with more clauses. The short-punch
  style of English copywriting needs adaptation — maintain rhythm but allow for
  natural Portuguese flow.
- Imperative mood is common and not perceived as aggressive: "Faça," "Descubra,"
  "Comece agora."
- Questions in Portuguese often add "né?" or "não é?" as conversational confirmers.
  Use sparingly in written content.

## Storytelling Framework Adaptations

### The Hero's Journey in Brazilian Context
The "lone hero" archetype is less resonant in Brazil. Adapt:
- The hero often has a strong community, family, or mentor presence.
- The "ordinary world" should feel warm and connected, not isolated.
- Transformation includes the community, not just the individual.

### Story of Self / Us / Now (Ganz Framework)
This framework translates exceptionally well to Brazilian culture because:
- "Story of Us" aligns with collectivist values.
- Emotional expressiveness makes "Story of Self" natural.
- Urgency in "Story of Now" resonates with Brazilian activist traditions.

**Adaptation:** Spend proportionally more time on "Story of Us" than in US English
applications.

### StoryBrand in Brazilian Portuguese
The framework works but the language must be warmer:
- "The guide" should feel like a trusted friend, not just an expert.
- "The plan" should feel like an invitation, not a prescription.
- "The call to action" should feel like encouragement, not a command.

## Regional Considerations Within Brazil

| Region | Communication Style | Notes |
|--------|-------------------|-------|
| São Paulo | Direct, fast-paced, business-oriented | Closest to US business style |
| Rio de Janeiro | Warm, informal, storytelling-friendly | Natural narrative culture |
| Nordeste | Expressive, poetic, oral tradition-rich | Strong storytelling heritage ("cordel") |
| Sul | More formal, influenced by European culture | Slightly higher formality threshold |
| Centro-Oeste | Growing tech hub, younger audience | Open to modern marketing language |

## Broader LATAM Considerations
- **Spanish LATAM is not one market.** Mexican Spanish, Argentine Spanish, and Colombian Spanish have significant tonal differences.
- **Portunhol is not acceptable.** Never mix Portuguese and Spanish. Audiences notice instantly.
- **Localize, don't just translate.** Cultural references, humor, idioms, and even color associations vary across LATAM.
- **US Hispanic audiences** are a distinct segment with their own bilingual dynamics. Content for this audience requires specialized adaptation, not direct translation from LATAM Spanish.

## Quality Checklist for BR Content
1. Read the content aloud in Portuguese. Does it sound like a real person talking?
2. Are there any anglicisms that don't belong? (Some English terms are naturalized in BR — "storytelling," "marketing," "mindset" — but others are not.)
3. Is the warmth level appropriate? Would a Brazilian reader feel welcomed or lectured?
4. Have you checked regional assumptions? What works in São Paulo may not work in Recife.
5. Has a native Brazilian Portuguese speaker reviewed the final text?
