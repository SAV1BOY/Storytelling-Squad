# Formality Scale

## Purpose
Calibrate language register to match audience expectations, channel norms, and
brand positioning. The wrong formality level creates friction: too casual feels
unprofessional, too formal feels cold and inaccessible.

## The Scale

### Level 1: Casual
**Register:** How you talk to a friend over coffee.
**Contractions:** Always ("don't," "can't," "we're," "it's").
**Slang:** Permitted (current, not dated). "TBH," "game-changer," "vibe."
**Sentence fragments:** Frequent. "So good." "Not even close." "Big yikes."
**Humor:** Frequent. Self-deprecation welcome.
**Emoji/punctuation:** Emoji OK in moderation. Exclamation marks allowed.
**Person:** Second person ("you") and first person ("I") dominant.
**Paragraph length:** 1–3 sentences.

**Example:**
> Okay so we completely redesigned the dashboard and honestly? It slaps. Loading
> time is basically instant, the layout makes sense for once, and you can finally
> customize your view without calling support. Try it. You'll thank us later.

**Channels:** Social media, community forums, internal Slack, casual newsletters,
DTC brand emails.

---

### Level 2: Professional
**Register:** How you'd present to a friendly client.
**Contractions:** Selective ("we're," "it's" — yes. "ain't," "gonna" — no).
**Slang:** Avoided. Industry terms OK.
**Sentence fragments:** Occasional, for emphasis only.
**Humor:** Occasional. Wit over slapstick.
**Emoji/punctuation:** No emoji. Restrained exclamation marks (max 1 per piece).
**Person:** Second person ("you") or first plural ("we").
**Paragraph length:** 2–4 sentences.

**Example:**
> We've redesigned the dashboard with three priorities: speed, clarity, and
> customization. Pages load in under two seconds. The layout groups related
> metrics so you spend less time searching. And you can now configure your own
> view — no support ticket required.

**Channels:** Business blogs, product announcements, client emails, case studies,
webinars, sales decks.

---

### Level 3: Corporate
**Register:** How a company communicates to stakeholders and the public record.
**Contractions:** Rare. Full forms preferred ("do not," "we are," "it is").
**Slang:** Never.
**Sentence fragments:** Never.
**Humor:** Extremely rare and carefully calibrated.
**Emoji/punctuation:** Never. Conservative punctuation.
**Person:** First plural ("we") or third person ("the company").
**Paragraph length:** 3–5 sentences.

**Example:**
> The redesigned dashboard represents a significant improvement in user experience.
> Page load times have been reduced to under two seconds, and the new layout
> organizes metrics by functional area. Users now have the ability to configure
> personalized views without requiring assistance from the support team. These
> enhancements are available to all subscribers effective immediately.

**Channels:** Press releases, annual reports, investor communications, regulatory
filings, formal partnership announcements, enterprise proposals.

---

### Level 4: Scholarly / Formal
**Register:** Academic, legal, or ceremonial language.
**Contractions:** Never.
**Slang:** Never.
**Sentence fragments:** Never.
**Humor:** Inappropriate in most contexts.
**Emoji/punctuation:** Never.
**Person:** Third person or passive voice. "It was observed that…"
**Paragraph length:** 4–8 sentences. Dense.

**Example:**
> The interface redesign was undertaken with the objective of improving three
> principal dimensions of user experience: response latency, information
> architecture, and configurability. Quantitative analysis of performance metrics
> indicates that page load times have been reduced by approximately 74%, from a
> median of 7.6 seconds to 1.9 seconds. The revised information architecture
> employs a functional grouping model that aligns displayed metrics with
> established user workflows, as determined through contextual inquiry sessions
> conducted with a representative sample of 120 users across four market segments.

**Channels:** Academic papers, legal documents, patent applications, government
submissions, medical/clinical contexts, formal ceremonies.

## Choosing the Right Level

### By Audience

| Audience | Default Level | Notes |
|----------|--------------|-------|
| Gen Z / young consumers | 1 | Casual is expected; formal feels fake |
| Millennials | 1–2 | Depends on context (social = 1, work = 2) |
| Mid-career professionals | 2 | The safe default for most business content |
| C-suite executives | 2–3 | Professional but efficient; formality = respect for time |
| Board of directors | 3 | Corporate register expected |
| Academics / researchers | 4 | Convention requires formal register |
| Government / regulators | 3–4 | Formality = compliance signal |

### By Channel

| Channel | Default Level |
|---------|--------------|
| TikTok / Instagram | 1 |
| Twitter / X | 1–2 |
| LinkedIn | 2 |
| Blog | 2 |
| Email (marketing) | 1–2 |
| Email (sales) | 2 |
| Email (executive) | 2–3 |
| Press release | 3 |
| White paper | 3 |
| Annual report | 3–4 |

## Mixing Formality Levels

Sometimes the most effective writing blends levels. A corporate document with one
carefully placed casual sentence creates surprise and memorability.

> "The fiscal year 2025 results reflect disciplined execution across all business
> units. Revenue exceeded guidance by 12%. Margins expanded. And for the first time
> in company history, every single region was profitable. Not bad for a year that
> started with a pandemic variant and a supply chain crisis."

The "not bad" at the end is Level 1 inside a Level 3 document. It works because it's
earned, intentional, and human.

## Formality Traps

1. **Formality ≠ intelligence.** Writing at Level 4 doesn't make you sound smarter. It often makes you sound less clear.
2. **Casual ≠ unprofessional.** Level 1 writing can be rigorous, well-researched, and deeply expert.
3. **Inconsistent register.** Jumping between levels without purpose confuses the reader.
4. **Default to corporate.** Many writers default to Level 3 out of insecurity. Choose the level your audience needs, not the one that feels "safe."
5. **Forced casual.** A 60-year-old CEO writing "no cap, this quarterly report is fire" helps no one.
