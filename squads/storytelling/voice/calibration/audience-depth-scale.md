# Audience Depth Scale

## Purpose
Calibrate narrative depth, vocabulary, and explanation density based on who is
reading. The same story told at the wrong depth loses either attention or trust.

## The Scale

### Level 1: Child / Absolute Beginner (Ages 8–12 / Zero context)
**Reading level:** Flesch-Kincaid Grade 3–5
**Sentence length:** 8–12 words average
**Vocabulary:** Common, everyday words only. No jargon. No acronyms.
**Explanation density:** Define everything. Use analogies to familiar objects.
**Story structure:** Linear. Clear cause and effect. One protagonist.
**Emotional register:** Simple, clear emotions. Happy, sad, scared, proud.

**Example:**
> A company makes shoes. But the shoes were too expensive for most people. So a
> woman named Maria had an idea: what if they used different materials? She tried
> it. The new shoes cost half as much. Now more people could buy them. Maria's
> idea changed the whole company.

---

### Level 2: Mass Market / General Public
**Reading level:** Flesch-Kincaid Grade 6–8
**Sentence length:** 12–18 words average
**Vocabulary:** Common words plus widely understood terms. Explain any technical term on first use.
**Explanation density:** Assume intelligence but no specialized knowledge.
**Story structure:** Can use flashbacks, parallel threads, and subtext.
**Emotional register:** Full range, but anchored in universal experiences.

**Example:**
> When Maria joined the company, shoes were a luxury. The average pair cost what
> most families spent on a week of groceries. She saw the problem differently than
> her colleagues: it wasn't about cheaper materials. It was about smarter design.
> By rethinking the sole construction, she cut production costs by 40% — without
> sacrificing comfort. Within two years, the company went from niche to mainstream.

---

### Level 3: Executive / Business Decision-Maker
**Reading level:** Flesch-Kincaid Grade 9–11
**Sentence length:** 14–22 words average
**Vocabulary:** Business terminology assumed. Industry terms OK with light context.
**Explanation density:** Skip basics. Focus on implications, trade-offs, and outcomes.
**Story structure:** Start with the insight or outcome, then support with narrative.
**Emotional register:** Measured. Data-informed. Confidence without sentimentality.

**Example:**
> Maria's redesign of the sole construction reduced COGS by 40% while maintaining
> NPS above 70. The strategic implication was significant: the company could
> penetrate the mass market without diluting brand perception. Within two years,
> revenue grew 3.2x and the customer base shifted from predominantly urban
> professionals to a broad demographic that included first-time buyers in Tier 2
> and Tier 3 cities.

---

### Level 4: Expert / Technical Specialist
**Reading level:** Flesch-Kincaid Grade 11–14
**Sentence length:** 16–25 words average
**Vocabulary:** Full technical vocabulary. No simplification of domain terms.
**Explanation density:** Assume deep expertise. Focus on what's novel or non-obvious.
**Story structure:** Can handle non-linear, multi-variable, and nuanced narratives.
**Emotional register:** Intellectual excitement. Respect for complexity.

**Example:**
> Maria's approach challenged the prevailing assumption in footwear manufacturing
> that EVA foam density was the primary cost driver. Her team's finite element
> analysis of sole stress distribution revealed that a dual-density TPU lattice
> — printed rather than injection-molded — could achieve equivalent energy return
> at 60% of the material cost. The trade-off was cycle time (8 minutes vs. 3 for
> traditional molding), which she offset by parallelizing production across
> modular print stations. The resulting cost curve made the mass-market price point
> viable without the margin erosion that had sunk three prior attempts.

---

## How to Use the Scale

### Step 1: Identify the Audience Level
Ask: "What does my reader already know about this topic?" Map to Level 1–4.

### Step 2: Set Vocabulary Boundaries
At each level, list terms you can use freely and terms that need definition.

### Step 3: Adjust Story Complexity
| Element | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 | Level 4 |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Characters | 1–2 | 2–4 | Multiple stakeholders | Systems + people |
| Timeline | Linear | 1 flashback OK | Non-linear OK | Complex threading |
| Data | None | 1–2 stats | Key metrics | Full dataset |
| Nuance | Black/white | Some gray | Trade-offs | Paradoxes |
| Length | 200–500 words | 500–1500 words | 800–2000 words | 1000–3000 words |

### Step 4: Test with a Reader
If possible, have someone at the target level read the piece and ask:
- Was anything confusing?
- Was anything patronizing?
- Did you want more detail anywhere?
- Did you want less detail anywhere?

## Mixed-Audience Strategy
When the audience spans multiple levels (e.g., a presentation to both executives
and technical leads):
1. Lead with Level 3 (executive insight).
2. Provide Level 4 depth in appendices, footnotes, or expandable sections.
3. Use Level 2 language for the emotional narrative thread that connects everyone.

## Common Mistakes
- **Writing at your own level, not the audience's.** The curse of knowledge.
- **Condescending to Level 2 audiences.** They're not stupid; they're just not specialists.
- **Over-simplifying for Level 4.** Experts notice when you gloss over important nuance.
- **Forgetting that Level 3 (executives) want speed.** Respect their time; front-load the insight.
