# Emotional Intensity Scale

## Purpose
Not every story needs to make the audience cry. This scale helps calibrate emotional
depth to match the context, audience tolerance, and communication objective.

## The Scale

### Level 1: Light Touch
**Emotional register:** Pleasant, upbeat, mildly interesting.
**Reader experience:** "That's nice" / "Huh, interesting."
**Stakes:** Low. The outcome matters but not deeply.
**Vulnerability:** None. The narrator is composed and observational.
**Physical response:** None. The reader nods, maybe smiles.

**When to use:** Product updates, feature announcements, light social content, weekly
newsletters, internal updates.

**Example:**
> When we added the scheduling feature, we thought it would save users a few clicks.
> Turns out, it saved them an average of 45 minutes per week. Not life-changing,
> but definitely meeting-changing.

---

### Level 2: Moderate
**Emotional register:** Engaging, warm, relatable.
**Reader experience:** "I've felt that" / "That resonates."
**Stakes:** Medium. The outcome affects someone's work, relationship, or self-image.
**Vulnerability:** Mild. The narrator or character admits a common struggle.
**Physical response:** Slight lean-in. Emotional recognition.

**When to use:** Blog posts, case studies, email nurture sequences, webinar stories,
brand content.

**Example:**
> Sarah had been a marketing director for six years, and she was good at it. But
> every quarterly review felt like a performance she was rehearsing for — not
> because she didn't know the numbers, but because she couldn't tell the story
> behind them. The data was strong. Her confidence in presenting it wasn't.

---

### Level 3: Heavy
**Emotional register:** Moving, stirring, emotionally demanding.
**Reader experience:** "This hit me" / tightness in the chest.
**Stakes:** High. The outcome affects livelihood, identity, health, or deeply held values.
**Vulnerability:** Significant. The narrator shares fear, failure, shame, or loss.
**Physical response:** Throat tightening. Eyes stinging. Need to pause.

**When to use:** Keynote stories, fundraising appeals, brand manifestos, founder origin
stories (when authentic), transformation case studies.

**Example:**
> The morning Marcus got the call that his company had lost its biggest client —
> 60% of revenue, gone in one conversation — he drove to the office, sat in the
> parking lot for twenty minutes, and called his wife. "I think I'm going to have
> to let everyone go," he said. She was quiet for a long time. Then she said,
> "Call them first. Tell them the truth. You owe them that."

---

### Level 4: Devastating
**Emotional register:** Raw, exposed, unforgettable.
**Reader experience:** "I need a minute" / tears / changed perspective.
**Stakes:** Existential. Life, death, identity, injustice, irreversible loss.
**Vulnerability:** Total. The narrator holds nothing back.
**Physical response:** Crying. Silence. The need to share it with someone.

**When to use:** Rarely. Nonprofit impact stories involving genuine tragedy. Memorial
content. Health narratives. Social justice campaigns. The Moth stage.

**Example:**
> The last thing my father built was a bookshelf. It's crooked. The shelves don't
> quite sit level, and one of the brackets is a screw short. He finished it on a
> Saturday in October, carried it into my daughter's room, and said, "Tell her
> Grandpa made this when she's old enough to read." He died eleven days later. She's
> four now. She can't read yet. But every night when we pick a book from that shelf,
> I say, "Grandpa made this." And the shelf holds.

---

## Choosing the Right Level

### Match Intensity to Context

| Context | Recommended Level | Why |
|---------|------------------|-----|
| Product feature announcement | 1 | Audience doesn't expect emotion |
| Blog post / thought leadership | 1–2 | Engagement without overwhelm |
| Case study | 2–3 | Needs enough emotion to be memorable |
| Keynote / conference talk | 2–3 | Must move a room but maintain composure |
| Fundraising / nonprofit appeal | 3–4 | Emotion drives donation behavior |
| Brand manifesto | 2–3 | Inspiring without being manipulative |
| Crisis communication | 1–2 | Calm and honest; emotion must feel earned |
| Internal culture content | 2 | Relatable and human, not heavy |

### Match Intensity to Audience Tolerance

| Audience | Typical Tolerance | Notes |
|----------|------------------|-------|
| Engineers / developers | 1–2 | Suspicious of emotional manipulation |
| Executives (C-suite) | 2 | Emotion is OK if tied to outcomes |
| Sales professionals | 2–3 | Comfortable with emotional persuasion |
| Nonprofit supporters | 3–4 | Expect and respond to emotional stories |
| General consumers | 2–3 | Depends heavily on brand and platform |
| Healthcare professionals | 2–3 | High tolerance for difficult content |

## Escalation and De-escalation Techniques

### Escalating Intensity
- Add specific sensory detail (the crooked bookshelf, the parking lot).
- Slow the pace. Shorter sentences. More white space.
- Shift to present tense at the emotional peak.
- Include silence, pauses, what was NOT said.
- End the emotional beat without explaining it. Let it sit.

### De-escalating Intensity
- Widen the lens (zoom out from individual to context).
- Add gentle humor or warmth.
- Shift to future tense ("Today, three years later…").
- Introduce action or resolution.
- Use the narrator's voice to provide perspective.

## Ethical Guidelines
1. **Never manufacture emotion the story doesn't earn.** If the real stakes are Level 2, don't write it at Level 4.
2. **Protect real people.** If using someone's real story at Level 3+, get explicit consent.
3. **Don't exploit suffering.** Vulnerability is a gift. Treat it with reverence.
4. **Provide resolution or hope.** Level 4 content without any redemptive thread can traumatize rather than move.
5. **Know your own limits.** If writing at Level 4 costs you emotionally, step away and return later.
