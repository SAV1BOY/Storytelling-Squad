# Intimate-Personal Tone Profile

## Lineage
Inspired by Matthew Dicks (*Storyworthy*), personal essayists, and memoirists who
trust the audience with the small, real, often embarrassing details of lived experience.

## Core Attributes

| Attribute | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Vulnerability** | The narrator risks something by telling this. They share what most people hide. |
| **Specificity** | Concrete nouns, exact times, real names, particular colors. Never "a car" when you mean "a 1994 green Honda Civic with a cracked taillight." |
| **First-person authority** | "I" is present, accountable, and flawed. The narrator does not hide behind "we" or "one." |
| **Conversational cadence** | Sentences vary wildly — fragments next to longer, rolling clauses. It sounds like someone talking to a friend at 1 a.m. |
| **Present-tense moments** | Key scenes shift to present tense to pull the listener into the room. |

## Voice Signature Phrases
- "Here's what I didn't tell anyone at the time…"
- "I remember the exact moment because…"
- "This is the part where I should have…"
- "I wish I could say I was brave. I wasn't."
- "Let me back up."
- "And I know how that sounds."

## Sentence-Level Patterns
1. **Short punch after long setup:** "I had been rehearsing the speech for three weeks, rewriting the opening paragraph six times, standing in front of the bathroom mirror at midnight mouthing words to my own reflection. And then I forgot my name."
2. **Direct address:** "You know that feeling when the room goes quiet and every pair of eyes finds you? That."
3. **Mid-sentence correction:** "She was angry — no, she was terrified, and anger was just the costume."
4. **Catalog of sensory detail:** "The fluorescent hum, the smell of dry-erase markers, the one chair with the wobbly leg that everyone avoided."

## Paragraph Rhythm
- Open with a grounding detail (time, place, object).
- Escalate with internal thought or dialogue.
- Land on a single, resonant image or admission.
- White space between beats — let the reader breathe.

## When to Use
- Personal brand content, founder stories, memoir-driven marketing.
- Email sequences where trust-building matters more than authority.
- Social posts designed to stop the scroll with authenticity.
- Case studies told from the client's first-person perspective.

## When to Avoid
- Formal B2B white papers or regulatory communications.
- Contexts where vulnerability could undermine perceived competence (e.g., crisis comms).
- Audiences that expect data-first reasoning before personal anecdote.

## Calibration Dials

| Dial | Low (1) | Mid (5) | High (10) |
|------|---------|---------|-----------|
| Vulnerability depth | Surface preference | Mild admission | Deep fear or shame |
| Specificity | General scene | Named place + time | Exact sensory snapshot |
| Humor | Earnest throughout | Wry aside | Self-deprecating thread |
| Emotional weight | Light reflection | Meaningful lesson | Gut-punch revelation |

## Example Paragraph (Calibration 7/10)
> I was standing in aisle nine of a CVS in Newark, holding a pregnancy test I
> didn't need and a bag of Sour Patch Kids I absolutely did, when my phone
> buzzed with the email that said I didn't get the job. Not "we regret to
> inform you" — just "Hi, we've decided to move forward with another
> candidate. Best, Tina." Best, Tina. I put the Sour Patch Kids back. Then I
> picked them up again. Some losses require sugar.

## Pitfalls
- **Over-sharing without purpose.** Every vulnerable detail must serve the story's transformation.
- **Navel-gazing.** The audience needs to see themselves in your story; keep "I" in service of "you."
- **Monotone intimacy.** Vary emotional register — humor, wonder, frustration — so the reader doesn't drown in one feeling.
