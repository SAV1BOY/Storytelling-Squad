# Story Insights Notes
> Running notes on narrative insights gathered from research, observation, and practice

## Purpose
This document captures raw insights about storytelling as they emerge — from reading, from
audience reactions, from experiments, from conversations. These notes feed into framework
development, workflow refinement, and training content. Entries are chronological and unpolished
by design; the goal is capture speed, not publication quality.

---

## 2026-03-07 — Narrative transportation research (Green & Brock revisited)
**Source**: Re-reading "The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of Public Narratives"
**Insight**: Transportation (being absorbed into a story) reduces counter-arguing. When a reader
is transported, they are less likely to generate objections to embedded claims. This has direct
implications for how we position proof points — they should be embedded within the narrative flow,
not presented as separate evidence blocks.
**Application**: Update case study and pitch workflows to integrate proof WITHIN the story arc
rather than in separate "evidence" sections. The proof should feel like a natural part of the
story, not an interruption to present data.

## 2026-03-03 — Observation from Series A pitch round
**Source**: Post-pitch debrief with CEO after VC meetings
**Insight**: The most engaged investor moments consistently occurred during the origin story
(grandmother in the ER) and the live demo, not during the market size or competitive landscape
slides. Two investors specifically said "Tell me more about why you started this" when given
the opportunity for questions. The emotional anchor creates permission for the rational ask.
**Application**: Consider reordering the investor deck to lead with origin story rather than
market problem. Test whether emotional investment early increases receptivity to the ask later.

## 2026-02-25 — Audience-specific hook performance divergence
**Source**: Hook bank performance analysis
**Insight**: Technical audiences (engineering leaders) respond to mid-scene hooks with specific
details ("At 2:47 AM, every dashboard went black"). Executive audiences respond to contradiction
hooks ("The tool designed to prevent outages was causing them"). The same story needs different
entry points depending on audience data literacy and role.
**Application**: When creating stories for multi-audience use, develop at least 2 hook variants
per story — one for technical and one for executive audiences. Tag hooks in the bank with
audience-type effectiveness data.

## 2026-02-18 — The "third telling" effect in improv
**Source**: Improv training session 4 debrief
**Insight**: When participants tell the same story three times in improv exercises, the third
telling is consistently the best — shorter, more emotionally honest, and more structurally
sound. The first telling is discovery, the second is refinement, the third is truth. The
unnecessary details fall away and the emotional core emerges.
**Application**: Build a "triple telling" exercise into the pitch rehearsal workflow. Before
scripting, have the presenter tell the story three times unscripted, with brief feedback between
each telling. Use the third telling as the foundation for the written script.

## 2026-02-12 — Data story analogies and retention
**Source**: Post-webinar attendee survey analysis
**Insight**: When asked "What do you remember from the webinar?", 73% of respondents recalled
the analogy used to explain the data insight, while only 22% recalled the actual data point.
The analogy was "That's like every nurse spending 40 minutes per shift looking for a stapler
instead of caring for patients." The data point was "40 minutes of operational waste per
nursing shift."
**Application**: Every data story must include at least one strong analogy that translates the
number into a human experience. The analogy IS the memory device. Invest more time in analogy
development during the data-to-story workflow. Update the analogy bank after every data story.

## 2026-02-01 — Internal vs. external story performance gap
**Source**: Story bank performance review
**Insight**: Stories about internal culture and transparency (the outage story, the founding
story) are outperforming customer case studies on engagement rate by 2.1x. Hypothesis: audiences
are saturated with case studies that follow predictable formats. Stories that reveal something
unexpected about the company's character break through the noise.
**Application**: Increase the ratio of culture and transparency stories in the Story Bank.
Aim for 30% culture/internal stories alongside 50% customer stories and 20% data/product stories.
The current mix is 15%/70%/15% — too heavily weighted toward customer stories.

## 2026-01-25 — The "villain clarity" principle
**Source**: StoryBrand workshop notes and subsequent A/B testing
**Insight**: Stories with a clearly named villain (legacy systems, outdated processes, the status
quo) outperform stories with vague antagonists (challenges, difficulties, complexity). Naming
the villain creates emotional alignment — the audience and the brand are on the same side against
a shared enemy. But the villain must be a system or force, never a person or competitor.
**Application**: Every BrandScript review should include a "villain clarity check." If the villain
is vague ("many organizations face challenges..."), it needs to be sharpened ("legacy operations
tools built for a slower era are failing today's patients"). Add this check to cl-brand-discovery.

## 2026-01-15 — First customer interview technique refinement
**Source**: Meridian Health case study interview debrief
**Insight**: The best customer quotes came from the question "What would you tell a colleague
who is skeptical about making this change?" rather than "How would you describe the results?"
The first question triggers advocacy and authentic language. The second triggers measured,
corporate-safe responses. Asking the customer to persuade a skeptic activates their storytelling
instinct.
**Application**: Add the "persuade a skeptic" question to the standard customer interview guide.
Position it in the final third of the interview when the customer is warmed up and emotionally
invested. This single question has produced 4 of our top 10 customer quotes.
